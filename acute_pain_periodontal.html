Part A (sections 1–6) covers the management of acute dental pain. Part B (sections 7–10) covers the acute periodontal conditions.

1. Where acute orofacial pain comes from

Odontogenic — most common

Originates from the teeth or their supporting structures.

Non-odontogenic

Inflammation from a tumour, infection or trauma. Common sources: sinuses, salivary glands, ears, eyes, throat, and maxillary/mandibular bone pathology.

2. The limited oral examination

A patient in acute pain gets a limited oral examination, focused on the complaint rather than a full comprehensive exam.

Chief complaint

Record it in the patient's own words where possible. There may be one complaint or several.

History of the complaint: SOCRATES

LetterAsk
SiteWhere is the pain?
OnsetWhen did it start? Sudden or gradual? Getting worse or better?
CharacterDull and throbbing, or sharp and stabbing?
RadiationDoes it spread anywhere?
AssociationsAny other signs or symptoms?
TimingWhen, how often, how long does it last?
Exacerbating / relievingWhat changes it? Hot, cold?
SeverityScore out of 10

Then take medical (conditions, medications, allergies), dental and social histories. The dental and social histories should focus on what relates to the pain.

Extraoral

  • TMJ: click or crepitus
  • Lymphadenopathy
  • Masseter and temporalis
  • Mouth opening: trismus, deviation
  • Swelling or lumps, thyroid, symmetry

Intraoral

  • All soft tissues, oropharynx and saliva. Any abnormality: record site, size, colour, texture, fluctuance, associated structures, and draw it.
  • At the painful site: caries, periodontitis, fractured teeth or restorations, large restorations, cracks, occlusion and parafunction.

Referred pain Bender 2000

Pain from posterior teeth can refer. It stays unilateral but can cross between maxilla and mandible. Don't assume the tooth the patient points at is the culprit.

Examine carefully

Wash, dry, light (operatory, transilluminating, head light). Look from different angles and at different parts of the tooth with a mirror, and check the adjacent teeth and opposing arch.

Then build a differential diagnosis: the likely causes, ordered most to least likely.

3. Diagnostic tests

Sensibility tests

Thermal and electric tests (cold, heat, EPT). They infer pulp health from a nerve response.

Vitality tests

Measure pulp blood flow, a better measure of true pulp health (e.g. laser Doppler flowmetry).

So the cold test and EPT are sensibility tests, not vitality tests, even though clinicians often say "vitality".

When to test pulp sensibility Jafarzadeh & Abbott 2010

  1. Diagnosing pain in the trigeminal area, including referred pain
  2. Monitoring teeth after trauma
  3. Before restorative procedures
  4. Periodically for teeth with pulp-preservation procedures or extensive restorations

The eight tests

TestWhat the result means
1. ColdNo response suggests necrosis Seltzer 1963; Dummer 1980, unless the pulp space is calcified. Agents: CO₂ ice, Endo-Frost (propane–butane, −50 °C).
2. EPTNo correlation between pain threshold and pulp condition Mumford 1965, 1967. With no response: 72% total necrosis, 25.7% partial necrosis Seltzer 1963.
3. HeatAn abnormal response does not correlate with histology; no response suggests necrosis. Sources: prophy cup, hot liquids, hot gutta-percha.
4. PercussionA positive response indicates periapical inflammation Seltzer 1963.
5. PalpationTenderness over the apex is reliable, but its absence doesn't rule out periapical inflammation.
6. ProbingPeriodontal involvement; narrow, isolated deep defects (cracks, endo-perio).
7. MobilitySupport loss, fracture, acute inflammation.
8. RadiographsCaries depth, restorations, periapical and periodontal bone.

Cracks: use a bite stick (FracFinder / Tooth Slooth) to load individual cusps, and transillumination. Required reading: Chen & Abbott 2009, Dental pulp testing: a review.

The slides list the full AAE set, including symptomatic and asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis, pulp necrosis, asymptomatic apical periodontitis, acute and chronic apical abscess, and condensing osteitis. Irreversible pulpitis is Year 3 material.

4. How pulp pain works

Brännström's hydrodynamic theory

When dentine is exposed, stimuli move the fluid inside open dentinal tubules. That movement activates pulpal nociceptors.

 Aδ fibresC fibres
MyelinMyelinated, largerUnmyelinated, smaller
SpeedFastSlow
PainSharp, shootingBurning, slower onset
HypoxiaBlocked first as pressure risesResistant; still fire as pulp degenerates

What decides how much it hurts

  • The number of permeable tubules
  • Up- or down-regulation by inflammatory mediators
  • Intrapulpal pressure: a rise blocks Aδ fibres and activates C fibres, which is why pain shifts from sharp to dull and burning as pulpitis progresses

Why pulpitis pain is so variable

With rapidly progressing caries, the pulp has less time to protect itself by occluding the tubules, so it hurts more. This is also why pulpal pain varies in quality, severity and trigger, and is often poorly localised.

5. Reading the pain: the Wetherell flowchart

The lecture's organising framework. Start with what the pain is like, then what triggers it.

Triggered by hot, cold or sweet → exposed dentine

  • Recession (loss of cementum)
  • Abrasion, abfraction, erosion (loss of enamel)
  • Caries: primary or secondary
  • Restoration fractured, lost or moved

Triggered by biting → structural

  • Cracked or fractured cusp
  • Loose filling
  • Fractured tooth

Ask about the direction and amount of pressure, and whether it hurts on biting or on release. Pain on release is classic for a cracked cusp.

6. Managing acute dental pain

Goals

Reduce, control, stop the pain. If it is beyond your scope or experience, moderate the pain and refer. Once settled, bring the patient back for the rest of the mouth: if one tooth is this bad, check what the others are doing.

Dentine hypersensitivity: a stepped ladder

Work down one step at a time, moving on only if pain continues.

  1. Eliminate predisposing factors so it won't recur
  2. Desensitising toothpaste or mouthrinse
  3. Topical tubule-occluding agents
  4. Adhesive resins, restorations, gingival grafting

Caries

DiagnosisManagement
Reversible pulpitis, primary cariesMinimal intervention dentistry principles; restore.
Reversible pulpitis, secondary cariesRemove all or part of the restoration. Take enough to access the caries, clear peripheral caries (enamel and DEJ) for a sealed margin, and leave resistance and retention form.
Irreversible pulpitisRefer at this stage of the course (Year 3 content). Advise on over-the-counter analgesia.

Cracked tooth / fractured cusp

  • Often hard to diagnose. Ask why it happened: heavily restored tooth? Parafunction?
  • Short term: remove or protect the offending cusp. An orthodontic band can splint it and help confirm the diagnosis.
  • Long term: cuspal-coverage direct restoration → onlay/crown → RCT → extraction

Fractured restoration

  • Remove or repair, and ask why it failed: not enough bulk? Parafunction?
  • With it out, check the tooth for cracks, then replace.

7. Periodontal abscess

Definition Herrera et al. 2000

A localised accumulation of pus within the gingival wall of the periodontal pocket, with periodontal breakdown over a limited period of time and easily detectable clinical symptoms.

Clinical features AAP 2000

Pain and tenderness to touch · smooth, shiny gingival swelling · pus, with increased probing depths · often rapid attachment loss · increased mobility · tender to percussion.

Prevalence

  • 7.7–14.0% of all dental emergencies: the third most common acute infection, behind dento-alveolar (periapical) abscess and pericoronitis.
  • Far more common in untreated periodontitis (59.7%) than during active periodontal treatment (13.5%) Gray et al. 1994.

Pathogenesis

  • The pocket opening becomes occluded (tissue tightening or trauma), so it can't drain. Infection spreads into the pocket wall Sanz 2008.
  • Bacterial entry starts it, but the acute inflammatory infiltrate and its enzymes destroy the connective tissue DeWitt 1985.
  • The course depends on host resistance versus bacterial number and virulence.

Histopathology, outside → inside DeWitt et al. 1985

  1. Normal oral epithelium and lamina propria
  2. Acute inflammatory infiltrate
  3. Intense foci of inflammation; surrounding CT destroyed and necrotic
  4. Destroyed, ulcerated pocket epithelium
  5. Central mass of granular, acidophilic, amorphous debris

Microbiology

Similar to periodontitis. P. gingivalis (found in 50–100%), plus P. intermedia, P. melaninogenica, F. nucleatum, T. forsythia, Treponema, Campylobacter, Capnocytophaga and A. actinomycetemcomitans.

Assessment Corbet 2004

Ask about: active periodontal therapy, current SPT, a previous abscess at the site, recent antibiotics Topoll 1990, trauma or foreign-body impaction.

Look for: swelling, redness, suppuration, deep probing, mobility, and systemic signs (fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy).

Radiographs show existing periodontal bone loss and/or bone loss from the abscess. An early abscess may show nothing radiographically.

Periodontal vs periapical abscess Corbet et al. 2004

 Periodontal (more likely)Periapical (more likely)
HistoryPeriodontal disease · periodontal treatment · previous antibiotic therapyCaries · fracture · tooth wear · restorative treatment · endodontic treatment
ClinicalVital pulp responses · probing releases pus · periodontal disease evidentQuestionable or no response to pulp tests · narrow probing defect (may be isolated) · advanced caries or wear, large restorations, discoloured tooth
RadiographicCrestal bone loss · angular defects · furcation involvementApical radiolucency (± prior RCT) · endodontic filling · endodontic or post perforation
Response to txResponds dramatically to draining pus and subgingival debridementResponds poorly or not at all to periodontal treatment

The single best discriminator

Pulp sensibility. A responsive pulp points to a periodontal abscess; a non-responsive one points to periapical. Remember that a root fracture can mimic a periodontal infection and won't respond to periodontal treatment, so exclude it at sites with acute purulent infection.

Differential diagnosis

  • Other odontogenic: dento-alveolar abscess, pericoronitis, endo-periodontal abscess, lateral periodontal cyst, post-operative infection
  • Tumours: metastatic carcinoma, odontogenic myxoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, SCC
  • Other lesions: pyogenic granuloma, osteomyelitis, odontogenic keratocyst, eosinophilic granuloma

Management

  1. Drain under LA, through the pocket beneath the swelling (or, less often, an external incision)
  2. Thorough debridement of plaque and calculus
  3. Irrigate with saline or LA solution
  4. Severe cases: extract to drain, then irrigate and curette the socket
  5. Antibiotics only if systemic signs (rare)

8. Pericoronitis

Inflammation of the soft tissue around the crown of a partially erupted tooth. The operculum is the flap of soft tissue covering it.

Aetiology

  • The operculum blocks self-performed biofilm removal
  • Impacted tooth, most commonly the mandibular third molar
  • Trauma to the operculum from the opposing tooth

Features

Pain · inflamed operculum, sometimes ulcerated · erythema · swelling (intra- and/or extra-oral) · halitosis · trismus · systemic signs: fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy.

Management

  1. Debride under LA and irrigate with saline or LA solution
  2. If the opposing tooth is traumatising the operculum: occlusal adjustment
  3. If pus is present: drain through the pocket (or an external incision)
  4. Antibiotics only if systemic signs; recommend analgesics
  5. Once the acute phase settles: consider operculectomy or extraction

9. Necrotising periodontal diseases (NPD)

Definition Holmstrup & Westergaard 2008

A microbial disease that most commonly occurs in an impaired host, with necrosis and sloughing of the gingiva, sometimes with systemic signs.

Terminology and the disease spectrum

  • The AAP 1999 terms were NUG and NUP. "Ulcerative" was dropped because ulceration is secondary to necrosis Feller 2005. Now: NG and NP.
  • NG and NP are probably stages of the same disease, with the same aetiology, features and treatment Lang 1999.

Who gets it

  • Males = females; more common under 25 Stevens 1984
  • Lower socio-economic groups and developing countries: 1.8–11.5% of Nigerian children aged 2–6 Sheiham 1967
  • Developed countries: ~0.5% Horning 1990
  • Military: 14% of Danish personnel Pindborg 1951
  • HIV: 0–28% Holmstrup & Westergaard 1994, reduced with antiretrovirals Tappuni & Fleming 2001

Risk factors

  • HIV/AIDS, malnutrition
  • Psychological stress, insufficient sleep
  • Poor OH, pre-existing gingivitis, previous NPD
  • Tobacco and alcohol
  • Young age, ethnicity, seasonal variation
  • Local: overcontoured crowns, ortho retainer wires

Clinical presentation Holmstrup & Westergaard 2008

  • Necrosis and ulceration of the interdental papillae, spreading buccally and lingually
  • Gingival bleeding, pain
  • Pseudomembrane, halitosis
  • Adenopathy, fever

Usually no pockets

Destruction is so extensive that the gingiva is lost along with the attachment, so pockets usually don't form. Pockets appear only when NPD occurs on top of existing periodontitis. Attachment loss can come from one episode or several. In severely immunocompromised patients, bone sequestrum can occur.

Necrotising stomatitis

Extends to and beyond the mucogingival junction into the alveolar mucosa, with larger areas of osteitis and bone sequestrum. Seen in severely immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS, severe malnutrition).

Microbiology

A "constant flora": Prevotella intermedia, Treponema, Selenomonas and Fusobacterium Loesche 1982. The role of spirochaetes has been confirmed by immunoassay Riviere 1991 and 16S rRNA PCR Dewhirst 2000.

Histopathology: Listgarten's four zones (1965)

Electron microscopy of NPD lesions gave some of the strongest evidence that bacteria invade the tissue. From the surface inward:

Management

Thorough mechanical debridement is the goal, but pain may prevent it at the first visit. Begin with:

  1. LA, then debridement with ultrasonics and hand instruments
  2. Local irrigation: chlorhexidine 0.2% or hydrogen peroxide 3%
  3. Metronidazole 400 mg orally, 12-hourly for 3–5 days
  4. Review at 48–72 hours

If pain stops the patient brushing, prescribe a short-term rinse, held for 1 minute then spat out, until the pain settles:

Contrast with abscess and pericoronitis

For a periodontal abscess or pericoronitis, antibiotics are only for systemic signs. For NPD, metronidazole is part of first-line management. The regimens match the Australian Therapeutic Guidelines: Oral and Dental.

10. Endo-periodontal lesions (EPL)

Conditions involving both the pulp and the periodontium, in acute or chronic forms.

  • After recent trauma or iatrogenic events (root fracture, perforation): usually acute, with abscess and pain.
  • In periodontitis patients: usually slow and symptom-free.

Signs and symptoms

  • Deep pocket reaching or near the apex
  • Negative or altered pulp sensibility
  • Bone resorption at the apex or furcation
  • Spontaneous pain, or pain on palpation/percussion
  • Pus, mobility, sinus tract, gingival colour change

Aetiology

Always involves microbial contamination of both pulp and periodontium.

Infection-driven: caries reaching the pulp then the periodontium · periodontal destruction reaching the canal · both at once.

Trauma/iatrogenic (usually poor prognosis): perforation (root, chamber, furcation) · root fracture or cracking · external root resorption · traumatic pulp necrosis draining through the periodontium.

Pathogenesis and microbiology

  • Pulp and periodontium communicate via the apical foramina, accessory (lateral) canals and dentinal tubules. Accessory canals are commonest in the apical third but can be numerous in furcations.
  • Root canal and pocket flora are very similar, mostly periodontal pathogens (P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, T. forsythia), plus Filifactor alocis and Enterococcus faecalis.

11. Slide errors to know about

I checked the slides against their sources. Most of it holds up; these are the places where the slide wording is off:

  • Slide 19, CO₂ ice "−70 °C": dry ice sits at about −78 °C. Endo-Frost at −50 °C is correct.
  • Slide 54, "third most prevalent… followed by dentoalveolar abscess and periodontitis": wrong order and wrong condition. The periodontal abscess ranks third behind the dento-alveolar abscess and pericoronitis (Herrera 2000; confirmed in StatPearls).
  • Slide 53, "APP 2000": should be AAP.
  • Slide 67: "If pus present the abscess" is missing a verb. It means drain the abscess.
  • Slide 9 (Wetherell 2001) says "acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis", the pre-2017 term. Use necrotising gingivitis (NG) in answers.
  • Spellings: Pinborg → Pindborg; Watergaard → Westergaard; forsynthia → forsythia; Topolli → Topoll.

Checked and correct: Seltzer's EPT figures (72% / 25.7%), metronidazole 400 mg 12-hourly for 3–5 days, the H₂O₂ and chlorhexidine rinse regimens, Listgarten's four zones, Loesche's constant flora, and all three Herrera 2018 classification tables, which I compared cell by cell with the slides.

Distinctions examiners like

  • Sensibility vs vitality testing: nerve response vs blood flow.
  • Aδ vs C fibres: sharp vs burning. Rising pressure silences Aδ first; C fibres survive hypoxia.
  • Periodontal vs periapical abscess: pulp response, probing pattern, radiograph, response to debridement.
  • NG vs NP vs NS: gingiva only → attachment and bone loss → past the MGJ.
  • EPL with vs without root damage: root damage means a poor prognosis.
  • Antibiotics: systemic signs only for abscess and pericoronitis, but first-line (metronidazole) for NPD.

Likely exam prompts

  • "A patient presents with acute pain. Outline your assessment." → CC in their words → SOCRATES → MHx, DHx, SHx → EO/IO → careful inspection → differential → special tests.
  • "Explain short, sharp pain to cold." → exposed dentine → hydrodynamic theory → Aδ fibres → DH ladder.
  • "Differentiate a periodontal from a periapical abscess." → the Corbet table.
  • "Describe the presentation and management of NG." → punched-out papillae, pain, bleeding, pseudomembrane, halitosis → LA debridement, irrigation, metronidazole, review at 48–72 h, rinses.
  • "Classify endo-periodontal lesions." → with/without root damage → grades 1–3.
