01What is Amalgam

Definition A non-adhesive metallic restorative based on mercury alloyed with silver, tin and copper. Durable, safe and effective; subject of extensive long-term research.
Key fact Amalgam is non-adhesive — retention is purely mechanical, so the cavity preparation must include undercuts.

02Advantages & Disadvantages

✓ Advantages

  • Lower cost vs other restoratives
  • Greater durability — especially posterior
  • Less technique-sensitive
  • Long-term clinical evidence

✗ Disadvantages

  • Non-adhesive material
  • May cause cracks or fractures of the tooth
  • Requires specific cavity preparation (undercuts)
  • Can undergo corrosion
  • Extensive restorations → cracks in enamel and dentine

03Steps for Amalgam Placement

Pre-placement Place rubber dam — moisture control + safety from mercury exposure.
  1. Specific cavity preparation — undercuts for mechanical retention.
  2. Mechanical manipulation (trituration) — ~6 seconds on the amalgamator. Shake it; if you feel a pebble inside the capsule, it's ready.
  3. Condensation — pack into prep firmly to express mercury and adapt to walls.
  4. Burnishing & carving — establish anatomy and margins.
  5. Finishing & polishing — usually at a subsequent visit.

04When & Why to Replace Amalgam

  • Caries at the margins or recurrent
  • Loss of tensile strength / bulk fracture
  • Cracks in surrounding enamel/dentine from extensive restorations
Important Long-term durability of amalgam is well documented. Do not replace amalgam for non-specific or perceived health complaints unless the patient is fully informed of the implications (see Policy 6.18 below).

06Composite Resin — A Comparison

Composite Resin Strengths

  • Elastic modulus similar to natural tooth structure
  • Bonds to dental structure → more conservative preparations
  • Mercury-free
  • Aesthetic (tooth-coloured)

Amalgam vs Composite — Quick Take

  • Amalgam → durable, cheap, less technique-sensitive, but non-adhesive & non-aesthetic
  • Composite → aesthetic, bonds, conservative — but technique-sensitive

07Minamata Convention & ADA Policy 6.18

Minamata Convention on Mercury Requires parties to phase down the use of dental amalgam. Restricts use in vulnerable populations — children, pregnant women.
Safety evidence Amalgam releases only nanogram amounts of mercury. Current evidence does not link amalgam restorations with chronic or degenerative diseases.

Policy 6.18 — Position

ClausePosition
2.1Dental amalgam should continue to be available for use in appropriate clinical situations.
2.2Only capsulated amalgam complying with ISO 24234:2015 should be used.
2.3Minimise use in children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, and individuals with kidney disease.
2.4Practice mercury hygiene; correctly dispose of amalgam waste.
2.5Do not remove/replace amalgam for non-specific health complaints unless the patient is fully informed.
2.6Further research into adverse effects of alternatives is desirable.

01Tooth Sensitivity = Pain

Core concept Tooth sensitivity = pain. The dentine-pulp complex is connected to the nervous system. Pain arises from dentine exposure.

02The Dentine-Pulp Complex

  • Dentine tubules run through the dentine
  • Odontoblasts have cell processes (Tomes' fibers) that extend through the predentine into dentine
Physiology In a physiologic tooth condition, no dentine is exposed.

03Mechanism — Hydrodynamic Theory

Hydrodynamic Theory Stimuli that induce rapid fluid movements in dentinal tubules distort odontoblasts and afferent nerves, leading to a sensation of pain.

Stimuli that induce rapid fluid movement

  • Operative procedures — cutting, air drying
  • Tooth brushing
  • Temperature changes in the oral cavity
Equation Opened dentine tubules = Sensitivity

04Where is Dentine Exposed?

Cervical region — most susceptible

CEJ junction typeDescription
Overlapping junctionEnamel overlaps cementum (or vice versa)
Butt junctionEnamel meets cementum edge-to-edge
Gap junctionDentine exposed — normally covered by gingiva. If gingiva recedes → sensitivity

Other exposure locations

Hard tissue

  • Cavity preparations
  • Failed restorations
  • Caries lesions
  • Tooth surface loss (non-carious)
  • Fracture, trauma, cracks

Soft tissue / external

  • Gingival retraction (recession)

05Sensitivity is Not Always the Same

  • Depends on the situation
  • Depends on the progress speed
  • The dentine-pulp complex has the capacity to react to stimuli (e.g. tertiary dentine formation)

06How Do We Treat?

01Overview & Definition

Non-Carious Cervical Lesions (NCCLs) Loss of dental structure without bacterial involvement — they are NOT caries. Typically present at the cervical region or on incisal/occlusal surfaces. Increasingly common as patients keep their teeth longer and consume more acidic foods/drinks.

02The Four Main Causes

CauseMechanism
ErosionChemical dissolution of enamel/dentine by acids
AbrasionMechanical wear from an external object (toothbrush + paste, hard food)
AttritionMechanical wear from tooth-on-tooth contact (function or parafunction/bruxism)
AbfractionBiomechanical loss at the cervical region — occlusal stress causes flexure & microcracking

03Dental Erosion

Erosion Loss of surface tooth structure by chemical action, in the continued presence of demineralizing agents with low pH. The resulting surface is usually smooth and shiny (no bacteria, no plaque, no chalkiness).

Classification by aetiology

Extrinsic — acid from outside the body

  • Diet: citrus, fruit juices, soft drinks, sports drinks, wine, vinegar
  • Medications: chewable vitamin C, aspirin chewables, some inhalers
  • Occupational/environmental: chlorinated pool water (swimmers), wine tasters, battery industry

Intrinsic — acid from inside the body

  • GERD (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease)
  • Eating disorders — bulimia, anorexia (recurrent self-induced vomiting)
  • Chronic alcoholism, pregnancy hyperemesis, rumination

Idiopathic — cause unknown.

Saliva matters Salivary flow rate and buffering capacity strongly impact rate of progression. Low salivary flow (medications, Sjögren's, radiation, dehydration) accelerates erosion dramatically.

Diagnosis of erosion

  • Clinical examination — smooth, glossy, cupped lesions; loss of anatomical detail
  • Detailed dietary history — frequency, timing, sipping habits, sports drinks, citrus
  • Medical history — reflux symptoms, eating-disorder screening, medication review, salivary flow assessment

04Dental Abrasion

Abrasion Abnormal tooth-surface loss caused by direct frictional forces between teeth and external objects — or between contacting teeth in the presence of an abrasive medium (toothpaste, food, dust).

Common causes

  • Aggressive toothbrushing — hard bristles, horizontal scrubbing, excessive force
  • Abrasive toothpaste — high RDA (e.g. whitening pastes used long-term)
  • Habits — pipe stems, pens, hairpins, fingernails held between teeth
  • Improper use of floss, picks, interdental brushes

Clinical appearance

  • ROUNDED notch in the cervical portion of the FACIAL surface
  • Most marked on canines / premolars on the dominant-hand side
  • Smooth, polished, often shiny dentine
  • Usually asymptomatic unless deep

05Attrition

Attrition Mechanical wear of the incisal or occlusal tooth structure resulting from functional or parafunctional mandibular movement — tooth-on-tooth wear.

Common causes

  • Bruxism — parafunctional grinding (day or night)
  • Clenching
  • Heavy / edge-to-edge occlusion; malocclusion
  • Loss of posterior support → anterior teeth take all the load

Clinical appearance

  • FLAT wear facets that match perfectly on opposing teeth in centric & excursions
  • Shiny, polished facets (contrast: matte erosion surfaces)
  • Loss of incisal length, fractured restorations
  • Hypertrophic masseters

06Abfraction

Abfraction Wedge-shaped defects with SHARP margins at the cervical region. Believed to result from excessive occlusal forces causing flexure and microcracking of enamel rods at the cervical area where stress concentrates.

Clinical hallmarks

  • WEDGE / V-shaped defect with sharp internal line angles (vs the rounded notch of pure abrasion)
  • Cervical location — usually buccal; often a single tooth subjected to heavy lateral occlusal load
  • Frequently associated with wear facets, bruxism signs, or heavy occlusal contacts on the same tooth

08Treatment Principles

Treatment depends on EXTENT, LOCATION and SENSITIVITY of the lesion — but always starts with controlling the cause.

The treatment ladder

  1. Control aetiology ALONE — diet counselling, brushing technique, splint for bruxism, GERD referral
  2. Control aetiology + desensitiser — no restoration yet
  3. Control aetiology + restoration — when indicated (see below)

Indications for restoration

  • Exposed dentine that won't be saved by remineralisation alone
  • Tooth hypersensitivity not controlled by desensitisers
  • Need to recover anatomical form / shape correction
  • Need to recover structural resistance (deep lesions threatening pulp)
  • Aesthetics

09Dentine Hypersensitivity

Hydrodynamic Theory (Brännström) Pain from exposed dentine. Open dentinal tubules allow fluid movement that stimulates nerves in the pulp → sharp pain.

Desensitiser options

CategoryExamplesMechanism
Remineralisation agents Sodium fluoride varnish, CPP-ACP Block (occlude) dentinal tubules + rebuild mineral
Nerve desensitisers Potassium nitrate 5% Calm the nerve by reducing its ability to fire
In-office treatments Strong varnishes, glutaraldehyde/HEMA, oxalates, bioactive glass Seal tubules more effectively & quickly

10Restorative Materials for NCCLs

The two workhorse materials for NCCLs are Glass Ionomer Cement (GIC) and Resin Composite — each with distinct strengths.

Glass Ionomer Cement (GIC)

  • Chemical adhesion to dentine — bonds without micromechanical etching
  • Releases fluoride → helps in high-caries-risk & erosion patients
  • Lower risk of post-operative sensitivity
  • Less aesthetic — more opaque, less polishable
  • Good first choice when isolation is poor or lesion is sub-gingival

Resin Composite

  • Superior aesthetics, polishability & wear resistance
  • Requires good moisture control & an adhesive bonding system
  • Bonding to NCCL dentine is technique-sensitive

Choosing — Quick Guide

SituationPreferred material
Anterior, aesthetic-critical, good isolationResin composite
Sub-gingival margin / poor isolationGlass ionomer (or sandwich technique)
High caries risk / xerostomiaGlass ionomer (fluoride release)
Dentine hypersensitivity dominantEither + desensitiser; GIC is gentler
Deep lesion close to pulpGIC liner + composite overlay

01Aims of Vital Pulp Therapy

  • Maintain and preserve pulpal health in teeth challenged by caries, trauma, restorative procedures and anatomic anomalies
  • Maintain the integrity of the dentine-pulp complex — both soft and hard tissue repair after injury
  • Less invasive than conventional endodontic treatment
  • Tooth is kept alive as the pulp is preserved
Limited indications Reversible pulpitis of mature teeth, and pulpitis of deciduous and immature permanent teeth.
Reactionary / Reparative Dentinogenesis Formation of tertiary dentine in response to injury — harder than primary/secondary dentine.

02Causes of Injury & Factors to Consider

Cause of injury

  • Caries lesion progression
  • Cavity preparation
  • Pressure
  • Heat
  • Material toxicity — monomers, acidic components, eugenol

Factors

  • Patient age — root incomplete in children
  • Depth of the cavity — use X-rays
  • Quality of dentine — tertiary? secondary?

03Relevant Aspects to Obtain Success

AspectNotes
Pulpal & periapical statusConfirm radiographically + clinically
Root formation statusKids vs adults — immature roots can still develop
Clinical managementInfection control (rubber dam!)
Restorative treatmentAdequate coronal seal = success
Expected results Tertiary dentine formation · root formation (immature teeth) · asymptomatic tooth · functional tooth.

05Indirect Pulp Capping

Indirect Pulp Capping Sealing off the advancing carious lesion from the oral environment via selective (partial) caries removal. Avoids pulp exposure. Promotes pulpal healing with formation of reactionary dentine.

Indications

  • Both primary and permanent dentition
  • Large carious lesion
  • Asymptomatic tooth or mild transient symptoms
  • Preoperative radiograph confirms absence of radicular pathology

Technique

  1. Pain control and isolation — rubber dam!
  2. Remove superficial caries
  3. Remove all peripheral caries, leaving deep caries over pulp
  4. Finalize cavity preparation
  5. Restore tooth — ensure adequate coronal seal
Mantra Vital & Seal — sealing properly means bacteria do not progress.

07Materials Used in VPT

Material Requirements

  • Promote the innate capacity of pulpal cells to form hard tissue — dentine bridge formation
  • Antimicrobial properties without being toxic to pulp
  • Create an artificial barrier between vital pulp and oral cavity to prevent bacterial ingress

Calcium hydroxide — Ca(OH)2

  • High alkalinity → stimulates fibroblasts & enzyme systems; antibacterial; promotes pulp defence & repair
  • Barrier formed is incomplete — has tunnel defects
  • Ca(OH)2 is dimensionally unstable — disintegration allows microleakage & bacterial ingress
  • Resulting pulpal degeneration → dystrophic calcification, necrosis
  • Variable success rates ~70% over 5 years
  • Requires GI overlay to protect it from dissolving

Bioceramics — Calcium Silicate Cements (CSC)

  • Hydraulic calcium silicate powder containing numerous oxides
  • Biocompatible & bioactive — promotes mineral deposition & tissue integration
  • Excellent sealing ability — tight seal at the canal/dentine interface
  • High compressive strength & dimensional stability
  • MTA → may cause hard-tissue discolouration (radiopacifier)

Biodentine

Composition & properties Biodentine = 80% tricalcium silicate + 15% calcium carbonate filler + zirconia (radiopacifier). No metal oxides.
Releases Ca(OH)2 → stimulates mineralisation & hard tissue formation. Bonds to dentine by micromechanical retention with shear bond strength similar to GICs.
Clinical pearl When odontoblasts repair → tertiary dentine looks brown, but it is not caries. Apply VPT to the deepest point of the cavity (close to pulp), on the axial wall. Not required for gingival wall (no pulp underneath).
Time pressure? Seal the cavity prep even if you're out of time. Always give yourself enough time for definitive restoration; if not, leave it.

08Clinical Technique

  1. Rubber dam isolation — ALWAYS
  2. Selective caries removal
  3. Remove unsupported enamel with high-speed diamond bur + total removal of peripheral caries with slow-speed carbide round bur or spoon excavator
  4. Remove infected dentine on pulpal & axial walls with a spoon excavator without pressure
  5. Remove debris with water / saline (CHX may be used)
  6. Apply bioactive material to the deepest portion of pulpal & axial walls:
    • Bioceramics — MTA / Biodentine + LC-GI Lining over pulpal & axial walls. Apply the conditioner over the bioceramic LAST to minimise contact time.
    • Calcium hydroxide — Life / Dycal + conventional GIC base over pulpal & axial walls only (no conditioning)
  7. Place definitive restoration on the same appointment — success depends on a good seal!
  8. Schedule follow-up appointments
Not indicated Ledermix cement — no current evidence, no randomized clinical trials.

09VPT Follow-up

  • Assess at 6 and 12 months postoperatively
  • Then yearly intervals (if necessary) for 4 years thereafter
  • Tooth should respond positively to pulp sensibility testing within normal limits

01Definition & Mechanisms

Adhesion The state in which two surfaces are held together by interfacial forces, which may consist of valence forces, interlocking forces, or both.
Historical context Classic operative dentistry concepts were challenged in the 1950s (enamel) and again in the 1980s (dentine). Michael G. Buonocore observed industrial 85% phosphoric acid making surfaces rougher to hold things — the basis of acid etching.

1955 paper: "A Simple Method of Increasing the Adhesion of Acrylic Filling Materials to Enamel Surfaces."

Four mechanisms of adhesion

  1. Mechanical — penetration of resin and formation of resin tags within the tooth surface
  2. Adsorption — chemical bonding to inorganic (hydroxyapatite) or organic (mainly type I collagen) components of tooth structure
  3. Diffusion — precipitation of substances on tooth surfaces to which resin monomers can bond
  4. Combination of the previous three

02Applications in Dentistry

Direct restorations & aesthetics

  • Class I–VI carious lesions or traumatic defects
  • Change shape/colour of anterior teeth (veneers)
  • Seal pits & fissures
  • Bond fractured anterior fragments
  • Desensitize NCCLs & exposed root surfaces
  • Impregnate enamel/dentin — less susceptible to caries

Indirect & specialised uses

  • Improve retention for PFM or metallic crowns
  • Bond all-ceramic restorations
  • Bond orthodontic brackets
  • Bond splints for tooth luxations & periodontally involved teeth
  • Repair existing restorations
  • Foundations for crowns; bond posts
  • Internally reinforce endodontically treated roots
  • Seal root canals; seal resected apices

03Adhesion to Enamel

Enamel composition 90% mineral — hydroxyapatite. Prismatic (rod) arrangement. Prisms meet the surface at 90°.
How it works Phosphoric acid 37% for 30 sec → surface demineralized; periphery of enamel prisms dissolved → micro-retentions. A "sludge" of dissolved constituents forms — must be removed for adhesive to flow into the micro-retentions.

Current Strategies for Adhesion to Enamel

Three-step Etch-and-Rinse

  • Etchant: 37% phosphoric acid
  • Primer: not used on enamel — contains hydrophilic components; enamel has no water and can be dried completely, whereas dentine has moisture
  • Bonding agent: mainly hydrophobic monomers (Bis-GMA); small % hydrophilic monomers (HEMA); light-curing agents. Some systems also have fillers, fluor and antimicrobials.

Two-step Etch-and-Rinse

Can be used on both enamel and dentine.

  • Etchant: 37% phosphoric acid
  • Adhesive (Primer + Bond): hydrophobic monomers (Bis-GMA); hydrophilic monomers (HEMA); bifunctional molecules; solvent (ethanol, acetone or water — needs air stream to evaporate before LC); light-curing agents
Note Self-etch adhesive systems can be used but are not ideal for enamel.

04Technique for Adhesion to Enamel

  1. Tooth surface MUST be clean
  2. Apply 37% phosphoric acid on surface — and a little beyond restoration margins — for 30 sec
  3. Wash and dry — remove sludge from newly created micro-retentions
  4. Apply adhesive with microbrush; remove excess with a dry microbrush
  5. Blow little air to thin adhesive
  6. If applicable, evaporate the solvent (check adhesive composition)
  7. Light cure — check instructions for LC duration & number of layers
Moisture control Moisture compromises hydrophobic monomers used for enamel bonding. Stay dry.
Survival Comparing veneers (fewer variables than occlusal restos): adhesive bonding to enamel has a higher success & survival rate than to dentine.

05Adhesion to Dentine

  • There's a smear layer on cut dentine
  • Etched dentine surface → demineralized with exposed network of collagen fibrils + water
  • The collagen network is like spaghetti — if dried, collagen fibrils collapse and stick together → less micromechanical retention
Over-drying = ruined If collagen is over-dried, adding more water won't fix it. You must cut slightly further with low speed and restart the etching.
Hybrid Layer (HL) A layer mixing collagen fibrils (organic) with the adhesive (artificial) after light curing.

• Resin tags close dentine tubules → prevent post-op sensitivity (deep tags don't add much to adhesion).
• The hybrid layer itself ensures microretention AND protects collagen from degradation over time.

06Etch & Rinse Strategies (Dentine)

Three-step E&R

  • Etchant: 37% phosphoric acid
  • Primer: solvent (alcohol/acetone — removes excess water intentionally left on dentine); bifunctional molecules (hydrophilic + hydrophobic) — envelops collagen fibrils, receptive to hydrophobic bond agent
  • Bonding agent: Bis-GMA + small % HEMA; LC agents

Two-step E&R

  • Etchant: 37% phosphoric acid
  • Adhesive (Primer + Bond): solvent; bifunctional molecules; hydrophobic monomers; LC agents

07Etch & Rinse — Clinical Techniques

Three-step Technique

  1. Acid etch enamel only 30 s (periphery of prep)
  2. Wait 15 s, then acid etch dentine 15 s (inside of prep)
  3. Wash 30 s to remove acid & sludge
  4. Dry — enamel dry, dentine damp (shiny appearance). Use absorbent paper (cotton ball can leave fibres in collagen network)
  5. Primer — active application 20 s with microbrush. Removes water from enamel surface; evaporates residual dentine water; diffuses monomers; envelops collagen fibrils
  6. Bond — passive application 10 s (not rubbing — fluid); remove excess with clean microbrush + gentle airstream
  7. Light activation 10–20 s — get close to the cavity

Two-step Technique

  1. Acid etch enamel 30 s
  2. Wait 15 s; acid etch dentine 15 s
  3. Wash 30 s
  4. Enamel dry, dentine damp
  5. 1st layer adhesive — active application 20 s
  6. 2nd layer adhesive — active application 20 s. (Solvents; no air bubble trapping.) Evaporate solvent
  7. Remove excess with clean microbrush + gentle airstream (air is OK now — collagen protected by monomers)
  8. Light activation 10–20 s

08Longevity of E&R Systems

Marginal degradation of the adhesive interface happens from:

  • Hydrolysis — water inside the adhesive layer causes degradation
  • Endogenous degradation (MMPs) — enzymes released from dentine when etched degrade any exposed collagen not protected by monomers
  • Too much water present, solvent not evaporated → hybrid layer is gone
Reality check Studies show clinical performance of posterior CR restorations up to 33 years. Modern CR is better. Marginal staining & degradation alone are not secondary caries — many composites are replaced for ego, not necessity.

09Self-Etching Adhesives

Do not rely on phosphoric acid for adhesion to dentine.

Two-step Self-Etch

  • Self-etch Primer: solvent (removes water); acidic monomers (etching); bifunctional molecules — modifies but does NOT remove smear layer; exposes ~0.5–1 µm of intertubular collagen; envelops collagen fibrils; receptive to bond agent
  • Bonding agent: same as in 3-step E&R

One-step Self-Etch

  • Adhesive (Etch + Primer + Bond): solvent; acidic monomers; bifunctional molecules; hydrophobic monomers; LC agents
  • ⚠ Hybrid layer more susceptible to degradation

Self-Etching — Enamel?

Selective enamel etching No phosphoric acid in the self-etch system itself for enamel. Apply 37% phosphoric acid for 30 s on enamel only beforehand — best practice.

Self-Etching — Dentine: pH matters

10-MDP Most commonly used acidic monomer in self-etching adhesives.
• Chemical bond to HA in the hybrid layer — high affinity to inorganic surfaces
• Envelops & protects collagen

10Self-Etch — Clinical Techniques

Two-step Self-Etch

  1. Selective enamel etching — 37% phosphoric acid 30 s
  2. Wash 30 s
  3. Enamel dry with air; dentine unaffected (not etched)
  4. Dry air stream
  5. Primer — active application 20 s everywhere (contains acidic monomers). Solvent evaporation; removes residual water; diffusion of monomers; envelops collagen
  6. Bond — passive 10 s (not rubbing — fluid). Avoid trapping air. Remove excess with microbrush + gentle airstream
  7. Light activation 10–20 s
Key advantage Dentine is not etched → you can dry it freely; collagen isn't exposed.

One-step Self-Etch

  1. Selective enamel etching — 37% phosphoric acid 30 s
  2. Wash 30 s
  3. Dry air stream
  4. 1st layer adhesive — active 20 s
  5. 2nd layer adhesive — active 20 s. Solvent evaporation; monomers envelop collagen
  6. Passive bond 10 s. Remove excess. Light activation 10–20 s

11Universal (Multi-mode) Adhesives

Universal adhesives Etch? No etch? Sure. Works with any technique.
Greatest bond durability = selective enamel etching + self-etching mode.

12What is the Best Adhesive?

Depends on bonding location — enamel or dentine.

SubstrateBest systemBonding mechanism
EnamelEtch & RinseMicromechanical bond
DentineMild Self-EtchChemical bond to HA

Best Etch & Rinse

  • 3-step
  • Ethanol or water as solvent (acetone is unstable)
  • Primer in a separate bottle

Best Self-Etch

  • 2-step
  • Mild acidity
  • Selective enamel etching with phosphoric acid
  • Primer in a separate bottle

Why separate primer & bond bottles?

  • Provides a uniform hydrophobic layer to bind to the adhesive
  • When mixed in one bottle: hydrophilic monomers + solvent stay in the bonding layer → higher degradation speed

How to achieve greater longevity

  • Cavity margins on enamel (enamel adhesion better than dentine)
  • Material — how much enamel can you bond to?
  • Operator skill
  • Shake the bottle; avoid light exposure before use; close immediately after use

01Definition & Indications

Composite Resin A 3D combination of at least two materials, chemically distinct, which interact at the interface and result in a new material that responds in bulk to stresses — such as chewing.

Indications

Direct restorations Indirect restorations Repairs Sealants Bonding

02Composition

Organic Phase

Resinous matrix — the resin monomers that polymerize into a network.

Inorganic Phase

Filler particles — give bulk, strength, less shrinkage.

Coupling Agent — Silane Creates strong, durable chemical bonds between organic resin materials and inorganic substrates (the fillers).

07Placement Technique (general)

  1. Use incremental placement for most cavities (max 2 mm thick, max 2 walls)
  2. Or bulk-fill base (4–5 mm) where indicated, then layer with conventional CR
  3. Cure each increment individually
  4. Build with dentine + enamel layers if anatomic shading required

08Polymerization & Shrinkage

Setting reaction Initiator creates free radicals; monomers bond into polymers. The space "lost" between monomers as they bond = shrinkage.

Volumetric shrinkage

The whole increment shrinks — not just the surface.

Dynamics of shrinkage

StateWhat happens
Free shrinkageVolumetric change (unbonded)
Effective shrinkage (adhered)Volumetric change + interfacial tension
ResultStress on the bonded surface
Critical equation Reduce the amount of bonded surfaces in relation to free surfaces to reduce stress.

If interfacial tension > bond strength → failure.

What determines interfacial tension / shrinkage stress?

  • Geometry of the cavity — configuration, size
  • Technique — incremental layering, curing protocol
  • Restorative material — elastic modulus, monomeric composition

09C-Factor & Incremental Class II

Cavity Configuration Factor (C-Factor) Ratio of bonded to unbonded (free) surfaces in a cavity.
The greater the C-Factor, the greater the interfacial tension.

Incremental technique — Class II cavities

  1. Close the proximal wall(s) with 2+ oblique increments
  2. Close the proximal step with 2+ oblique increments (if needed)
  3. Max 2 mm thick & max 2 walls
  4. Cure each increment individually
  5. Proceed as for Class I

01Repair? Replace? Polish? Do Nothing?

Paradigm shift Increasing knowledge about caries + advances in adhesive materials → Black's "extension for prevention" has been replaced by the principles of minimal intervention dentistry.

Replacement of restorations is one of the most common procedures in dental practice — up to 56% of restorations placed by dentists are replacements of existing restorations.

Modern mantra Replace the old "if in doubt, take it out" with "as a last resort, take it out."

02Longevity & Failure Factors

Key reference Longevity of posterior composite restorations: not only a matter of materials (Demarco et al., 2012). The modern restorative material is not the main cause of replacement — patient, operator and cavity factors dominate.
Factor categoryDetail
Clinical variablesType, size and site of restoration. Class I = high longevity (good resistance); Class II = increased risk. Bigger restoration = higher failure chance. Risk increases posterior → anterior is reversed for occlusal load: 2nd & 1st molars highest risk (highest occlusal load), anteriors lowest.
OperatorQualification, experience, training with the material
SocioeconomicIncome & dental service access — more access can mean more replacements
DemographicPatient age — young = caries risk (diet, hygiene); old = caries risk (less saliva flow)
IndividualBruxism; high caries risk → secondary caries
Two definitions to separate Longevity = the time the restoration stays in the mouth (ends when replaced or it falls off).
Success = the time until the restoration first needs any intervention (such as repair).
Repair does not end the longevity of a restoration — it extends it.

03The Tooth Death Cycle

Every restoration will fail at some point. Each replacement removes more sound tooth structure, so the restoration becomes bigger and more complex. The goal: extend the time between replacements.

Three main events of the cycle

  1. Loss of tooth structure due to trauma or initial caries disease process
  2. Loss of tooth structure from the necessary preparation to receive the restoration
  3. With replacement of the restoration, the cycle repeats
Consequences Inevitable tooth weakening from unnecessary loss of dental tissue · increased restoration size and complexity with each replacement.

Factors that speed up the cycle

  • Age, health, diet, occlusal load, level of oral hygiene (biofilm control)
  • Cycle shortens each time the patient goes through a cariogenic challenge
  • Physical characteristics of the chosen material + skill of the operator

04Main Causes of Failure

Posterior teeth

  • Secondary (dental) caries and fracture of the restoration (Demarco et al., 2012)
  • Prevention: educate the patient — "don't open beer bottles with your teeth"

Anterior teeth

  • Aesthetics and fractures (Baldissera et al., 2013)
  • Prevention: educate the patient about frequent visits for repolishing

05What is Repair?

Repair The partial replacement of a restoration with defects or failures that do not correspond to more than 50% of the original restoration. It is an alternative to full replacement for localized defects, as long as clinically acceptable — and it requires good planning.
Example — when NOT to repair No repair for an aesthetic complaint of a Class IV restoration with a localized defect at the proximal in an aesthetic zone — replace instead.

06Advantages of Repair

Tissue & cost benefits

  • Preserves more sound dental structure
  • Reduces costs and clinical time
  • Simpler placement, less invasive
  • Increases clinical longevity of the restoration

Patient & safety benefits

  • Restricted to the defect location
  • Reduces potentially harmful effects on the pulp (repair is far from pulp)
  • Pain reduction — usually no local anaesthetic (maybe topical for the clamp)
  • Less risk of iatrogenic damage to adjacent teeth

07Indications & Contraindications

✓ Indications

  • Superficial marginal discoloration (no caries) bothering the patient
  • Colour correction & discolored areas
  • Marginal defects & closure of small gaps (food trapping)
  • Small / localized restoration fracture
  • Small / localized tooth fracture

✗ Contraindications

  • Defects no longer incipient (extensive fissure / whole resto compromised)
  • Bulk fracture of the composite
  • Deep staining of the tooth-restoration interface + caries recurrence
  • Patient reluctance (wants new replacement)
  • Difficulty monitoring — irregular attendance, high caries risk
  • Caries involving a large part of the existing restoration
  • History of previous failed repairs
The 2-or-more rule When two or more indications for repair are present in the same restoration, it is preferable to replace the entire restoration than to make several repairs — the restoration is globally degrading.

08The Decision Matrix

DecisionWhenExample

Who repairs more? Dentists who placed the original restoration are more likely to repair than replace, compared with dentists who did not. Constant changes between clinicians increase the rate of replacement.

09Composite Aging & the Oxygen-Inhibited Layer

Significant changes occur in composite resin during aging, which can affect the success of repair:

  • Water sorption and chemical degradation
  • Water diffusion through polymer chains and filler interfaces
  • Hydrolytic deterioration of polymer chains
Oxygen-Inhibited Layer (OIL) The surface layer of resin that does not polymerize in contact with oxygen in the air. It is responsible for the chemical bond with the next incremental (or repair) layer.
Why fresh composite repairs easily — but old does not You can repair composite at the time of placement without any surface treatment, as long as there's no contamination. Finishing/polishing & contaminant contact reduce the free radicals available. If the OIL is removed, you must recreate it artificially with silane.

10Surface Treatment — Purposes

Surface treatment makes an existing CR surface receptive to bonding a new layer when the OIL is gone and the surface is contaminated.

PurposeHow
Remove contaminated surface layer (from saliva) → increase surface energyRoughening
Expose fresh filler particles & polymeric matrixRoughening
Increase surface area → micromechanical retentionDiamond bur OR air-abrasion with aluminium oxide
Chemical bond with new incrementPhosphoric acid · silane (on composite) · adhesive system (per surface type: enamel / dentine / old composite)

11Repair Technique — Step by Step

  1. Rubber dam — always necessary
  2. Re-preparation restricted to the defective area — remove caries, unsupported enamel, defective restoration portion. This step also leaves the old restoration rough.
  3. Conditioning with 37% phosphoric acid — cleans the surface to be joined; demineralizes/preps enamel (30 s) & dentine (15 s). On resin = 15 s. Wash & dry with absorbent paper or cotton pellet.
    If using a self-etch adhesive: phosphoric acid only on enamel and resin (selective etching).
  4. Silane on the surface of the old restoration → wait 60 s → remove solvent with air blast (silane carries a solvent, just like primer)
  5. Adhesive system: if dentine is exposed, apply primer → remove solvent with air blast. Apply bond → remove excess with a clean microbrush (applied to dentine, enamel, old restoration)
  6. Light cure for 10–20 s
  7. Repair using the regular incremental technique
  8. Occlusal adjustment
  9. Finishing and polishing
Etch times cheat Enamel 30 s · Dentine 15 s · Resin 15 s.

12Silane vs Adhesive — What Each Does

Silane

  • Chemical bonding capacity with the filler particles exposed on the roughened surface
  • Increases the adhesive system's wettability on the roughened surface (raises surface energy)
  • Coupling agent — artificially recreates the lost OIL function

Adhesive

  • Promotes chemical bonding with the organic matrix exposed on the roughened surface
  • Acts as the intermediate bonding agent between the new composite and the old restoration
Take-home Repair of composite restorations should be a frequently used clinical procedure, preferred whenever possible — it increases longevity of resin composite and is more conservative toward tooth structure.

13Amalgam — Repair, Replace, Repolish

Can you repair amalgam?

Disadvantages of amalgam "repair" Amalgam is not adhesive — there is no chemical bond between new and old amalgam. A "repair" would still require bulk and retention (cavity prep) — not conservative.
Instead, repair amalgam with composite resin.

Why / when to replace amalgam?

  • Both amalgam and composite show long longevity in studies
  • Composites fail by secondary caries; amalgam resists secondary caries well (Demarco et al., 2012)
  • Over time, amalgam often causes cracks — big risk on posterior teeth (creep), worse in Class II, worse in large restorations
Intersecting cracks Crack runs mesial → distal, can enter the pulp chamber and into the root surface (very poor prognosis).
• Molar teeth: OR 8.79 for intersecting cracks
• Teeth with amalgam restoration: OR 4.81
Replace with composite resin (Renner et al., 2025).
CR or Amalgam? The lesser of two evils Big restoration, bruxism, load-bearing area, high caries risk → which do you choose? Risk of caries vs risk of crack. Inform the patient. The lecturer recommends composite resin — secondary caries is easier to treat than a catastrophic crack.

How to replace an amalgam with composite

  1. Rubber dam — absolute isolation; moisture control + protect against amalgam residue
  2. Remove amalgam at high speed with diamond bur + plenty of water
  3. Surface clean — remove possible amalgam waste. Pigmented dentine will always be visible — don't remove it, mask it with the new restoration
  4. Selective conditioning of enamel with 35–37% phosphoric acid
  5. ACTIVE application of universal adhesive
  6. Final restoration using incremental technique (composite resin)

When to repolish amalgam

  • Amalgam restoration with intact margins but a rough surface
  • Repolishing with abrasive rubbers improves smoothness, gloss, corrosion resistance → extends longevity
  • Smooth, polished restorations = less biofilm retention (even though amalgam's secondary-caries risk is low, it's still possible)
