Neoplasia — Part 1

Nomenclature, features and clinical effects. What makes a growth a neoplasm, what makes a neoplasm malignant, and how a lesion that never spreads can still kill.

Dr Joseph Ryan

01

Tumour and neoplasm

Two words that are not quite synonyms.

Tumour

Two meanings

1. An abnormal swelling.
2. A neoplasm.

Neoplasm

The precise term

A lesion resulting from the autonomous or relatively autonomous abnormal growth of cells that persists in the absence of the initiating stimulus.

The phrase that carries the definition

“Persists in the absence of the initiating stimulus.” That is what separates a neoplasm from every adaptive response in Lecture 2. Hypertrophy, hyperplasia and metaplasia all regress when the stimulus is removed. A neoplasm does not — it has become autonomous.

Why “tumour” is loose

Every neoplasm is a tumour, but not every tumour is a neoplasm — an abscess or a haematoma is an abnormal swelling too. In clinical writing, prefer neoplasm when you mean the autonomous growth.

02

Benign and malignant

The distinction that decides everything else.

Benign

Stays put

Microscopic and gross characteristics are relatively innocent, implying that it will remain localised and is amenable to local surgical removal.

Malignant

Invades and spreads

Implies that the lesion can invade and destroy adjacent structures and spread to distant sites (metastasize) to cause death.

FeatureBenignMalignant
Growth rateSlowRelatively rapid
MitosesInfrequentFrequent and atypical
Histological resemblance to normal tissueGoodVariable, often poor
Nuclear morphologyNear normalUsually enlarged, hyperchromatic, irregular outline, multiple nucleoli and pleomorphic (variable sizes and shapes)
InvasionNoYes
MetastasesNoYes
BorderOften circumscribed or encapsulatedOften poorly defined borders
NecrosisRareCommon on skin or mucosal surfaces
Direction of growth on skin or mucosal surfacesOften exophyticOften endophytic
Two rows do the real work

Invasion and metastasis are the only absolute discriminators. Everything else — growth rate, mitoses, nuclear appearance — is a tendency with exceptions. A slow-growing, well-differentiated lesion that invades is still malignant.

Exophytic vs endophytic

Exo- outward, endo- inward. A benign lesion tends to pile up above the surface; a malignant one tends to burrow into it. Clinically that is why an ulcerated, indurated lesion with rolled margins worries you more than a pedunculated lump.

A lesion grows slowly and closely resembles its tissue of origin, but has invaded underlying muscle. Benign or malignant?

Malignant. Growth rate and degree of differentiation are only tendencies — some malignancies are slow-growing and well differentiated. Invasion and metastasis are the defining features, and invasion of adjacent structures is by definition malignant behaviour.

03

Naming benign neoplasms

The -oma suffix, and four names that break the rule.

Benign neoplasms generally take the suffix -oma:

Fibroma Chondroma Adenoma Cystadenoma
The exceptions — these end in -oma but are malignant
Lymphoma Mesothelioma Melanoma Seminoma

Four names, all malignant despite the suffix. This is a favourite examination trap, and it is worth memorising the list as a list.

Note the clinical consequence

Because melanoma reads like a benign lesion, some clinicians write “malignant melanoma” for emphasis. That is redundant — melanoma is malignant by definition. The benign counterpart is a naevus.

04

Naming malignant neoplasms

Sarcoma, carcinoma, adenocarcinoma — named by tissue of origin.

TermOriginExample
SarcomaOrigin in solid mesenchymal tissuesLiposarcoma, chondrosarcoma
CarcinomaMalignant neoplasm of epithelial cells regardless of tissue originSquamous cell carcinoma
AdenocarcinomaMalignant neoplasms of glandular or ductal epithelia, that grow in a glandular patternAdenocarcinoma of the thyroid
Why carcinoma matters most to you

Squamous cell carcinoma is a carcinoma of epithelial cells — and oral mucosa is squamous epithelium. It is by far the most common oral malignancy, and it is the endpoint of the carcinogen exposures covered in the companion carcinogens lecture.

Reading a diagnosis

The name usually tells you both the tissue and the behaviour. Lipo- + -sarcoma = malignant, from fat. Chondro- + -oma = benign, from cartilage. Learn the root and the suffix separately and most names decode themselves — apart from the four exceptions above.

05

Components and differentiation

What a neoplasm is made of, and how closely it resembles its origin.

Component 1

Parenchyma

Transformed / neoplastic cells — the tumour proper.

Component 2

Stroma

Supporting connective tissue — host-derived, and the route by which the tumour gets its blood supply.

Differentiation

The extent to which neoplasms resemble their parenchymal cells of origin, both morphologically and functionally.

Anaplasia

Lack of differentiation. Tumours composed of undifferentiated cells are said to be anaplastic, a feature that is a reliable indicator of malignancy.

Between the two sits dysplasia — disordered but not yet malignant growth, covered in detail in the next section.

06

Oral epithelial dysplasia

Sixteen histological features, in two groups.

Dysplasia is graded on architectural features (how the epithelium is organised as a whole) and cellular features (how individual cells look).

Architectural featuresCellular features
Irregular epithelial maturationAbnormal variation in nuclear size
Loss of polarity of basal cellsAbnormal variation in nuclear shape
Drop-shaped rete ridgesAbnormal variation in cell size
Increased number of mitotic figuresAbnormal variation in cell shape
Abnormally superficial mitotic figuresIncreased nuclear–cytoplasmic ratio
Premature keratinisation in single cellsAtypical mitotic figures
Keratin pearls within rete ridgesIncreased number and size of nucleoli
Loss of epithelial cell cohesionNuclear hyperchromatism
The two most characteristic architectural signs

Drop-shaped rete ridges and abnormally superficial mitotic figures. Normally mitosis is confined to the basal layer; finding dividing cells high in the epithelium means maturation control has been lost. Both are worth naming specifically.

Where you have met this before

Lecture 6 raised the correlation between oral yeast carriage and the presence and degree of epithelial dysplasia, and left the direction of causation open. This table is what “degree of dysplasia” actually means — the pathologist is counting these features.

Why is a mitotic figure in the upper third of the epithelium abnormal?

Because proliferation is normally confined to the basal layer, with cells maturing and losing the ability to divide as they move towards the surface. An abnormally superficial mitotic figure means that maturation is irregular and the normal coupling between position and differentiation has broken down — one of the architectural features of dysplasia.

07

Clinical effects of benign neoplasms

Five ways something that never spreads can still cause harm.

  1. Pressure on adjacent tissues
    e.g. a benign meningeal tumour causing epilepsy.
  2. Obstruction to the flow of fluid
    e.g. a benign epithelial tumour blocking a duct.
  3. Production of a hormone
    e.g. a benign thyroid tumour causing thyrotoxicosis.
  4. Transformation into a malignant neoplasm
    e.g. an adenomatous polyp progressing to an adenocarcinoma.
  5. Anxiety
    Because the patient fears that the lesion may be something more sinister.
“Benign” does not mean harmless

A benign meningeal tumour can cause epilepsy purely by pressure; a benign thyroid tumour can cause thyrotoxicosis purely by function. Location and secretion matter as much as behaviour — which is why benign lesions in confined spaces (skull, duct, airway) are treated seriously.

Do not dismiss the last one

The lecture lists anxiety as a clinical effect in its own right. For a patient with an oral lump, uncertainty is itself a burden — and it is a reason to investigate and communicate promptly rather than watch indefinitely.

08

Clinical effects of malignant neoplasms

Everything above, plus spread.

  1. Pressure on and destruction of adjacent tissue
    Not just displacement — destruction.
  2. Formation of secondary tumours (metastases)
    The defining malignant effect.
  3. Blood loss from ulcerated surfaces
    Malignant lesions ulcerate; ulcers bleed.
  4. Obstruction of flow
    e.g. a malignant tumour of the colon causing intestinal obstruction.
  5. Production of a hormone
    e.g. ACTH and ADH from some lung tumours.
  6. Other paraneoplastic effects
    Causing weight loss and debility.
  7. Anxiety and pain
     
Compare the two lists

Benign neoplasms cause harm through position and function. Malignant ones add destruction, spread, bleeding and systemic effects. The extra items are all consequences of invasion — which is why invasion is the pivotal property.

09

Cachexia and paraneoplastic syndromes

Systemic illness that the tumour does not directly cause.

Cancer cachexia

Many cancer patients suffer progressive loss of body fat and lean body mass, accompanied by profound weakness, anorexia, and anaemia — a condition referred to as cachexia.

Paraneoplastic syndromes

Symptom complexes that occur in patients with cancer and that cannot be readily explained by local or distant spread of the tumour or by the elaboration of hormones indigenous to the tissue of origin of the tumour.

The most common are:

Hypercalcaemia Cushing syndrome Nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis

Why they matter clinically

  1. They may come first
    Such syndromes may represent the earliest manifestation of an occult neoplasm.
  2. They can be serious in themselves
    The pathologic changes may be associated with significant clinical illness and may even be lethal.
  3. They mislead
    The symptom complex may mimic metastatic disease, thereby confounding treatment.
Read the definition carefully

A paraneoplastic syndrome is explicitly not explained by spread, and not explained by hormones normal to that tissue. A lung tumour producing ADH qualifies — lung does not normally make ADH. A thyroid tumour producing thyroid hormone does not.

Why can a paraneoplastic syndrome complicate treatment rather than just accompany it?

Because the symptom complex may mimic metastatic disease. A patient with hypercalcaemia or unexplained systemic illness may appear to have widespread metastases, which could push management towards palliation when the primary is still potentially curable. Paraneoplastic syndromes may also be the earliest manifestation of an occult neoplasm and may themselves be lethal.

Epidemiology of cancers

Where the burden falls, and how survival has changed.

The lecture presents international and Australian data: age-standardised incidence and mortality worldwide, age-specific rates by sex, five-year relative survival trends from 1984–1988 to 2009–2013, the top ten cancer sites worldwide, and the most commonly diagnosed cancers in Australia.

What to take from the graphs
  • Incidence and mortality maps differ. High-income regions have higher recorded incidence; the mortality-to-incidence ratio is worse in lower-income regions — reflecting detection and access to treatment, not just biology.
  • Incidence rises steeply with age. Consistent with the multistep model in Part 2 — cancer requires accumulated mutations, and accumulation takes time.
  • Five-year survival has improved substantially across the period shown, for both sexes.
Relevance to dentistry

Lip, oral cavity and pharyngeal cancers do not top the global lists, but they are cancers a dentist is uniquely placed to detect early — and the carcinogens driving them are the subject of the companion lecture (L7C).

Carcinogenesis needs both columns

The same exposure produces cancer in one person and not another. The carcinogen supplies the insult; host factors determine how it is metabolised, how well the damage is repaired, and whether a transformed cell survives. L7C develops this for oral cancer specifically, where P450 polymorphisms and DNA repair capacity vary between individuals.

Two you have already met

Viruses as carcinogens ran through Lecture 6 — HPV in oropharyngeal cancer, EBV in lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, HHV-8 in Kaposi's sarcoma. And premalignant lesions include the leukoplakias from that lecture: chronic hyperplastic candidosis and syphilitic leukoplakia.

Summary and glossary

Part 1 on one screen.

The through-line

  1. A neoplasm is autonomous
    It persists in the absence of the initiating stimulus — unlike every adaptation in Lecture 2.
  2. Invasion and metastasis define malignancy
    Every other feature is a tendency with exceptions.
  3. Names encode origin and behaviour
    -oma benign, sarcoma from mesenchyme, carcinoma from epithelium — with four -oma exceptions that are malignant.
  4. Differentiation is a spectrum
    Anaplasia reliably indicates malignancy; good differentiation does not exclude it.
  5. Benign lesions still cause harm
    By pressure, obstruction, hormone production, transformation and anxiety.
  6. Systemic effects need not come from spread
    Cachexia and paraneoplastic syndromes.

Glossary

Tumour
An abnormal swelling; also used to mean a neoplasm.
Neoplasm
A lesion resulting from the autonomous or relatively autonomous abnormal growth of cells that persists in the absence of the initiating stimulus.
Benign
Remains localised and is amenable to local surgical removal.
Malignant
Can invade and destroy adjacent structures and spread to distant sites to cause death.
Metastasis
Spread of a neoplasm to a distant site.
Exophytic
Growing outward from a surface; typical of benign surface lesions.
Endophytic
Growing inward into the tissue; typical of malignant surface lesions.
Pleomorphic
Showing variable sizes and shapes — of nuclei or cells.
Hyperchromatic
Darkly staining, reflecting increased nuclear DNA.
Sarcoma
Malignant neoplasm of solid mesenchymal tissues.
Carcinoma
Malignant neoplasm of epithelial cells, regardless of tissue origin.
Adenocarcinoma
Malignant neoplasm of glandular or ductal epithelium growing in a glandular pattern.
Parenchyma
The transformed / neoplastic cells of a tumour.
Stroma
The supporting connective tissue of a tumour.
Differentiation
The extent to which a neoplasm resembles its parenchymal cells of origin, morphologically and functionally.
Anaplasia
Lack of differentiation; a reliable indicator of malignancy.
Dysplasia
Disordered growth with architectural and cellular atypia, short of malignancy.
Cachexia
Progressive loss of body fat and lean body mass with weakness, anorexia and anaemia.
Paraneoplastic syndrome
A symptom complex in a cancer patient not explained by local or distant spread, or by hormones indigenous to the tissue of origin.
