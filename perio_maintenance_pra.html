1. Where SPT sits in the treatment sequence

Systemic phase→ Initial phase therapy→ 4–6 wk OH review→ Reassessment at 3 months→ Supportive Periodontal Therapy
  • Reassessment can loop to corrective phase therapy (surgery etc.) and back.
  • If disease recurs during SPT, the patient returns to initial phase therapy — the cycle restarts.
  • Disease control phase = initial therapy → reassessment. Maintenance phase = SPT.

2. Supportive periodontal therapy

Objectives AAP 2003

  1. Prevent/minimise disease recurrence and progression
  2. Prevent or reduce tooth loss by monitoring the dentition and any prostheses
  3. Detect and treat other oral diseases in a timely manner

Maintenance may be temporarily discontinued and active treatment reinstituted if recurrent disease is found — more likely when recall intervals are over-extended.

Every SPT visit has three parts

Part 1
Examine & re-plan
  • Examination, risk assessment, review diagnoses
  • New plan for persisting/new risk factors and unresponsive sites
Part 2
Treat
  • OH reinforcement
  • Debridement, prophylaxis
  • Preventive care (e.g. topical fluoride)
Part 3
Set the recall
  • Next recall based on the risk profile — i.e. the PRA

3. Evidence: does maintenance actually work?

The one table that tells the story

ScenarioStudyTeeth lost / patient / year
No treatmentBecker 19790.36
Treatment, no maintenanceBecker 1984a0.22
Treatment + maintenanceBecker 1984b0.11 (excl. hopeless teeth; 0.24 overall)

Each step roughly halves tooth loss. Treatment helps; treatment plus maintenance helps most.

Becker et al. 1979 — no treatment

  • 30 patients (699 teeth) consulted but declined treatment; reviewed 10 years later.
  • 58 teeth lost (7.7%) — 36 if hopeless teeth are excluded. 0.36 teeth/patient/year.

Becker et al. 1984a — treatment without maintenance

  • 44 patients (1,117 teeth), reviewed after an average 5.25 years.
  • 67 teeth lost (6.0%), 0.22 teeth/patient/year. 34 of 51 hopeless teeth were still present.
  • Without maintenance, pockets drift deeper: 25.7% of healthy 1–3 mm surfaces became 4–6 mm; 62% of 4–6 mm sites stayed 4–6 mm; 31.6% of ≥7 mm sites stayed ≥7 mm.

Becker et al. 1984b — treatment with maintenance

  • 95 patients (2,414 teeth) over 3–11 years; surgically treated sites; average recall 5.2 months.
  • 150 teeth lost (6.21%) between reviews — 0.24/patient/year, or 0.11 excluding hopeless teeth.
  • Pockets improved: 55% of 4–6 mm sites fell to 1–3 mm; of ≥7 mm sites, 35% fell to 1–3 mm and 47% to 4–6 mm.

Axelsson et al. 1981 — specialist recall vs return to GDP

  • 90 treated patients (including surgery). Every 3rd patient sent back to their GDP; the rest stayed on a strict recall program.
  • Surfaces with PD > 3 mm: ~1% in recall patients vs 20% in GDP patients.
  • Attachment loss in the GDP group: 44% lost ≤ 1 mm, 55% lost 2–5 mm, 1% lost ≥ 6 mm.

Axelsson et al. 2004 — 30-year prevention study

  • Repeated OHI and professional maintenance: 2-monthly for 2 years, then 3–12-monthly for 28 years.
  • Very low caries and periodontal disease: only 21 teeth lost to periodontitis and/or caries over 30 years.
  • The most common cause of tooth loss was root fracture of root-filled teeth — not perio, not caries.

4. Tooth loss during maintenance — Hirschfeld & Wasserman 1978

600 patients, maintained 4–6-monthly for an average of 22 years. 15,666 teeth present after initial treatment.

GroupTeeth lostPatients
Well maintained (WM)0–3499 (300 lost none)
Downhill (D)4–976
Extreme downhill (ED)10–2325
  • 7.1% of teeth lost to periodontal causes; 1.2% to other causes. Loss showed bilateral symmetry.
  • Questionable prognosis (QPx) criteria: furcation involvement, deep non-eradicable pocket, extensive bone loss, marked mobility (2–2.5+ of 3).
  • 2,139 teeth were given QPx; 666 were lost — i.e. most "questionable" teeth survived.
  • 394 of those were lost by the D and ED groups — only ~1/6 of patients.
  • Non-QPx teeth lost: WM 20.5%, D 22.7%, ED 55.4% — in the worst patients, over half the teeth lost had not been flagged as questionable.

What to take from this

Tooth loss concentrates in a minority of susceptible patients. For most well-maintained patients, even "questionable" teeth last decades. This is exactly why we risk-assess the patient, not just the tooth.

5. Why periodontal risk assessment?

  • Few prognostic factors can truly identify sites, teeth or patients at risk of recurrent breakdown.
  • Disease occurrence varies between patients — some need more frequent visits and extra therapy.
  • PRA estimates the likelihood of further attachment loss and helps tailor the plan and recall interval.
  • Most, if not all, periodontitis patients are at moderate–high risk of recurrence, so they need continuous risk assessment and lifelong supportive care.

The slide prints the moderate band for PD and tooth loss as "4–8", overlapping the low band at 4. Lang & Tonetti's text puts up to 4 in low, so moderate is effectively 5–8. Systemic/genetic has no moderate band — it is either absent or high.

1. Bleeding on probing

  • Gentle probing (0.25 N) — an objective inflammatory parameter.
  • Full-mouth BOP % summarises plaque control, host response and compliance in one number.
  • Low PPV, but high NPV — absence of BOP predicts stability Lang 1990.
  • 25% separated stable from recurrent patients over 4 years in private practice Joss 1994. < 10% = low risk Lang 1990.

2. Residual pockets ≥ 5 mm

  • Many deep residual pockets, or pockets deepening during SPT, = high risk of progression Badersten 1990; Claffey 1990.
  • ≥ 9 sites ≥ 5 mm was a patient-level risk for progression during maintenance Matuliene 2008.

3. Tooth loss from 28 teeth

  • A true end-point reflecting a lifetime of oral disease and trauma, even when the cause is unknown.
  • A shortened dental arch (premolar to premolar, 20 teeth) is still functional and doesn't cause mandibular dysfunction Witter 1990, 1994.
  • Losing more than 8 of 28 usually impairs function Käyser 1981, 1994, 1996.

4. Bone loss relative to age

  • Radiographic bone height reflects past disease; dividing by age gives the rate. Calculation in the next section.
  • Angular defects predict loss Papapanou & Wennström 1991, retrospective: teeth lost over 10 years — horizontal bone loss 13%, degree 2 angular (2.5–4 mm) 50%, degree 3 angular (≥ 4.5 mm) 70%.

5. Systemic and genetic

  • Diabetes (T1 and T2) has the strongest evidence for modifying susceptibility/progression Grossi 1994; Emrich 1991.
  • Twin studies: genetics explain variance in severity Michalowicz 2000. IL-1 genotype raises risk of progression and severity McGuire & Nunn 1999.
  • Clinically, assess genetic predisposition through family history in the CDE — chairside genetic tests aren't practical.

6. Smoking

  • Dose-dependent effect on prevalence and severity Grossi 1994, 1995; poorer response to non-surgical Darby 2005 and surgical Ah 1994 therapy; cessation helps Grossi 1997; Preshaw 2005; Rosa 2014.
  • For PRA, a former smoker = quit more than 5 years ago. Heavy smoker = more than 20/day → high risk.

Don't mix up the two "former smoker" thresholds

PRA: former smoker means > 5 years since quitting. Healing response: quitting ≥ 1 year gives comparable healing to non-smokers (Grossi 1997; Preshaw 2005 — from the Risk Factors lecture). Different questions, different cut-offs.

7. Calculating bone loss / age

  1. Find the worst-affected site on a periapical, OPG or bitewing.
  2. Estimate bone loss as a % of root length. Healthy crest sits 1–2 mm below the CEJ; measure to the base of the defect.
  3. On a bitewing, 1 mm = 10% bone loss.
  4. Divide % bone loss by age.

Worked example (from lecture)

40-year-old, 90% bone loss at worst site
90 ÷ 40 = 2.25 → > 1.0 → high risk

Thresholds: < 0.5 low · 0.5–1.0 moderate · > 1.0 high (scale in 0.25 steps). The same %BL/age calculation is used for grading in the 2017 AAP/EFP classification (Grade A < 0.25, B 0.25–1.0, C > 1.0).

Limitations of using the worst site

  • Overestimates if one isolated deep lesion has a local cause.
  • Underestimates in generalised advanced disease.
  • But the worst site does tend to reflect the history of the whole dentition Persson 2003.

8. Building the PRA diagram

The six parameters are plotted on six axes of a hexagon. The inner bold ring marks the low/moderate boundary; the outer bold ring marks moderate/high. Join the points — the size of the polygon shows the risk.

In practice

The online tool at perio-tools.com/PRA draws the diagram and suggests a recall interval from the inputs (age, teeth, BOP sites, sites ≥ 5 mm, missing teeth, % bone loss, systemic, smoking). The vectors reflect current evidence and will be revised as research develops.

9. High-yield exam summary

Conclusion points from the lecture

  • Long-term tooth retention depends on the frequency and quality of maintenance.
  • Use risk assessment, and educate patients on why maintenance matters.
  • SPT is for life.
  • Patients who don't return for SPT lose more teeth than compliant patients.

Likely exam prompts

  • "Justify lifelong SPT." → Becker's three-step tooth-loss comparison → Axelsson 1981 recall vs GDP → Axelsson 2004 → Hirschfeld: loss concentrates in susceptible patients.
  • "Calculate a patient's PRA." → place each of the six parameters into low/mod/high → apply the three rules. Watch the edge cases: one moderate is still low; systemic positive jumps straight to high.
  • "Calculate BL/age." → worst site, % of root length from the healthy crest, ÷ age; state the risk band.
  • "Why is BOP useful despite a low PPV?" → high NPV; absence of bleeding predicts stability.
