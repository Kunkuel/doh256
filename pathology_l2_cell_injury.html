Cellular Responses to Stress & Injury

Cells sit in a steady state. Stress and injury disturb it — and the cell must either adapt, recover, or die. This lecture covers the four adaptations, the mechanisms of injury, and the two routes to cell death.

01

Injury and the response to injury

The whole lecture rests on one chain of events. Learn it precisely.

Injury — a four-step process

1. A stress is placed upon cells and tissues → 2. resulting in changes in their structure and function → 3. the cells and tissues react to the injury in an attempt to maintain homeostasis → 4. the pathological process leads to microscopic then macroscopic changes, which manifest as signs and symptoms.

Response to injury

There are only two possible destinations:

Outcome A

Homeostasis / adaptation

The cell adapts and survives.

Outcome B

Cell death

The cell succumbs and dies.

Which occurs depends on two variables: the properties of the injury (type, severity, duration) and the cells and tissues involved.

02

Stress, injury and adaptation

Homeostasis

The steady state in which cells are maintained, within a range. Stress and injury alter this steady state.

  • Cells may adapt to stress or succumb to injury
  • A cell may die due to sustained or progressive injurious stimuli
03

Cellular adaptation — overview

Usually a response to variations in functional demand. Four responses to know.

04

Cell types by regenerative capacity

Whether a tissue can regenerate depends on the dividing ability of its cells.

TypeDividing behaviourExamples
LabileDivide throughout life — continuously renewingSurface epithelia (oral mucosa, skin), bone marrow, GI lining
StableDivide when needed — normally quiescent but can be recruitedLiver, kidney, fibroblasts
PermanentDivide only during foetal life — cannot replace themselves after birthNeurons, cardiac and skeletal muscle
Why it matters

Injury to permanent cells — after a heart attack or nerve damage — cannot be repaired by regeneration. It is replaced by scar, with permanent loss of function.

Why can the liver regenerate after partial resection, but the heart cannot after an infarct?

The liver consists of stable cells: normally quiescent, but capable of dividing when needed. Cardiac muscle consists of permanent cells, which divide only during foetal life and cannot replace themselves after birth — so the damaged area is replaced by scar tissue with a permanent loss of function.

05

Hypertrophy and hyperplasia

Both increase organ size, but by different means.

↑ cell size

Hypertrophy

An increase in the size of cells. The organ enlarges because each cell grows bigger — more organelles and proteins — not because there are more cells.

↑ cell number

Hyperplasia

An increase in the number of cells in a tissue or organ.

Classic example — the pregnant (gravid) uterus

During pregnancy the uterus enlarges by both mechanisms: smooth muscle cells hypertrophy and hyperplasia occurs. Comparing the smooth muscle of a normal uterus with a gravid uterus shows the difference dramatically.

A pure example of hypertrophy is cardiac myocytes: heart muscle enlarges under pressure load because heart cells are permanent and cannot divide, so they can only grow larger.

The logic

Whether a tissue responds to increased demand by hypertrophy, hyperplasia or both is decided by section 4. Permanent cells can only hypertrophy. Labile and stable cells have the option of hyperplasia too.

06

Atrophy

A decrease in the size and/or number of cells.

Physiologic atrophyPathologic atrophy
  • Development — normal remodelling
  • Ageing
  • Decreased function / disuse
  • Loss of innervation (denervation)
  • Loss of blood supply
  • Pressure
  • Lack of nutrition
  • Loss of stimulation
Dental relevance — atrophy of the residual ridge

After teeth are lost, the alveolar bone is no longer loaded through the teeth. With the loss of functional stimulation and altered pressure, the residual ridge atrophies — a central consideration in denture design and implant planning.

A limb is immobilised in a cast for eight weeks and the muscle visibly shrinks. Which cause of atrophy is this, and is it physiologic or pathologic?

Decreased function / disuse, and it is classified as pathologic atrophy — the physiologic causes are limited to normal development and ageing. The muscle reduces in size and number of cells because the functional demand placed upon it has fallen.

07

Metaplasia

Definition

Metaplasia is the transformation of one mature tissue type into another — usually a reversible change into a cell type better suited to withstand a chronic stress.

Clinical example — Barrett’s oesophagus

Chronic acid reflux stresses the normal squamous lining of the lower oesophagus, which undergoes metaplasia to a columnar (intestinal-type) epithelium that better tolerates acid. Although metaplasia is adaptive, this change carries an increased risk of dysplasia and malignancy — adaptation is not always benign.

08

Causes of cell injury

When adaptation is not possible, injury results.

Category 1

Physicochemical agents

Trauma, temperature, radiation, chemicals, toxins.

Category 2

Biological agents

Bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites.

Category 3

Loss of stimulation

Withdrawal of hormonal or growth factor signals.

Category 4

Deprivation

Oxygen and glucose deprivation.

09

Targets of cell injury

Whatever the cause, injurious stimuli affect the structure and function of three targets.

10

Mechanisms — ischaemia and hypoxia

Key principle

Cellular processes and structures are inter-dependent. An insult to one process or organelle causes derangement of another — injury cascades.

TermMeaningExample
IschaemiaReduced blood supply — so both oxygen and glucose fallAtherosclerosis
HypoxiaReduced oxygen supply specificallyPneumonia
High yield

Because ischaemia cuts off both glucose and oxygen, it is generally more damaging than hypoxia alone — it starves the cell of both its fuel and the oxygen needed to burn it.

11

Mechanisms — ionising radiation

Definition

Radiation that causes the formation of ions on interaction with matter.

TypeExamples
Electromagnetic radiationX-rays and gamma rays
Particulate radiationAlpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (electrons), neutrons

Mode of action

  • Mainly indirect — through reactive oxygen species (ROS)
  • Also direct — direct damage to DNA and proteins

Two types of effect

Dose determines severity

Deterministic effects

Predictable according to the dose received. There is a threshold, and severity rises with dose — e.g. skin burns, mucositis.

Dose determines probability

Stochastic effects

Only the probability is related to the dose, not the severity. No safe threshold — e.g. radiation-induced cancer.

Tissues differ in their response to ionising radiation: rapidly dividing tissues are the most sensitive.

A radiographer receives a small dose of scatter radiation. Which type of effect is the concern, and why is there no safe threshold?

The concern is a stochastic effect, principally radiation-induced cancer. For stochastic effects only the probability is related to dose — the severity is not. A single ionising event can in principle produce a mutation, so there is no dose below which the risk becomes zero; the risk simply becomes smaller. Deterministic effects such as burns, by contrast, have a threshold below which they do not occur at all.

12

Reversible vs irreversible injury

The single most important distinction in cell injury: can the cell still recover?

High yield

If the injurious stimulus persists or is severe, the cell crosses the threshold, becomes irreversibly injured, and dies.

13

Microscopic manifestations of reversible injury

Two classic appearances.

Manifestation 1

Hydropic degeneration (cellular swelling)

  • Swelling of the cell
  • Due to failure of ion and fluid homeostasis — pump failure lets Na⁺ and water accumulate
  • Cytoplasm appears more eosinophilic (pink)
Manifestation 2

Fatty change

  • Accumulation of fat
  • Due to dissociation of lipid from protein
  • Clear vacuoles within the cytoplasm
Histology cue — kidney tubules

The lecture contrasts normal kidney tubules with reversibly injured tubules (swollen and vacuolated but structurally intact) and irreversibly injured tubules (loss of nuclei and architecture). Loss of the nucleus is the key marker of irreversibility.

14

Necrosis and autophagy

Necrosis

Necrosis

Unintended, pathologic cell death following irreversible injury. Cell contents leak out, which elicits inflammation in the surrounding tissue.

Autophagy

  • Organelles are digested, and the products are used to provide nutrients for the cell — a survival strategy under stress such as starvation
  • Will result in apoptosis if the cell can no longer cope
The idea

Autophagy is the cell “eating itself” to survive — a last-ditch attempt at homeostasis before programmed death.

15

Apoptosis

Programmed cell death — orderly, regulated, and silent.

Definition

Apoptosis is ‘programmed cell death’, which may be physiologic or pathologic. The cell fragments in a controlled way, there is no inflammatory response, and harmful cellular contents — lysosomal contents — are contained.

Regulation of apoptosis

Inhibitors (promote survival)Inducers (trigger death)
  • Growth factors
  • Extracellular matrix
  • Sex steroids
  • Some viral proteins
  • Growth factor withdrawal
  • Loss of matrix attachment
  • Glucocorticoids
  • Some viruses
  • Free radicals
  • Ionising radiation
  • DNA damage
  • Ligand-binding at ‘death receptors’

The two apoptotic pathways

Anti- and pro-apoptotic proteins

GroupDetail
Anti-apoptotic (survival)Bcl-2, Bcl-xL
Pro-apoptotic (death)Bak, Bax
p53Attempts DNA repair; triggers apoptosis if repair fails
Why p53 matters

The balance between Bcl-2/Bcl-xL and Bak/Bax decides whether a cell lives or dies. Loss of p53 function — common in cancer — lets DNA-damaged cells survive and accumulate further mutations.

16

Apoptosis vs necrosis

A high-yield comparison. Know these differences cold.

FeatureApoptosisNecrosis
NatureProgrammed, regulatedUnintended, unregulated
TriggerPhysiologic or pathologicAlways pathologic — severe injury
Cell sizeShrinks and fragmentsSwells and ruptures
MembraneIntact — contents packaged into apoptotic bodiesLost — contents leak out
InflammationNoneYes — leaked contents provoke it
Energy (ATP)Requires energy — an active processOccurs with energy failure
The one-line answer

Apoptosis is tidy, deliberate and silent; necrosis is messy, accidental and inflammatory. Every other difference follows from whether the membrane stays intact.

Why does necrosis cause inflammation but apoptosis does not?

In necrosis the cell swells and ruptures, so its contents — including lysosomal enzymes — leak into the surrounding tissue, and those leaked contents are exactly the stimulus that provokes inflammation. In apoptosis the membrane remains intact: the cell fragments into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies that contain the harmful contents, so nothing escapes to trigger a response.

17

DNA damage and radiotherapy

Outcomes for a DNA-damaged cell

Outcome 1

Cell death

Either immediately, or at the next attempted mitosis.

Outcome 2

Repair

Successful repair → no further consequence.

Outcome 3

Permanent change

A permanent change in genotype — the basis of neoplasia.

Radiotherapy

  • Aims to induce damage to tumour cells
  • Works because cancer cells divide rapidly
  • Normal cells are also damaged
  • Normal cells that divide rapidly are the most sensitive — oral mucosa, bone marrow, hair follicles
Dental relevance

Because oral mucosa is a rapidly dividing labile tissue, it is among the most radiosensitive normal tissues in the body. This is why mucositis is such a common and debilitating side effect of head and neck radiotherapy, and why these patients need intensive oral care.

Why does head and neck radiotherapy so reliably cause oral mucositis?

Radiotherapy exploits the fact that rapidly dividing cells are most vulnerable to DNA damage — which is what makes it effective against tumours. But normal cells that also divide rapidly are equally sensitive, and oral mucosa is a labile tissue that divides continuously throughout life. It therefore sustains substantial collateral damage, producing mucositis.

18

Summary

Adaptation

Stimuli that disturb homeostasis can cause stress or injury to cells. Cells adapt to stress by:

↑ size — Hypertrophy ↑ number — Hyperplasia ↓ size/number — Atrophy change of type — Metaplasia

Injury

  • Injurious stimuli affect metabolic pathways and cellular processes, DNA and protein, and membrane structure and function
  • Cells can be reversibly or irreversibly injured
  • If the injurious stimulus persists or is severe, the cell becomes irreversibly injured

Cell death

ApoptosisNecrosis
Programmed cell death · physiologic or pathologic · no inflammation Unintended pathologic cell death · elicits inflammation
Where this leads

Necrosis elicits inflammation — and inflammation is the entire subject of Lecture 3A. The dying cell of this lecture is the injurious stimulus of the next.
