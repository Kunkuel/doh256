01 Injury & the Response to Injury

The whole lecture rests on one chain of events, so it's worth learning it precisely.

◆

Injury — a 4-step process 1. A stress is placed upon cells and tissues → 2. resulting in changes in their structure and function → 3. cells and tissues react to the injury in an attempt to maintain homeostasis → 4. the pathological process leads to microscopic then macroscopic changes, which manifest as signs and symptoms.

Response to injury

When a cell is injured, there are only two possible destinations — and which one occurs depends on two variables:

Outcome A

Homeostasis / adaptation

The cell adapts and survives.

Outcome B

Cell death

The cell succumbs and dies.

The response depends on (a) the properties of the injury (type, severity, duration) and (b) the cells and tissues involved (their type and vulnerability).

02 Stress, Injury & Adaptation

◆

HomeostasisThe steady state in which cells are maintained (within a normal range). Stress and injury alter this steady state.

  • Cells may adapt to stress or succumb to injury
  • A cell may die due to sustained or progressive injurious stimuli

03 Cellular Adaptation — Overview

Adaptation is usually a response to variations in functional demand. There are four adaptive responses to know.

04 Cell Types by Regenerative Capacity

Whether a tissue can regenerate (and how it adapts) depends on the dividing ability of its cells.

Cell typeDividing behaviourExamples
LabileDivide throughout life — continuously renewingSurface epithelia, bone marrow, GI lining
StableDivide when needed — normally quiescent but can be recruitedLiver, kidney, fibroblasts
PermanentDivide only during foetal life — cannot replace themselves after birthNeurons, cardiac & skeletal muscle
▶

Why it mattersInjury to permanent cells (e.g. after a heart attack or nerve damage) cannot be repaired by regeneration — it is replaced by scar, with permanent loss of function.

05 Hypertrophy & Hyperplasia

↑ Cell size

Hypertrophy

An increase in the size of cells. The organ enlarges because each cell grows bigger (more organelles & proteins), not because there are more cells.

↑ Cell number

Hyperplasia

An increase in the number of cells in a tissue or organ.

◆

Classic example — the pregnant (gravid) uterusDuring pregnancy the uterus enlarges by both mechanisms: smooth muscle cells hypertrophy (each fibre grows) and hyperplasia occurs (more fibres). Compare smooth muscle of a normal uterus with a gravid uterus and the difference is dramatic.

A commonly-cited pure example of hypertrophy is cardiac myocytes — the heart muscle enlarges under pressure load because heart cells are permanent and cannot divide, so they can only grow larger.

06 Atrophy

◆

DefinitionA decrease in the size and/or number of cells. Atrophy can be a normal (physiologic) process or a sign of disease (pathologic).

Physiologic atrophyPathologic atrophy
  • Development (normal remodelling)
  • Ageing
  • Decreased function / disuse
  • Loss of innervation (denervation)
  • Loss of blood supply
  • Pressure
  • Lack of nutrition
  • Loss of (hormonal) stimulation
🦷

Dental relevance — atrophy of the residual ridgeAfter teeth are lost, the alveolar bone is no longer loaded through the teeth. With the loss of functional stimulation (and pressure changes), the residual ridge atrophies — a key consideration in denture design and implant planning.

07 Metaplasia

◆

DefinitionThe transformation of one mature tissue type into another. Usually a reversible change of a cell type into one better suited to withstand a chronic stress.

⚠

Clinical example — Barrett's oesophagusChronic acid reflux stresses the normal squamous lining of the lower oesophagus, which undergoes metaplasia to a columnar (intestinal-type) epithelium that better tolerates acid. Although metaplasia is adaptive, this change carries an increased risk of dysplasia and malignancy — a reminder that adaptation is not always benign.

08 Cell Injury — Causes

When adaptation is not possible, injury results. The lecture groups causes into four categories.

Category 1

Physicochemical agents

Trauma, temperature, radiation, chemicals, toxins.

Category 2

Biological agents

Bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites.

Category 3

Loss of stimulation

Withdrawal of hormonal or growth-factor signals.

Category 4

Deprivation

Oxygen and glucose deprivation.

09 Targets of Cell Injury

Whatever the cause, injurious stimuli affect the structure and function of three main targets.

10 Mechanisms — Ischaemia & Hypoxia

▶

Key principleCellular processes and structures are inter-dependent — an insult to one process or organelle causes derangement of another. Injury cascades.

TermMeaningExample
IschaemiaReduced blood supply (so both oxygen and glucose fall)Atherosclerosis
HypoxiaReduced oxygen supply specificallyPneumonia
★

Because ischaemia cuts off both glucose and oxygen, it is generally more damaging than hypoxia alone — it starves the cell of both its fuel and the oxygen needed to burn it.

11 Mechanisms — Ionising Radiation

◆

DefinitionRadiation that causes the formation of ions on interaction with matter.

TypeExamples
Electromagnetic radiationX-rays and gamma rays
Particulate radiationAlpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (electrons), neutrons

Mode of action

  • Mainly indirect — through reactive oxygen species (ROS) (free radicals generated from water)
  • Also direct — direct damage to DNA and proteins

Effects of radiation — two types

Dose determines severity

Deterministic effects

Predictable according to the dose received. There is a threshold, and severity rises with dose (e.g. skin burns, mucositis).

Dose determines probability

Stochastic effects

Only the probability is related to dose (not the severity). No safe threshold — e.g. radiation-induced cancer.

▶

Tissues differ in their sensitivity to ionising radiation — rapidly dividing tissues are the most vulnerable (see radiotherapy, §17).

12 Reversible vs Irreversible Injury

The single most important distinction in cell injury: can the cell still recover?

★

If the injurious stimulus persists or is severe, the cell crosses the threshold and becomes irreversibly injured — and dies.

13 Microscopic Manifestations of Reversible Injury

Two classic microscopic appearances of reversible injury.

Manifestation 1

Hydropic degeneration (cellular swelling)

  • Swelling of the cell
  • Due to failure of ion and fluid homeostasis (pump failure → Na⁺ & water accumulate)
  • Cytoplasm looks more eosinophilic (pink)
Manifestation 2

Fatty change (steatosis)

  • Accumulation of fat within the cell
  • Due to dissociation of lipid from protein
  • Seen as clear vacuoles within the cytoplasm
🔬

Histology cue — kidney tubulesThe lecture contrasts normal kidney tubules with reversibly injured (swollen, vacuolated but intact) and irreversibly injured tubules (loss of nuclei & architecture). Loss of the nucleus is a key marker of irreversibility.

14 Necrosis & Autophagy

Necrosis

⚠

NecrosisUnintended, pathologic cell death following irreversible injury. Cell contents leak out, which elicits inflammation in the surrounding tissue.

Autophagy

  • Organelles are digested, and the products are recycled to provide nutrients for the cell — a survival strategy under stress (e.g. starvation)
  • If the cell can no longer cope, autophagy will ultimately result in apoptosis
▶

Think of autophagy as the cell "eating itself" to survive — a last-ditch attempt at homeostasis before programmed death.

15 Apoptosis

◆

Apoptosis — "programmed cell death"An orderly, regulated form of cell death that can be physiologic or pathologic. The cell fragments in a controlled way, there is no inflammatory response, and harmful cellular contents (e.g. lysosomal contents) are contained within membrane-bound bodies.

Regulation of apoptosis

Inhibitors (promote survival)Inducers (trigger death)
  • Growth factors
  • Extracellular matrix (attachment)
  • Sex steroids
  • Some viral proteins
  • Growth factor withdrawal
  • Loss of matrix attachment
  • Glucocorticoids
  • Some viruses
  • Free radicals
  • Ionising radiation
  • DNA damage
  • Ligand-binding at "death receptors"

The two apoptotic pathways

Anti- and pro-apoptotic proteins

GroupProteins
Anti-apoptotic (survival)Bcl-2, Bcl-xL
Pro-apoptotic (death)Bak, Bax
p53 — the "guardian"Attempts DNA repair; if repair fails, it triggers apoptosis to remove the damaged cell
▶

The balance between Bcl-2/Bcl-xL and Bak/Bax decides whether a cell lives or dies. Loss of p53 function (common in cancer) lets DNA-damaged cells survive and accumulate mutations.

16 Apoptosis vs Necrosis

A high-yield comparison — know these differences cold.

FeatureApoptosisNecrosis
NatureProgrammed, regulatedUnintended, unregulated
TriggerPhysiologic or pathologicAlways pathologic (severe injury)
Cell sizeShrinks & fragmentsSwells & ruptures
MembraneIntact — contents packaged into apoptotic bodiesLost — contents leak out
InflammationNoneYes — leaked contents provoke it
Energy (ATP)Requires energy (active process)Occurs with energy failure

17 DNA Damage & Radiotherapy

Outcomes for a DNA-damaged cell

A cell whose DNA has been damaged has three possible fates:

Outcome 1

Cell death

Either immediately, or at the next attempted mitosis.

Outcome 2

Repair

Successful repair → no further consequence.

Outcome 3

Permanent change

A permanent change in genotype (mutation) — the basis of neoplasia.

Radiotherapy — applying these principles

  • Aims to induce damage to tumour cells
  • Works because cancer cells divide rapidly and are therefore vulnerable to DNA damage
  • Normal cells are also damaged — this causes side effects
  • Normal cells that divide rapidly are the most sensitive (e.g. oral mucosa, bone marrow, hair follicles) — explaining common radiotherapy toxicities such as mucositis
★

Radiotherapy is a direct clinical application of everything in this lecture: it deliberately delivers irreversible DNA injury to exploit the vulnerability of rapidly dividing cells.

18 Summary

Adaptation

Stimuli that disturb homeostasis can cause stress or injury. Cells adapt to stress by:

↑ size — Hypertrophy ↑ number — Hyperplasia ↓ size/number — Atrophy change of type — Metaplasia

Injury

  • Injurious stimuli affect metabolic pathways & cellular processes, DNA & protein, and membrane structure & function
  • Cells can be reversibly or irreversibly injured
  • If the stimulus persists or is severe, injury becomes irreversible

Cell death

ApoptosisNecrosis
Programmed cell death · physiologic or pathologic · no inflammation Unintended pathologic cell death · elicits inflammation
