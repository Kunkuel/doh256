1. Clinical evaluation of periodontal healing

Four parameters are used to evaluate healing following scaling and root debridement:

Why review at 3 months?

The greatest change in probing depth reduction and gain in clinical attachment occurs within 1–3 months post-SRP for incisors and premolars Badersten et al. 1984 and molars Loos et al. 1989 — although healing and maturation of the periodontium may continue over the following 9–12 months Badersten et al. 1984.

2. A word on periodontal probing

  • Periodontal probes assess the health of the periodontium and are used to evaluate change throughout the treatment process.
  • A tapered probe, tip diameter 0.5 mm, at a force of 0.25 N (25 g) may be most suitable for assessment Bulthuis et al. 1998.

How much is 0.25 N?

Roughly the pressure required to blanch your fingernail. Use this as the calibration cue in clinic.

Why histological ≠ clinical sulcus depth Listgarten 1980, review

  • Insertion force
  • Connective tissue destruction — the probe meets decreased resistance from gingival tissues at diseased sites
  • The probe may pass the apical cells of the junctional epithelium Armitage 1977
  • Angle of insertion
  • Contour of the tooth surface
  • Presence of calculus

3. Armitage et al. 1977 — where does the probe tip actually stop?

Investigated the level at which the periodontal probe corresponds to the level of connective tissue attachment, at a standardised force of 0.25 N, in three tissue states.

Tissue stateProbe tip relative to JE
Healthy gingivaStopped short of JE by 0.39 mm
Experimental gingivitisStopped short of JE by 0.10 mm
Advanced periodontitisWent past JE by 0.24 mm

The pattern to remember

As inflammation increases, the probe penetrates further. In health it under-reads; in disease it over-reads. Probing depth is therefore a clinical approximation, not a histological measurement.

4. Badersten et al. 1985 — reproducibility of probing

Studied reproducibility of probing level measurements in incisors, canines and premolars, examining both inter- and intra-examiner reproducibility.

  • 90% of recordings could be reproduced within ± 1.0 mm for both inter- and intra-examiner recordings.
  • Reproducibility improved following non-surgical periodontal therapy.
  • Reproducibility varied significantly between tooth types, tooth surfaces and pocket depths.

Clinical consequence

A change of 1 mm or less between visits may simply be measurement error. Only changes beyond ± 1 mm should be interpreted as genuine disease progression or improvement.

5. Four definitions you must be able to separate

TermDefinition
Repair Healing of a wound by tissue that does not fully restore the architecture or function of the part. This is what occurs following SRD at periodontal pockets.
Reattachment To attach again — the reunion of epithelial and connective tissues with root surfaces and bone, such as occurs after an incision or injury. Not to be confused with new attachment.
New attachment Union of connective tissue or epithelium with a root surface that has been deprived of its original attachment apparatus. May be epithelial adhesion and/or connective tissue adaptation or attachment, and may include new cementum.
Regeneration Reproduction or reconstitution of a lost or injured part. The ultimate goal of periodontal therapy.

The central tension of this lecture

The ultimate goal is to regenerate the lost attachment apparatus — but periodontal treatment by SRD generally results in wound healing by repair, not regeneration. The attachment formed is primarily a long junctional epithelium rather than new connective tissue attachment Waerhaug 1978; Caton & Zander 1979.

Distinguishing reattachment from new attachment

Reattachment — the root surface still has its original attachment apparatus (e.g. after a clean incision). New attachment — the root surface has been stripped of it by disease. The word "new" signals that something was lost first.

6. Histological healing response following SRD

Waerhaug 1978a/b

  • After non-surgical SRD the pocket epithelium remains — its remnants give rise to reformation of the junctional epithelium.
  • JE forms (as a long junctional epithelium, LJE) at the tooth surface in areas previously covered by subgingival calculus and plaque → the pathological pocket is reversible.
  • Ideal healing can only take place if adequate daily supragingival plaque control is performed.
  • New subgingival plaque can reform if remnants of subgingival plaque are left behind, or if supragingival plaque remains on the tooth surface for prolonged periods.

What kind of attachment forms?

  • Healing following SRP occurs by formation of an LJE, not new connective tissue attachment Caton & Zander 1979, or with minimal CT only Beaumont et al. 1984.
  • Critically, the LJE provides a proper barrier function against plaque infection and is not inferior to a dento-gingival junction of normal length Magnusson et al. 1983; Beaumont et al. 1984.

The race for the root surface

It is the cells that repopulate the root surface that determine the nature of the attachment that forms. In LJE formation, the faster-migrating epithelial cells arrive first, preventing the slower-migrating PDL cells from reaching the surface. This epithelial downgrowth in turn prevents new connective tissue attachment.

This single mechanism is also the entire rationale for guided tissue regeneration — a membrane exists to exclude epithelium so the PDL cells can win the race.

7. The long junctional epithelium

Characteristics Shimono et al. 2003

  • Similar ultrastructure to junctional epithelium of normal length.
  • Composed of non-keratinised squamous epithelium.
  • Consists of 2–3 layers aligned parallel to the tooth surface.
  • Attaches to the cementum surface by hemidesmosomes and basal lamina.
  • Permeable, due to its wide intercellular spaces.
  • Fewer capillaries in the connective tissue compared with normal JE.
  • Can be produced rapidly during wound healing, due to high proliferative activity of the epithelium.

8. Is the LJE permanent?

  • It has been suggested that the LJE is a transient feature of the healing process on the path to connective tissue attachment, and not the final healing stage Listgarten et al. 1982.
  • Epithelial cells of the LJE can migrate coronally at 4 weeks post-treatment, resulting in a shorter LJE over time Katayanagi 1992.
  • Therefore a rapidly produced LJE may become shorter over time, being replaced by connective tissue Katayanagi 1992.

9. High-yield exam summary

Conclusion points from the lecture

  • Clinical parameters (PPD, CAL, BOP) are how we evaluate the healing response to periodontal therapy.
  • Histological studies show healing occurs via repair with formation of an LJE, which has a similar ultrastructure to JE of normal length.
  • The LJE provides barrier function similar to JE of normal length.
  • The LJE may be transient, eventually replaced by connective tissue.
  • Knowledge of these clinical and histological changes allows the clinician to accurately diagnose persistent sites of inflammation and plan treatment, and to inform the patient of the changes that will occur after treatment.
