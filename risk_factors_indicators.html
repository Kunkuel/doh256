1. Definitions — the four categories

TermDefinitionEvidence base
Risk factor Environmental, behavioural or biologic factors which, when present, increase the likelihood that an individual will develop disease. Exposure must occur before disease onset. Exposure can be at single, multiple or continuous time points. Interventions can be identified which, when implemented, help modify the risk factor. Cross-sectional AND longitudinal studies
Risk determinant
(background characteristic)		 Sometimes substituted for "risk factor", but should be reserved for risk factors that cannot be modified. —
Risk indicator Probable risk factors identified in cross-sectional studies but not yet confirmed longitudinally. Cross-sectional only
Risk predictor / marker Associated with increased risk for disease but do not cause the disease. Cross-sectional and longitudinal

The discriminators

Factor vs determinant = modifiable or not. Factor vs indicator = confirmed longitudinally or not. Factor vs marker = causal or merely associated. Getting an item into the right category is a classic short-answer question.

2. Risk factor: tobacco smoking

Why smoking qualifies as a risk factor Genco & Borgnakke 2013

  1. Consistency of results across many studies
  2. Strength of the association
  3. Dose–response of the association
  4. Temporal sequence of smoking and periodontal disease
  5. Biologic plausibility

Dose-dependent increase in prevalence and severity

  • OR 2–5 for loss of attachment and OR 3–7 for radiographic bone loss Grossi et al. 1994, 1995
  • Current smokers show greater disease progression in CAL and RBL Machtei et al. 1999
  • Increased severity of periodontitis associated with smoking Eke et al. 2012
  • 5.3× greater risk of PPD > 3.5 mm in smokers vs non-smokers Stoltenberg et al. 1993
  • LOA is pronounced in the anterior maxilla and mandible Preber & Bergström 1986
  • Greater risk of tooth loss Salvi et al. 2014; Dannewitz et al. 2006

Poorer response to non-surgical therapy

  • Significantly less PPD reduction in smokers Preber et al. 1995; Darby et al. 2005
  • Significantly less BOP reduction despite greater plaque reduction Preber & Bergström 1986
  • Inferior improvement in PPD and CAL Renvert et al. 1998; Darby et al. 2005, with greater persistence of P. intermedia Darby 2005 and A.a Renvert 1998 post-SRD

Poorer response to surgical therapy

  • Significantly less PPD reduction and less CAL gain immediately after active therapy and during 6 years of maintenance Ah et al. 1994
  • Poorer healing (decreased PPD/CAL response, membrane exposure) following guided tissue regeneration for intra-bony defects Tonetti et al. 1995 and recession defects Trombelli & Scabbia 1997

Cessation is beneficial — and this is the hopeful message for patients

  • Comparable healing response in former smokers who quit ≥ 1 year ago Grossi et al. 1997; Preshaw et al. 2005
  • May take 24 months for this to become clinically evident Rosa et al. 2014

The classic exam trap

Smokers show less bleeding on probing despite having more disease, because reduced vascularity masks the inflammatory signal. BOP therefore under-estimates disease in smokers — do not read a "quiet" chart as health.

3. Smoking — pathogenic mechanisms

Approximately 4000 toxic substances are reported in tobacco, including carbon monoxide, oxidising radicals, carcinogens (e.g. nitrosamine) and nicotine Genco & Borgnakke 2013.

System alteredEffect
Microbiology
(controversial)		 Greater prevalence of anaerobes due to reduced oxygen tension in pockets — more T. forsythia and P. gingivalis in smokers Zambon et al. 1996, Erie County study, pooled samples.
In contrast: no differences in A.a, P.g, P.i Preber et al. 1992; no difference in P.g, A.a, P.i, E.c, F.n Boström et al. 1998.

T-cell subsets Increased CD3+, CD4+, CD8+ T cells and increased T-cell proliferation Loos et al. 2004.
Wound healing Poor healing response due to impairment of fibroblasts James et al. 1999.

4. The dental professional's role in smoking cessation

Dental practitioners should always ask about current and past smoking.

Pack-years

Pack-years = number of packs smoked per day × number of years smoking.

1 pack-year = the cumulative exposure of smoking 1 pack of 20 cigarettes per day for 1 year. E.g. a smoker of 20 cigarettes/day for 15 years has 15 pack-years.

Former smokers should always be congratulated for quitting. Document how much they used to smoke, how many they smoked, and when they quit.

Brief intervention — the 5 A's

StepWhat it involves
ASKAsk about smoking status — part of the medical history
ADVISEAdvise smokers of the association between oral disease and smoking
ASSESSAssess interest in and readiness to attempt cessation
ASSISTAssist the quit attempt — willpower alone, self-help materials, primary care intervention programs, nicotine replacement therapy (patches, lozenges, gum, nasal spray, inhalator), varenicline (Champix)
ARRANGEArrange follow-up or referral to smoking cessation services

5. Risk factor: diabetes mellitus

Scale and definition

  • In Australia, an estimated 1.2 million people over 2 years old have diabetes mellitus — 5.1% of the population.
  • T2DM accounted for 85% of total DM diagnoses in 2014–15.
  • DM is a chronic condition characterised by hyperglycaemia secondary to an absolute or relative decrease in insulin.

Classification

  • T1DM — autoimmune-mediated destruction of pancreatic beta-cells, normally responsible for producing insulin.
  • T2DM — combination of peripheral insulin resistance and progressive insulin secretory failure. Most common form; colloquially "lifestyle" related.
  • Hyperglycaemia of pregnancy (including gestational diabetes) — onset or first recognition during pregnancy; usually resolves post-partum.
  • Other — hyperglycaemia secondary to another primary disorder (drug-induced, cystic fibrosis, pancreatitis, Cushing syndrome).

Diagnostic thresholds

TestDiagnostic of DM
HbA1c — glycated haemoglobin, formed by non-enzymatic binding of sugars to haemoglobin; a surrogate marker for hyperglycaemia over the preceding 3 months ≥ 48 mmol/mol (6.5%)
Fasting BGL (confirmed on two occasions) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L
Random BGL (confirmed on two occasions) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L
OGTT — 75 g glucose load, samples before and after; also diagnoses "prediabetes" where levels lie between normal and diagnostic Fasting ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or 2-hour post-load ≥ 11.1 mmol/L

The evidence

  • Greater prevalence and severity of periodontitis with poor metabolic control — diabetics are 2× more likely to exhibit LOA Grossi et al. 1994; Emrich et al. 1991.
  • Poorer response to therapy with poor control — after NSPT, patients with poor metabolic control show greater prevalence of attachment loss (> 2 mm) and greater and faster occurrence of PPD > 4 mm Tervonen & Karjalainen 1997.
  • A small body of evidence supports adverse effects of periodontal disease on glycaemic control, diabetes complications, and development of T2DM (and possibly gestational diabetes) Borgnakke et al. 2013.

Periodontal treatment may improve diabetic control

Time after NSPTHbA1c improvementSource
3 months~0.38%Corbella et al. 2013
4 months0.29–0.6%Gay et al. 2014; Simpson et al. 2015
6 months0.31%Corbella et al. 2013

Mechanism — the AGE/RAGE axis

Hyperglycaemia drives formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) and expression of their signalling receptor RAGE, resulting in:

  • Immune cell dysfunction
  • Altered phenotype and function of cells in the periodontium
  • Cytokine imbalance and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines

Hyperglycaemia also increases ROS and oxidative stress — directly and indirectly via AGE/RAGE — and modulates the RANKL/OPG ratio. All of this is complemented by ecological shifts in the subgingival biofilm and increased adipokines. End result: accelerated periodontitis.

The relationship is bidirectional.

6. Risk factor: pathogenic bacteria in plaque

The composition or quality of the plaque biofilm may increase risk for periodontitis in a susceptible person.

  • Plaque accumulation at the gingival margin causes gingivitis, reversible with oral hygiene Löe et al. 1965.
  • But plaque accumulation does not mean someone will develop periodontitis — this depends on the interaction between host and plaque.

The three established aetiologic agents Genco et al. 1996

Identified in both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies:

  • Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans
  • Porphyromonas gingivalis
  • Tannerella forsythia

Additional evidence implicating these organisms Haffajee & Socransky 1994

  1. Their elimination or suppression impacts the success of therapy
  2. There is a host response to these pathogens
  3. Virulence factors are associated with these pathogens
  4. Inoculation into animal models induces periodontal disease

Moderate evidence — though not fully meeting the criteria for causation — also implicates Campylobacter rectus, Eubacterium nodatum, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, Prevotella nigrescens, Peptostreptococcus micros, Streptococcus intermedius and Treponema denticola Genco et al. 1996.

  • Keystone pathogens such as P. gingivalis induce a shift from a symbiotic microbial community to a dysbiotic one.
  • → The quantity of plaque may matter less than the quality of plaque in determining risk.
  • Anatomic factors — furcations, root concavities, developmental grooves, cervical enamel projections, enamel pearls, root grooves, calculus — increase plaque retention at a site and challenge the clinician. Not clear risk factors for periodontitis, but may drive susceptibility at specific teeth Bleiden et al. 1999.

7. Risk determinants: age, gender, race, SES

Age

  • Prevalence Brown et al. 1990; Eke et al. 2012 and severity Grossi et al. 1994; Eke et al. 2012 of periodontitis increase with age, shown in longitudinal studies.
  • Likely due to prolonged exposure to risk factors and cumulative tissue destruction over a lifetime, rather than an age-related intrinsic deficiency affecting susceptibility.

Gender

  • Males > females for risk of attachment loss Beck et al. 1990; Oliver et al. 1991.
  • Females > males for risk of tooth loss Gilbert et al. 2002.
  • There appears to be no inherent difference in susceptibility; disparities likely reflect lifestyle differences — smoking, attendance at health practitioners, attitudes towards oral hygiene.

Race

  • Oliver et al. 1991 — greater prevalence in Black populations than White across all age groups.
  • Beck et al. 1990 — greater extent and severity, but only when analysed with socio-economic factors.
  • Eke et al. 2012 — differences in prevalence across ethnic groups.
  • Certain groups may be more susceptible, but SES and education may contribute to and modulate the results — the two are confounded.

Socio-economic status

  • Lower education — individuals with less than 12 years of education were more than 2× at risk of periodontal pocketing (26% vs 11%) Oliver et al. 1991; Brown et al. 1994; Eke et al. 2012.
  • Poorer financial situation — increased risk of attachment loss for having 4+ sites with loss of attachment Beck et al. 1990.

Confounding matters here

  • Grossi et al. 1994 — when adjusted for oral hygiene and smoking status, the association between SES and more severe periodontal disease was not seen.
  • Oliver et al. 1991 — level of income made little difference to prevalence or extent of attachment loss.

8. Risk determinant: genetics

  • Genetic variance is a major determinant of the differential risk of periodontitis Kinane et al. 2005.
  • Genes play a role in predisposition to and progression of periodontal disease, by impairing (a) inflammatory and/or (b) immune responses.
  • To date, no major disease gene mutation has been identified which results in periodontitis in otherwise systemically healthy individuals.

Not everyone is equally susceptible

  • Not all individuals will develop periodontitis Löe et al. 1986.
  • Only a subset of the population will develop severe disease Lindhe et al. 1989.
  • Systemic disease (e.g. diabetes) and environmental factors (smoking, oral hygiene) are plausible modifiers of susceptibility and progression — but the effectiveness of the individual's immune response also determines the extent of destruction, so the genetic profile plays a role in susceptibility.

Twin study — Michalowicz et al. 2000

  • Statistically significant genetic variance for both severity and extent of disease.
  • Periodontitis estimated to have approximately 50% heritability, unaltered after adjusting for behavioural variables including smoking.
  • No evidence for heritability of gingivitis after adjusting for environmental factors (smoking, OH).

The IL-1 genotype has been proposed as conferring increased risk of progression and severity of chronic periodontitis.

9. Risk determinant: stress

The association of psychological stress with periodontal disease was first postulated in early studies of acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis. Psychological stress, distress, inadequate coping mechanisms and self-neglect have been linked to greater prevalence and severity of periodontitis.

  • Inadequate coping OR 1.7 and financial strain OR 2.24 associated with greater attachment loss after adjusting for age, gender and cigarette smoking — adequate coping may decrease risk Genco et al. 1999.
  • Stress may induce neglect of oral hygiene and increased plaque accumulation Deinzer et al. 2001.

Three biological pathways

Pathway 1
Autonomic nervous system
  • Mediated by adrenergic sympathetic stimulation
  • Release of adrenaline and noradrenaline → decreased blood flow
  • Alters immune responses: differentiation and maturation of T cells, growth of cytotoxic T cells, antibody production by B cells/plasma cells
Pathway 2
HPA axis
  • Stress → hypothalamus releases CRF and AVP → ACTH into circulation → glucocorticoids
  • GCs initially inhibit immune cells (macrophages, T-cell development); prolonged exposure stimulates them
  • CRF stimulates cytokine production (IL-1 by monocytes, IL-2) and T-cell proliferation
  • ACTH inhibits IFN-γ production and receptor expression, blocking macrophage activation; promotes B-cell proliferation but inhibits antibody production
Pathway 3
Neuropeptides
  • Nerve fibres release neuropeptides that modulate the immune system
  • Substance P is released from sensory C-type nerve fibres found in the periodontium
  • SP causes vasodilatation, increases blood flow and stimulates immune cells
  • Beneficial short-term — but long-lasting emotional stress excessively increases SP release, producing imbalanced inflammatory reactions promoting tissue damage

10. Risk indicators

Infrequent dental visits

  • Non-compliant, irregularly maintained patients tend to experience greater attachment loss, deeper probing depths and more tooth loss.
  • Only a minority comply with the prescribed regimen — approximately 20% dropout rate in maintenance.
  • Recall frequency affects compliance: 39% compliant when recommended a 5–7-monthly recall, compared with 9% when recommended 2-monthly recalls Wilson et al. 1984.
  • Factors affecting compliance Tan et al. 1992: fear, economic factors, indifferent behaviour of the clinician, longer appointment time, change in socio-economic factors, stress, family influence.
  • Difficult to exclude the contribution of confounding variables in longitudinal studies.

Obesity

  • Defined as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that presents a risk to health Linden et al. 2013; obese if BMI ≥ 30 kg/m².
  • A modest positive association between obesity and prevalent periodontitis has been demonstrated — OR 1.35–1.81 Chaffee & Weston 2010; Suvan et al. 2011.
  • Suggested pro-inflammatory link.

Alcohol

  • Alcohol consumption may be associated, in a dose-dependent manner, with increased severity of clinical attachment loss.
  • OR 1.22 at 5 standard drinks per week, increasing to OR 1.67 at 20 standard drinks per week Genco & Borgnakke 2013.
  • Further studies are required to understand the association more fully.

11. Risk markers

Previous history of periodontitis

A previous history of periodontitis may increase the risk of future attachment loss — but the evidence conflicts:

Against

In a longitudinal study of disease progression among untreated patients, previous attachment loss was a poor predictor of future attachment loss progression Lindhe et al. 1983.

For

Past history of disease was a prognostic factor for further breakdown in untreated subjects with moderate to advanced periodontitis Machtei et al. 1997b.

Bleeding on probing

BOP is only moderately associated with future CAL loss.

StudyDesignFinding
Lang et al. 1986 4 years; advanced periodontitis patients maintained 3–5-monthly Pockets bleeding at all 4 recalls had a 30% chance of losing attachment. A limited but useful prognostic indicator during maintenance.
Lang et al. 1990 1.5 years; periodontitis patients maintained 2–6-monthly; only bleeding sites debrided BOP had only a 6% positive predictive value for disease progression, but absence of BOP had a 98% negative predictive value for periodontal stability.

The interpretation that gets marks

BOP is a poor positive predictor but an excellent negative predictor. Bleeding does not tell you a site will break down; the absence of bleeding tells you, with 98% confidence, that it will not. This is why "no BOP" is the clinical endpoint we aim for.

12. High-yield exam summary

Conclusion points from the lecture

  • Risk assessment involves identifying elements that may predispose a patient to developing periodontitis, or that may influence the progression of existing disease.
  • Patients may require modification of their prognosis and treatment plan in accordance with their risk status.
  • Beyond evaluating risk, patients should be educated regarding their risk, and suitable intervention strategies implemented where appropriate.
