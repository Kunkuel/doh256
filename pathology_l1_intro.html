Introduction to Pathology

Pathology is the scientific study of disease. This lecture establishes the framework the whole subject runs on — aetiology, pathogenesis and morphologic change — and reviews the cell and molecular biology that underpins it.

01

What is disease?

Before studying disease, we need a precise idea of what the word means.

Definition

Disease is a state of abnormal structure and/or abnormal function affecting cells, tissues, organs, or systems of the body — a condition in which normal homeostasis is disturbed, resulting in impaired physiological, biochemical, or behavioural function.

A recognisable clinical entity

A disease is characterised by a consistent pattern of three things:

Element 1

Symptoms

Subjective — what the patient experiences and reports, e.g. pain, fatigue.

Element 2

Signs

Objective — what the clinician can observe or measure, e.g. swelling, fever.

Element 3

Pathological abnormalities

The underlying structural and functional changes in the tissue itself.

Disease is a process, described along three axes

AxisOne endOther end
Time courseAcute — rapid onset, shortChronic — slow, prolonged
ExtentLocalised — confinedSystemic — body-wide
OutcomeReversibleIrreversible
Distinguish a symptom from a sign, and give an example of each.

A symptom is subjective — the patient's own experience, which they report to you (pain, fatigue, nausea). A sign is objective — something the clinician can observe or measure (swelling, fever, a visible lesion). Both, together with the underlying pathological abnormalities, make up the recognisable pattern that defines a disease.

02

What is pathology?

The discipline, its levels, and its two roles.

Definition

Pathology is the scientific study of disease. It explains what disease is, why it happens, and how it affects the body — connecting basic science → clinical signs and symptoms → investigations → diagnosis → treatment.

Two broad roles

Role 1

Scientific discipline

Understanding the mechanisms of disease.

Role 2

Diagnostic discipline

Interpreting samples to guide patient care.

03

The three core questions

The backbone of the entire subject. Every disease is approached by asking these three, in order.

QuestionWhat it covers
1. Aetiology
(the cause)		Genetic factors (inherited or acquired mutations) · environmental factors (infection, toxins, radiation, nutrition) · lifestyle and exposures (smoking, alcohol, occupation) · immune and inflammatory triggers · multifactorial causation (gene–environment interaction).
2. Pathogenesis
(the mechanism)		Molecular and cellular pathways disrupted · the host response (inflammation, immunity, repair) · progression over time (acute → chronic, reversible → irreversible).
3. Morphologic + functional changesMorphology — gross and microscopic architecture. Function — organ performance, biomarkers, physiological consequences. Clinical correlation — symptoms, signs, complications, prognosis.
For a patient with lung cancer caused by smoking, name the aetiology, the pathogenesis and the morphologic change.

Aetiology — tobacco smoke: an environmental and lifestyle exposure, interacting with genetic susceptibility. Pathogenesis — carcinogens produce DNA damage and mutations, disordered cell-cycle control and unregulated proliferation over time. Morphologic and functional changes — a tumour mass visible grossly and microscopically, with impaired gas exchange, and clinical correlates such as cough, breathlessness and weight loss.

04

Why pathology matters

Four things clinical care cannot do without.

1 · Diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis

  • Turns symptoms and signs into a specific disease entity
  • Reduces uncertainty; avoids harmful misclassification
  • Confirms or refines clinical impressions, e.g. benign vs malignant
2 · Treatment

Evidence-based treatment

  • Therapy targets mechanisms — infection, inflammation, thrombosis, malignancy
  • Enables stratification: severity, subtype, stage, risk
  • Guides choice and intensity, including “no treatment” when appropriate
3 · Prevention

Prevention and public health

  • Identifies causes and modifiable risks
  • Enables screening and early detection
  • Provides reliable incidence data — registries, surveillance
4 · Monitoring

Monitoring and prognosis

  • Biomarkers and findings track response to treatment
  • Predicts outcomes — grade/stage, molecular features, complications
05

Pathology as a scientific discipline

Pathology is a science of disease, not a catalogue of findings.

  • Built on systematic observation
  • Uses hypothesis-driven investigation
  • Integrates laboratory findings with clinical data
  • Evolves with advances in technology and biology
  • Converts observations into testable disease models
06

Pathology links structure and function

Core idea

Disease = disturbed structure and disturbed function.

  • Normal structure underpins normal function
  • Structural change → predictable functional impairment
  • Functional change may precede visible structural change
  • Pathology explains how anatomy becomes clinical disease

The bridge between science and clinical medicine

07

The rise of microscopy

A 19th-century turning point.

  • Introduction of light microscopy into medical science
  • Enabled examination of thin tissue sections
  • Revealed disease at the cellular and subcellular level
  • Challenged earlier ideas of spontaneous generation
  • Shifted pathology from organ-level description → cellular analysis
08

Cellular pathology

Disease begins in cells.

Central principle

Cells are recognised as the smallest functional units of life. Disease is explained by cell injury, adaptation, and death.

  • “Omnis cellula e cellula” — all cells arise from pre-existing cells
  • A shift from humours and organs → cell-based mechanisms
  • The foundation of modern histopathology

The cell in health and disease

Two ideas from the lecture are worth holding onto: the same cellular components that keep a cell healthy become the sites of disease when they fail, and the products a cell secretes are altered in disease — both under- and over-production can be pathological.

09

Limits of cellular pathology

Why cells alone do not explain all disease.

  • Some diseases show minimal structural change
  • Functional disturbance may precede visible cell injury
  • Biochemical and molecular defects can be primary
This is why molecular pathology exists

It reveals what a microscope cannot see: gene mutations, abnormal proteins, and dysregulated signalling pathways.

10

Molecular pathology

Disease at the level of genes and proteins.

Definition

Molecular pathology studies disease at the level of genes and proteins. The primary lesion may be molecular before structural change is visible.

Key molecular events — the initial fault

DNA sequence change (SNV, insertion/deletion) Chromosomal abnormalities (deletions, translocations, aneuploidy) Epigenetic change (methylation, chromatin modification) Altered gene expression (up/down regulation, abnormal transcripts)

Consequences — how the fault causes harm

Molecular consequenceResult
Abnormal proteinsWrong structure, folding, function, or localisation
Enzyme defectsMetabolic block → toxic accumulation or deficiency
Receptor / signalling defectsInappropriate growth and survival signalling
Disordered cell-cycle controlNeoplasia
Clinical impact

Molecular pathology explains congenital disease and cancer biology, and enables precision diagnosis, prognosis and targeted therapy.

11

Cellular and molecular injury

Cell injury targets specific structures — and each target produces predictable outcomes.

TargetWhat goes wrongOutcomes
NucleusDNA damage, mutations, impaired repair; viral integration and replication effectsDysplasia · malignant transformation · cell death
MitochondriaReduced ATP production, oxidative stress, release of pro-apoptotic factorsEnergy failure · apoptosis/necrosis · organ dysfunction
Membrane & transportIon channel and pump dysfunction, permeability changes, receptor and signalling failureOedema · cell swelling · impaired excitability
ProteinsMisfolding and accumulation, ER stress, impaired degradation pathwaysDegenerative disease · toxic aggregates
Patterns of response

Adaptation (hypertrophy, atrophy, metaplasia) vs reversible injury vs irreversible injury (necrosis/apoptosis). These are the whole subject of Lecture 2.

12

Scope of pathology

Where pathological knowledge comes from.

Most important

Human patient material

  • Biopsies, resections, cytology
  • Blood, urine, CSF, other body fluids
  • Autopsy tissue and clinicopathological correlation
Supporting evidence

Experimental systems

  • Animal models — mechanisms, interventions, whole-organism effects
  • Cell cultures — controlled environment, pathways, drug response
  • Organoids / tissue models — intermediate complexity
  • Computational models — simulation, pattern recognition, prediction
Direction of travel

Translational pathway

Bench discovery → validation in human samples → clinical implementation.

Central principle

Pathology must remain clinically anchored — findings matter because they change patient care.

14

Pathology in precision medicine

From disease categories to individual biology.

Step 1

Molecular profiling

  • Identification of genetic and molecular abnormalities
  • Subclassification beyond morphology alone
  • Prognostic and predictive biomarkers
Step 2

Targeted therapies

  • Directed at specific molecular pathways
  • Improved efficacy vs non-specific therapy
  • Avoidance of ineffective or harmful treatments
Step 3

Individualised care

  • Treatment selection based on tumour or disease biology
  • Integration with clinical and imaging data
  • Dynamic adjustment of therapy over time
Key message

Precision medicine is impossible without modern pathology.

Genetic vs environmental causes

Genetic causesEnvironmental causes

  • Prenatally acquired mutations
  • Postnatally acquired genetic damage
  • Monogenic vs polygenic disease
  • Variable age of onset
  • Chemical agents
  • Physical agents

  • Depend on dose, duration and exposure
  • Carry potential for prevention

Evidence for the two contributions

Family aggregation

Does the disease cluster in families?

Twin concordance

Identical vs non-identical twin agreement rates.

Adoption studies

Separates shared genes from shared environment.

Migrant populations

Change in risk on moving reveals exposure effects.

A disease is common in Japan but rare in the USA. Japanese people who migrate to the USA develop American rates within a generation. Genetic or environmental?

Environmental. This is the classic migrant population study design. If the cause were genetic, migrants would carry their risk with them and retain the Japanese rate. Because risk shifts to match the new environment, the determining factor must be something in that environment — diet, exposure or lifestyle — rather than inherited genotype.

Predisposing factors and multifactorial disease

Predisposing factors and disease precursors

Genetic susceptibility Host factors (immunity, nutrition, age) Preneoplastic conditions Permissive diseases Disease progression pathways

Prenatal and early-life influences

  • Transplacental exposure to toxins and infection
  • Critical windows of development
  • Foetal nutrition and growth
  • Lifelong disease risk
  • Developmental programming — the early environment shapes later disease susceptibility

Multifactorial aetiology

Most common chronic diseases are multifactorial

They arise from multiple genetic variants plus environmental exposures, subject to threshold effects and showing variable clinical expression.

18

Integrated framework of disease causation

A unified model, from cause to clinical disease.

Disease causation drives clinical reasoning

Guides diagnosis

Identifies preventable factors

Predicts disease behaviour

Informs treatment & prognosis

What is the single key principle of the integrated framework?

That disease is the biological outcome of interacting influences, not a single event. Genetic factors, environmental exposures and host susceptibility converge to produce cellular and molecular injury, which is what finally manifests as clinical disease.
