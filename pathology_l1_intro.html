01 What is Disease?

Before studying disease, we need a precise idea of what "disease" means.

◆

Definition — DiseaseA state of abnormal structure and/or abnormal function affecting cells, tissues, organs, or systems of the body — a condition in which normal homeostasis is disturbed, resulting in impaired physiological, biochemical, or behavioural function.

A disease is a recognisable clinical entity characterised by a consistent pattern of three things:

Symptoms

Subjective

What the patient experiences and reports (e.g. pain, fatigue).

Signs

Objective

What the clinician can observe or measure (e.g. swelling, fever).

Pathological

Abnormalities

The underlying structural/functional changes in tissue.

Disease is a process — it has "axes"

Any disease can be positioned along several descriptive axes:

AxisOne endOther end
Time courseAcute (rapid onset, short)Chronic (slow, prolonged)
ExtentLocalised (confined)Systemic (body-wide)
OutcomeReversibleIrreversible

02 What is Pathology?

◆

Definition — PathologyThe scientific study of disease. It explains what disease is, why it happens, and how it affects the body — connecting basic science → clinical signs/symptoms → investigations → diagnosis → treatment.

Pathology studies disease across every level of biology

Two broad roles of pathology

Role 1

Scientific discipline

Understanding the mechanisms of disease.

Role 2

Diagnostic discipline

Interpreting samples to guide patient care.

03 The Three Core Questions

Pathology approaches every disease by asking three linked questions. This is the backbone of the whole subject.

QuestionWhat it covers
1. Aetiology
(the cause)		 Genetic factors (inherited or acquired mutations) · environmental factors (infection, toxins, radiation, nutrition) · lifestyle & exposures (smoking, alcohol, occupation) · immune/inflammatory triggers · multifactorial causation (gene–environment interaction).
2. Pathogenesis
(the mechanism)		 Which molecular & cellular pathways are disrupted · the host response (inflammation, immunity, repair) · progression over time (acute → chronic, reversible → irreversible).
3. Morphologic + functional changes Morphology: gross + microscopic architecture. Function: organ performance, biomarkers, physiological consequences. Clinical correlation: symptoms, signs, complications, prognosis.

04 Why Pathology Matters

Pathology is essential because it enables four things clinical care cannot do without.

1 · Diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis

  • Turns symptoms/signs into a specific disease entity
  • Reduces uncertainty; avoids harmful misclassification
  • Confirms/refines clinical impressions (e.g. benign vs malignant)
2 · Treatment

Evidence-based treatment

  • Therapy targets mechanisms (infection, inflammation, thrombosis, malignancy)
  • Enables stratification: severity, subtype, stage, risk
  • Guides choice & intensity (incl. "no treatment" when appropriate)
3 · Prevention

Prevention & public health

  • Identifies causes & modifiable risks
  • Enables screening & early detection
  • Provides reliable incidence data (registries, surveillance)
4 · Monitoring

Monitoring & prognosis

  • Biomarkers & findings track response to treatment
  • Predicts outcomes (grade/stage, molecular features, complications)

05 Pathology as a Scientific Discipline

Pathology is a science of disease, not just a catalogue of findings.

  • Built on systematic observation
  • Uses hypothesis-driven investigation
  • Integrates laboratory findings with clinical data
  • Evolves with advances in technology and biology
  • Converts observations into testable disease models

06 Pathology Links Structure & Function

★

Core ideaDisease = disturbed structure and disturbed function.

  • Normal structure underpins normal function
  • Structural change → predictable functional impairment
  • Functional change may precede visible structural change
  • Pathology explains how anatomy becomes clinical disease

Pathology as the bridge between science & clinical medicine

07 History — The Rise of Microscopy

Microscopy was a 19th-century turning point in pathology.

  • Introduction of light microscopy into medical science
  • Enabled examination of thin tissue sections
  • Revealed disease at the cellular and subcellular level
  • Challenged earlier ideas of spontaneous generation
  • Shifted pathology from organ-level description → cellular analysis

08 Cellular Pathology

★

Central principleDisease begins in cells — the smallest functional units of life.

  • Disease is explained by cell injury, adaptation, and death
  • "Omnis cellula e cellula" — all cells arise from pre-existing cells
  • A shift from "humours and organs" → cell-based mechanisms
  • The foundation of modern histopathology

The cell in health & disease

Two ideas from the lecture are worth holding onto: the same cellular components that keep a cell healthy become the sites of disease when they fail, and the products a cell secretes are altered in disease (both under- and over-production can be pathological).

09 Limits of Cellular Pathology

Cells alone do not explain all disease.

  • Some diseases show minimal structural change
  • Functional disturbance may precede visible cell injury
  • Biochemical and molecular defects can be the primary problem
▶

This is why molecular pathology existsIt reveals gene mutations, abnormal proteins, and dysregulated signalling pathways that a microscope cannot see.

10 Molecular Pathology

◆

DefinitionMolecular pathology studies disease at the level of genes and proteins. The primary lesion may be molecular before any structural change is visible.

Key molecular events (the initial fault)

DNA sequence change (SNV, insertion/deletion) Chromosomal abnormalities (deletions, translocations, aneuploidy) Epigenetic change (methylation, chromatin modification) Altered gene expression (up/down-regulation, abnormal transcripts)

Consequences (how the fault causes harm)

Molecular consequenceResult
Abnormal proteinsWrong structure, folding, function, or localisation
Enzyme defectsMetabolic block → toxic accumulation or deficiency
Receptor / signalling defectsInappropriate growth/survival signalling
Disordered cell-cycle controlNeoplasia (cancer)
★

Clinical impactMolecular pathology explains congenital disease and cancer biology, and enables precision diagnosis, prognosis, and targeted therapy.

11 Cellular & Molecular Injury

Cell injury targets specific structures — and each target produces predictable outcomes.

Target of injuryWhat goes wrongOutcomes
Nucleus DNA damage, mutations, impaired repair; viral integration/replication effects Dysplasia · malignant transformation · cell death
Mitochondria Reduced ATP production, oxidative stress, release of pro-apoptotic factors Energy failure · apoptosis/necrosis · organ dysfunction
Membrane & transport Ion channel/pump dysfunction, permeability changes, receptor & signalling failure Oedema · cell swelling · impaired excitability/function
Proteins Misfolding & accumulation, ER stress, impaired degradation pathways Degenerative disease · toxic aggregates
▶

Patterns of responseAdaptation (hypertrophy, atrophy, metaplasia) vs reversible injury vs irreversible injury (necrosis/apoptosis). These are the focus of Lecture 2.

12 Scope of Pathology — Where Knowledge Comes From

Most important

Human patient material

  • Biopsies, resections, cytology
  • Blood, urine, CSF, other fluids
  • Autopsy + clinicopathological correlation
Supporting evidence

Experimental systems

  • Animal models (whole-organism mechanisms)
  • Cell cultures (controlled pathways, drug response)
  • Organoids / tissue models
  • Computational models (simulation, prediction)
Direction of travel

Translational pathway

Bench discovery → validation in human samples → clinical implementation.

★

Central principlePathology must remain clinically anchored — findings matter because they change patient care.

14 Pathology in Precision Medicine

Moving from broad disease categories → individual biology.

Profiling

Molecular profiling

  • Identifies genetic & molecular abnormalities
  • Subclassifies disease beyond morphology
  • Prognostic & predictive biomarkers
Therapy

Targeted therapies

  • Directed at specific molecular pathways
  • Better efficacy than non-specific therapy
  • Avoids ineffective/harmful treatments
Care

Individualised care

  • Treatment based on tumour/disease biology
  • Integrated with clinical & imaging data
  • Dynamically adjusted over time
★

Key messagePrecision medicine is impossible without modern pathology.

16 Genetic vs Environmental Causes

Genetic causesEnvironmental causes
  • Inherited genetic abnormalities
  • Prenatally acquired mutations
  • Postnatally acquired genetic damage
  • Monogenic vs polygenic disease
  • Variable age of onset
  • Chemical agents
  • Physical agents
  • Infective agents
  • Depend on dose, duration & exposure
  • Often carry potential for prevention

Evidence for genetic vs environmental contributions

The relative role of "nature vs nurture" is teased apart using classic study designs:

Family aggregation

Does the disease cluster in families?

Twin concordance

Identical vs non-identical twin agreement rates.

Adoption studies

Separates shared genes from shared environment.

Migrant populations

Risk change on moving environment reveals exposure effects.
