DOH 256 · Presentation papers

The 20 papers, simply

One card per talk. Start with In plain words and the diagram; that is the idea. The two columns underneath give each paper’s key points, then the numbers to memorise and the exam trap. Tap Bottom lines only for a quick run-through of just the summaries, diagrams and numbers.

If you only remember five things

  1. Look at the patient, not just the toothRisk tools (CAMBRA for caries, PRA for gums) decide prevention and recall, and a high-risk patient drags down every tooth’s prognosis.
  2. Seal, don’t scoopSealed lesions stop. Remove decay selectively, leave soft dentine over the pulp in deep lesions, and repair rather than replace.
  3. Find the cause before you fillCervical notches, sensitivity and wear have causes such as acid, brushing and grinding. Fix those, monitor, and restore late and additively.
  4. Prognosis is a guess, so re-check itPredictions for compromised teeth are about a coin toss, and many “questionable” teeth last decades with maintenance.
  5. Know how strong the evidence isOnly one paper is a systematic review (West). Most are expert opinion, so critique them accordingly.
Radiology & cariology
01

Reading an OPG

Perschbacher 2012 · Cosson 2020

In plain wordsAn OPG is a quick, low-dose picture of the whole jaw, but it is blurrier than intra-oral films and things overlap. Use the same checklist every time, look at the edges as well as the teeth, and know when you need a better view.

Why it matters: missed findings on an OPG are usually at the edges (sinuses, TMJ, C-spine), not on the teeth everyone looks at first.

Perschbacher 2012How the image is made

  • The back of the image is a side view and the front is a front-to-back view, as if two side X-rays and one front X-ray were stitched together.
  • Ghost shadows are blurry, enlarged copies of objects outside the focal trough, appearing higher and on the opposite side: the other side of the jaw, the spine, earrings.
  • Many artefacts are preventable at exposure (see the table).
ArtefactFix
Dark band over upper roots (air under the tongue)Tongue flat on the palate
“Kiss-shaped” dark area over incisorsLips closed on the bite-stick
Spine shadow over front teethStand tall, neck stretched
Earring ghostsRemove jewellery

Cosson 2020What to do with what you see

  • Dose is tiny: about 0.014 mSv, less than a chest X-ray (0.02 mSv).
  • Cyst vs abscess: a cyst is well defined and often painless. An abscess hurts and always touches the tooth it came from.
  • Some lesions (OKCT, ameloblastoma) can only be named by biopsy, so refer them. OKCTs come back in up to 62%, so follow them for life.
  • Broken jaw: the angle is the commonest site (32%), and 40% have a second break at the front, so always look for it.

Exam trapThe two papers read in opposite orders, and neither has data proving its order is better. Both agree an OPG can’t show depth side to side, so use CT or CBCT for that.

02

CAMBRA: caries by risk

Young & Featherstone 2013 · Featherstone & Chaffee 2018

In plain wordsCaries is a balance between things that cause it and things that protect against it. Work out which way a patient is tipping, push it back with prevention matched to their risk, and only fill actual holes.

Why it matters: filling cavities alone doesn’t lower a patient’s bacteria. In the trial, about 70% of patients who only got fillings had new cavities within 2 years.

Young & Featherstone 2013The idea and the forms

  • Remember the acronyms:
  • WREC = White spots, Restorations in the last 3 years, Enamel lesions between teeth on X-ray, Cavities into dentine. Any one of these means high risk.
  • BAD = Bad bacteria, Absence of saliva, Destructive habits (diet, drugs, poor hygiene).
  • SAFER = Saliva & sealants, Antibacterials, Fluoride, Effective lifestyle, Risk-based recall.
  • High risk plus a dry mouth = extreme risk.
  • Only a hole through the enamel justifies drilling. Early lesions, even just into dentine on an X-ray (D1), get fluoride first.

Featherstone & Chaffee 2018Does it work?

  • RCT: fluoride plus chlorhexidine (CHX) mouthwash cut new caries by 24% in high-risk adults.
  • The risk score really predicts: 24% of low-risk, about 70% of high-risk and 88% of extreme-risk patients got new cavities.
  • Patients who actually used the products had about 20% fewer new cavities.
  • High-risk recipe: 5000 ppm fluoride toothpaste twice a day, plus 0.12% CHX rinse daily for 1 week each month.
24%fewer new caries (RCT)~70%high risk got cavities88%extreme risk5000 ppmF toothpaste

Exam trapThe 2013 paper says the evidence for antibacterials is “very limited”; the 2018 paper says high-risk patients need them. Both papers come from the tool’s own developers at one university, a point to raise in critique.

03

ICDAS & ICCMS: grading caries

Pitts & Ekstrand 2013

In plain wordsICDAS is a shared scale (0–6) for how far a lesion has gone, judged by eye on clean, dried teeth. ICCMS wraps it in a care cycle that also considers the patient’s risk.

Why it matters: everyone describing lesions the same way means fewer unnecessary fillings, and studies you can compare.

ICDASThe scale

  • Three words, three meanings: detection = is something there? assessment = what is it like, is it active? diagnosis = the clinician’s overall judgement.
  • Always ask: is this lesion active today? Record it and re-check it over time.
  • Codes 3 and 4 were swapped in 2005, so know which way round they are: 3 = enamel breakdown, 4 = dentine shadow.

ICCMSThe care cycle

  • Three rules: prevent first, drill last; remove as little as possible; let saving tooth decide the cavity and material.
  • Four-step loop: history → find lesions + check risk (CAMBRA) → decide → treat and prevent, then repeat at risk-based recalls.
  • Iceland example: “caries-free” 12-year-olds were 48% or 15%, depending only on where the line was drawn. Always say which codes you counted.
0–6ICDAS codes5 sair-dry first48% vs 15%same kids, different threshold

Exam trapICDAS looks at the lesion and CAMBRA looks at the patient; they complement each other. The paper was written by ICDAS’s own developers, with no new data.

04

ICCC: managing carious lesions

Innes 2016 (words) · Schwendicke 2016 (what to do)

In plain wordsYou can’t drill out the disease (caries), only the damaged tissue it leaves. So treat the disease with prevention, and when you must restore, take out only as much as needed: less near the pulp.

Why it matters: removing all soft dentine in deep lesions causes pulp exposures and root canals that weren’t needed.

Innes 2016Agreeing the words

  • Caries management = patient level (diet, fluoride, brushing). Lesion management = what you do to one tooth.
  • Say “selective removal”, not “partial”. “Complete removal to hard dentine” is now called overtreatment.
  • Non-drilling options: fissure sealant; Hall crown (baby molars only); opening a cavity up so it can be brushed.
  • Stepwise removal: remove to soft, seal for 6–12 months, then go back to firm. Permanent teeth only.

Schwendicke 2016The recommendations

  • Deep lesion → protect the pulp: leave soft dentine on the floor.
  • Shallow lesion → make the filling last: remove to firm dentine.
  • Only one good reason is left for removing tissue: to get a surface the filling can bond to, with enough thickness.
  • Judge by hardness. No cavity disinfectants, no routine liners, and repair rather than replace old fillings.
14recommendations7of them strong6–12 mostepwise re-entry

Exam trapStepwise removal is not used in baby teeth (use selective to soft or a Hall crown). Critique: expert opinion, half the recommendations are weak, and the meeting was industry-funded.

05

How clean must a cavity be?

Kidd 2004 · Banerjee 2017

In plain wordsNot very clean, as long as it’s well sealed. Bacteria trapped under a good seal can’t feed, so the lesion stops. The seal at the edges matters more than scraping the floor.

Why it matters: this is the evidence behind the ICCC rules in talk 04, and the classic exam answer to “why leave soft dentine?”

Kidd 2004The evidence

  • Old debate: Tomes (1859) said leave stained dentine to protect the pulp; Black (1908) said better to expose the pulp. The evidence sides with Tomes.
  • Sealants placed over decay: lesions stopped and bacteria dropped.
  • Mertz-Fairhurst: soft, infected dentine sealed under composite was still stopped after 10 years.
  • After stepwise removal, the dentine left behind came back drier, harder and darker, a sign the lesion had stopped.
  • Caries-detector dye stains more than it should, so it causes over-drilling.

Banerjee 2017The practical version

  • “Any lesion at any stage can arrest” if the plaque is regularly disturbed.
  • Why fill at all? (1) so the patient can clean it; (2) to seal the lesion and protect the pulp; (3) to restore function and looks.
  • Feel: soft = sticky, leathery = tacky.
  • Use low-stress methods: hand instruments (ART), Hall crowns, Carisolv gel. The second stepwise visit can often be skipped.
15 vs 53%exposures, baby teeth17.5 vs 40%exposures, adult teeth10 yrsealed lesions still stopped

Exam trapKidd’s evidence is mostly small, old, uncontrolled studies, and Banerjee adds no new data. Black vs Tomes is a favourite contrast question.

Grading a tooth’s prognosis

Samet & Jotkowitz 2009

In plain wordsBefore planning big work, give every tooth a grade from A (good) to X (extract). The tooth’s worst feature sets the grade, and a high-risk patient pulls every tooth down one grade.

Why it matters: building a bridge or denture on a C or D tooth is how expensive treatment fails. Grade the abutments first.

The systemSamet & Jotkowitz 2009

  • First look at the patient: health, saliva, smoking, hygiene, diet, motivation and money. Note which can change.
  • Then each tooth, on four things: gum and bone support, how much sound tooth is left, root-canal status, and position or bite.
  • D vs X: a D tooth doesn’t have to come out, but may not be worth investing in. An X tooth must go (e.g. a vertical root fracture, or nothing sound above the gum).
  • The grade is relative and can move up once risks are fixed.
80 / 50 / 30%A → B → C → D cut-offs4tooth criteria3grading steps

Exam trapThe system has never been tested: no data on whether it predicts outcomes. McGuire & Nunn (talk 07) show why that matters.

07

How reliable are prognoses? · Endo-perio

McGuire & Nunn 1996 · Abbott & Castro Salgado 2009

In plain wordsFor teeth in trouble, our prognosis is basically a guess, so re-check it after treatment. And if a tooth has an infected canal and gum disease, treat the canal first.

Why it matters: condemning a tooth too early, or root planing before the canal is clean, both cost teeth.

McGuire & Nunn 19962,484 teeth followed 5–8 years

  • Overall the prediction was right 81% of the time, but only because most teeth were healthy. For compromised teeth it was 43% and 35%.
  • Made teeth worse: root-canal problem (odds 3.87×), tooth out of line, diabetes (2.09×), smoking (1.90×).
  • Made teeth better: good hygiene (2.50×). Smoking cut the odds of improving by about 60% (0.418).
  • Deep pockets and furcations show past damage. Things you know on day 1 (smoking, crown-to-root ratio) deserve more weight.

Abbott & Castro Salgado 2009Teeth with both diseases

  • Only two types: the endo lesion and the gum pocket either do or don’t join up.
  • Tell them apart: a narrow probing defect suggests a sinus or crack; a wide one suggests gum disease. Always test pulp sensibility.
  • Not joined: finish the root canal, then treat the gums soon after.
  • Joined: follow the timeline in the diagram. Don’t make a crown until healing is proven.
81%overall accuracy43% / 35%compromised teeth3.87×endo → worse4 wk → 3 moendo-perio protocol

Exam trap“81% accurate” is misleading: healthy teeth inflate it. A risk factor causes disease; a prognostic factor predicts how it turns out. Abbott’s protocol rests on low-level evidence.

08

Who loses teeth? · Periodontal risk (PRA)

Hirschfeld & Wasserman 1978 · Lang & Tonetti 2003

In plain wordsWith regular maintenance most perio patients keep their teeth. A small high-risk group loses most of them, so find those patients and see them more often.

Why it matters: recall intervals should be set by the patient’s risk, not one size fits all, and “questionable” teeth often last decades.

Hirschfeld & Wasserman 1978600 patients followed ~22 years

  • Only 7.1% of teeth were lost to gum disease, and half the patients lost none.
  • Most at risk: upper molars. Most resistant: lower canines.
  • Tooth loss mirrored left and right, and depended more on the patient than on the surgery done.
  • Gingivitis often didn’t turn into bone loss, and disease came in bursts.

Lang & Tonetti 2003 (PRA)Six risk factors on a hexagon

  • Bleeding on probing, pockets ≥5 mm, teeth lost, bone loss for age, diabetes/genetics, smoking.
  • Overall: all low (or one moderate) = low; two or more moderate (and at most one high) = moderate; two or more high = high.
  • Bone loss ÷ age example: 30% bone loss at age 50 = 0.6 → moderate.
  • Bleeding and pockets change quickly with treatment; lost teeth and genetics don’t.
7.1%teeth lost over 22 yr1 in 6patients lost most>25%BOP = high risk≥20/daysmoking = high

Exam trapDon’t extract questionable teeth too early in a compliant patient. H&W is old and retrospective, with no controls; the PRA was built by experts and hasn’t been validated.

Non-carious cervical lesions (NCCLs)

Goodacre 2022 · Grippo 2012

In plain wordsNotches at the gumline that aren’t decay. They usually have several causes at once (brushing, acid, possibly biting stress), so find the causes, prevent, watch, and only fill when there’s a reason.

Why it matters: filling a notch without fixing the cause just means the filling fails. Grinding the bite to stop NCCLs doesn’t work.

Goodacre 2022Practical review

  • Very common, increasing with age. Most often on the outer (cheek) side of upper premolars.
  • Abfraction (stress causing the notch) is unproven in patients. A 2020 consensus says avoid the term.
  • Fill only if: sensitivity, looks, food trapping, very deep, or decay. Otherwise monitor.
  • Bonding tips: the dentine is glassy (sclerotic), so roughen it, bevel the enamel and etch for 30 s. For decay, 38% SDF can stop it (it stains black).

Grippo 2012The cause model

  • Three forces: stress + friction + biocorrosion (acid and enzymes). He prefers “biocorrosion” to “erosion”.
  • Why the tongue side is rarely affected: about 5× more protective saliva, and the tongue cleans it.
  • In patients who couldn’t brush, 47% still had NCCLs, so brushing isn’t the only cause.
0.36 mm³/yrtypical growth5×more saliva on tongue side47%non-brushers with NCCLs

Exam trapGrippo says the bite matters; Goodacre says that’s unproven. Either way, don’t adjust the occlusion. Grippo invented the word “abfraction”, so he has a stake in it.

10

Dentine hypersensitivity

Bartold 2006 · West 2015

In plain wordsShort, sharp pain from exposed dentine with open tubules. Rule out decay or a crack first, fix whatever is exposing the dentine, then use a toothpaste that plugs the tubules.

Why it matters: the potassium toothpastes many patients buy are the weakest option on the evidence.

Bartold 2006Narrative review

  • Common: usually 10–30% of people, peaking at age 20–40. Upper premolars are hit most, and gum patients most of all.
  • Causes of exposure: gum recession, toothbrush abrasion, acid erosion.
  • Treatments: plug tubules, calm the nerve (potassium), seal or cover the area. Said potassium works (US FDA approved).

West 2015Systematic review of 105 trials

  • Recommended toothpastes: arginine and stannous fluoride (strongest evidence), strontium acetate, CSPS (NovaMin).
  • Potassium: not recommended. Often no better than ordinary toothpaste, and slow (about 2 weeks).
  • In-surgery treatments (resins, varnishes, lasers) help but can’t be ranked against each other.
  • Only 24 of 105 trials had no industry involvement.
10–30%usual prevalence105RCTs reviewed2top picks: arginine, SnF₂

Exam trapBartold says potassium works; West says it doesn’t. A systematic review outranks a narrative review, so West wins.

11

Tooth wear

Mehta 2012 · Loomans 2017

In plain wordsSome wear is normal with age. When it’s too much, find the cause (often acid), prevent and monitor first. Only restore for a real problem, add material rather than cut, and never while wear is still active.

Why it matters: every restoration needs replacing one day, so starting too early (especially in young patients) begins a costly cycle: “once a restorative patient, always a restorative patient”.

Mehta 2012Assess and prevent

  • Types: attrition = tooth on tooth; erosion = acid; abrasion = an object (brush, pipe); abfraction = stress (disputed).
  • Erosion clue: drinks/diet hit the front of upper teeth; vomiting or reflux hits the back (palate side) of upper teeth.
  • Score it: BEWE (0–3 per sextant, like the BPE), TWI (0–4), ACE (I–VI for front teeth).
  • Prevent: acid drinks only at meals, through a straw; don’t brush right after acid; neutral fluoride; a splint for grinding; refer reflux or eating disorders to the GP.

Loomans 2017When and how to restore

  • Severe = how much is lost (dentine showing, ≥⅓ of the crown). Pathological = unusual for age and causing problems. A young patient can be pathological without being severe.
  • Wear comes in bursts: some reflux patients lost >100 µm in 6 months.
  • A high index score alone isn’t a reason to restore. The patient’s complaint or real progression is.
  • Add, don’t cut: composite, the Dahl technique. Crowns are a last resort. Record everything.
3% → 17%severe wear, age 20 → 70≥⅓ crown= “severe”2–3 yrre-check once stable

Exam trapMehta lists four types of wear (including abfraction); Loomans only three. Mehta monitors every 6–12 months, while Loomans re-checks every 2–3 years once wear is stable.
