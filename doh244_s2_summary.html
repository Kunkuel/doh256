1. Caries diagnosis and management WEEK 1

Disease vs lesion. Caries is a dynamic process (demineralisation > remineralisation over time); the lesion is the tissue sign. Restoring a lesion does not remove disease susceptibility.

Four core determinants

  • Susceptible host and tooth (anatomy, saliva, fluoride, host factors)
  • Cariogenic biofilm (composition, pH)
  • Fermentable carbohydrate — frequency matters more than amount
  • Time (repeated low-pH episodes without recovery)

Primary modifiers: anatomy, saliva, biofilm, fluoride, diet, OH, immune, genetic. Secondary: SES, education, lifestyle, environment, age, ethnicity, occupation.

Four diagnostic axes — record for every lesion

  1. Site/surface (smooth, proximal, cervical, root; adjacent to restoration?)
  2. Surface integrity — cavitated or non-cavitated (opaque ≠ cavity)
  3. Severity — initial / moderate / extensive
  4. Activity — active / inactive
Severity and activity are independent. An initial lesion can be active; a dark cavitated lesion can be inactive. Diet: fermentable carbohydrate drives caries; acidic food/drink drives erosive wear (sweetened acidic products do both).
SeverityDescriptionManagement
InitialEarliest visible mineral change (opaque white/brown), no obvious breakdownActive: risk control, plaque/fluoride, monitor — operative care not automatic. Inactive: monitor; aesthetic treatment only if justified
ModerateLocalised breakdown or shallow cavityDecide on cavitation, cleansability, depth, activity, progression, symptoms, aesthetic/functional need
ExtensiveFrank cavitation into dentin, substantial lossRestoration usually required — first assess pulp, restorability, remaining structure, risk
ActivityEnamel appearanceDentin / site
ActiveMatte, opaque, rough; plaque-stagnation siteSoft or leathery; progression on serial review
InactiveSmooth, shiny, hard; may be dark-stainedCleansable, stable over time
Colour alone proves nothing. Separate stain / inactive lesion / active lesion by texture, lustre, cavitation, plaque stagnation, risk context and serial change. Assess texture gently — never force a sharp explorer into a non-cavitated surface. A single radiograph gives depth, not activity.
Assessment sequence: clean & dry, inspect → risk history (sugar frequency, OH, fluoride, saliva/xerostomia, medications) → radiograph (depth; lesions not visible clinically; beneath sealants/restorations) → longitudinal comparison → integrated diagnosis with symptoms and priorities.

Restoration is not the endpoint. Reinforce brushing and interdental cleaning, reduce sugar frequency, optimise fluoride, manage dry mouth, set recall and radiographic review interval.

2. Aesthetic analysis and optics WEEKS 1 · 4 · 5

Anterior teeth cut, support speech and lips, and carry facial harmony and self-esteem. A technically sound restoration fails if the patient's expectation was never clarified. Success = aesthetics + function + biology + longevity, never one alone.

Element (six)Assess and reproduceRestorative implication
Shape / formOutline, incisal contour, facial line angles, cervical contour, width-to-length; square / triangular / oval typeLine angles control apparent width; a flat, over-contoured face reflects light broadly and looks artificial
Symmetry & proportionContralateral tooth, central–lateral relationship, midline, gingival levels, embrasure progressionUse opposite tooth as guide; aim for natural, not mathematical, symmetry

Translucency / opacityDentin opacity + chroma, enamel translucency, incisal haloToo opaque = flat; too translucent = grey / low value; one opaque mass = monochromatic block

Optics

Enamel is relatively translucent — transmits light; thickness and texture shape the final look. Dentin is relatively opaque and chromatic — absorbs, scatters, reflects; supplies internal body and colour. Restorations must recreate this interaction with opacity, translucency, shade and layer thickness.

Visual illusion by contour (cosmetic contouring — preserve enamel)

To make a tooth appear…Do this
NarrowerMove mesiofacial and distofacial line angles closer together (inward)
WiderMove line angles / developmental depressions farther apart; flatten depressions
ShorterEmphasise horizontal elements (gingival perikymata); move gingival height of contour more incisally
LongerReduce horizontal cues; emphasise vertical form

Golden proportion — apparent frontal widths central : lateral : canine ≈ 1.618 : 1 : 0.618. Visible width, not anatomical width; a reference, not a rule. The central facial highlight is bordered by line angles and depressions — finishing changes perceived size, not just anatomy.

Anterior restorative options

OptionKey points
Direct compositeConservative, additive/defect-oriented, repairable, one visit, lower cost. Technique-sensitive: isolation, remaining enamel, shade/opacity, layering, curing, finishing, occlusal design
Indirect (ceramic, lab / CAD-CAM)For extensive loss, multiple surfaces, major colour/form change, or demands not predictably met directly. More reduction, planning, cost, visits; success depends on prep, fit, substrate-specific surface treatment, cementation, occlusion. Partial-coverage can be conservative
InterdisciplinaryOrthodontics for position/alignment; periodontics for soft-tissue/gingival discrepancies
No size rule. A large defect does not automatically require an indirect restoration. Romero et al. chose direct composite for a large lateral-incisor fracture because circumferential enamel remained and no ferrule existed for an indirect option — one case report, not a comparative trial.

3. Shade selection STATED ONCE — REPEATED IN WEEKS 1, 2, 4, 5

  1. Before preparation and before rubber dam, on a clean, hydrated tooth. Dehydration raises value and opacity → restoration too light.
  2. Consistent cool artificial light or daylight; avoid yellow-biased incandescent light and strong surrounding colours. Neutral surroundings, brief comparisons, rest the eyes.
  3. Assess the restoration area, not one whole-tooth tab. Map optical zones: cervical/body dentin, facial enamel, incisal translucency, local effects.
  4. Cured composite trial: place unbonded composite on the relevant facial area (no etch, no adhesive), light-cure it — polymerisation changes colour/translucency — compare, then flick off with a probe.
  5. Layered case: choose a more opaque dentin/body shade + a more translucent enamel shade. Incisal effects only if present in the reference tooth.
  6. Dark substrate: plan masking deliberately; simply increasing opacity gives a lifeless block.
  7. Electronic device (e.g. EasyShade): calibrate, tip flat and stable on the intended area, follow device mode instructions.

Simulation vs clinic. A plastic tooth is a standardised exercise. A real tooth requires diagnosis of cause, pulp, supporting tissues, prognosis, goals and alternatives before any restorative step.

5. Class III — proximal, incisal angle intact WEEK 2

Definition. Proximal surface of an incisor or canine not involving the incisal edge/angle. Involvement of the incisal edge/angle → Class IV. Class III recreates proximal surface, contact, embrasures, lingual contour and appearance while preserving facial enamel.

Access route — decision rule

RouteWhenCaveat
Palatal / lingualOften preferred: direct conservative access, leaves facial enamel intact; lesion nearer palatal surfaceNot if it sacrifices more sound tissue or prevents controlled excavation
Facial / buccalLesion facially positioned, facial enamel undermined/defective, large lesion, or palatal access less conservativeVisible margin → careful bevel, shade, contour, polish
Strictly proximalVery small, exclusively proximal lesion after separation and visual control; preparation reproduces lesion size/shapeMajor risk of adjacent-tooth damage
Palatal access is an aesthetic preference, not a rule. Choose the route that permits complete diagnosis and controlled caries removal with the least loss of sound enamel and dentin.

Preparation principles (Class III and IV)

  • Smallest bur that gives controlled access; match instrument to lesion — do not enlarge the lesion to fit a standard form. Spoon excavator / slow round bur as appropriate.
  • Bonded composite preparations need no routine mechanical undercuts or standard internal form.
  • Remove friable infected soft dentin and unsupported enamel; clean accessible peripheral margins; round stress-concentrating irregularities.
  • Deep lesion: colour is not the excavation endpoint — dark dentin may be hard/inactive. Use hardness, depth, peripheral seal and the deep-caries protocol. Weigh optical shadowing through facial enamel against tissue loss and pulpal risk.
  • Adjacent lesions: complete in the order that preserves access, matrix control and contact — smaller-first is sometimes useful, not a universal rule.

Enamel bevel — course rule

PointDetail
LocationFacial/labial enamel margin. Course cross-section shows a facial bevel, not indiscriminate palatal removal
Angle / instrument≈45° to external surface; fine-grit flame or round diamond
WidthClass III ≈ 0.5–1.2 mm (audio guide); Class IV 0.5–2 mm (diagram ≈ 1–1.5 mm). Adjusted to missing structure and need for bond area / blending
Benefits↑ enamel bonding area, exposes prism ends, gradual composite-to-enamel colour and contour transition. A non-linear/irregular bevel is less visually obvious than a straight line (but preps more enamel — use deliberately)
Modify / omitNo bevel on cementum or dentin; not if it needlessly enlarges the prep; not where a thin composite margin would sit in heavy functional contact
Too absolute — correct in an exam: "always bevel", "remove all dark dentin", "etch 30 s enamel / 15 s dentin always". Bevel suitable enamel only; excavate by hardness/depth/protocol; etch and adhesive times are product-specific (memorise 30/15 s only as the simulation-clinic protocol).

Class III sequence (12 steps)

Diagnose & plan → LA (aids field control; never replaces isolation) → record occlusion → shade on hydrated tooth (cured trial) → rubber dam → pre-wedge / fender → most conservative access, excavate → remove unsupported enamel, bevel where indicated → clear polyester strip + wedge beyond all margins → etch & bond per system → proximal enamel shell → dentin/body → final enamel (cure each) → finish, floss, reassess occlusion after dam removal

6. Class IV — proximal plus incisal angle WEEK 3

Definition. Proximal surface of an anterior tooth including the incisal angle/edge; carious, traumatic, or lost restoration. Adds to Class III: incisal-edge form, optical stratification, fracture resistance, compatibility with anterior guidance — and must resist functional loading at the edge.

Palatal scaffold

AidRoleLimit
Silicone indexTransfers planned palatal + incisal contour from wax-up, digital replica or intact pre-op form; repeatable scaffold for first enamel shellControls only what it recorded — verify seating, orientation, incisal length, palatal clearance, no dam/wedge interference before bonding
Clear matrix + wedge + finger supportCan form the palatal wall when no index availableLess repeatable
Index workflow: obtain intended form (wax-up / printed replica / pre-op) → rigid silicone index of palatal surfaces + incisal edges of tooth and stable neighbours, trimmed to seat under dam → trial-seat before etching → after adhesive, controlled enamel increment in index at the defect, seat without distortion, remove excess, cure → remove index, inspect shell for continuity, thickness, adaptation, incisal length before internal layers

Class IV sequence

Diagnose & consent → LA → record occlusion → shade (enamel + dentin/body) → plan form (contralateral, wax-up, digital) → rubber dam → pre-wedge/shield if contact involved → defect-centred preparation, extend proximally only to access/finish/contour (just free of contact) → 45° facial bevel 0.5–2 mm → protect neighbour (matrix / PTFE), etch & bond → palatal shell → proximal wall/contact → dentin core (mamelons if present) → effects (only if needed; keep opacity off the edge) → facial enamel feathered over bevel → final cure from extra aspects → finish, polish, occlusion, review

Shared support from both: pre-op planning, rubber dam, neighbour protection, conservative enamel prep, palatal guide, layered composite, careful finishing, occlusal verification. Course prescription for exams: 45°, 0.5–2 mm facial bevel.

7. Isolation, neighbour protection, matrix and wedge WEEKS 2 · 3 · 5 · 6

AidFunction
Rubber damVisibility, moisture control (saliva, blood, crevicular fluid), soft-tissue protection/retraction, adhesive predictability. Lecture shows it as preferred; the requirement is effective, stable contamination control per course protocol — "mandatory in every case" is too absolute. Supplement with floss ligature, retraction, haemostasis as margin demands
Pre-wedgeSlight separation before preparation; protects adjacent surface; improves access; compensates for matrix thickness when re-establishing contact
Fender / shieldBarrier against rotary damage to adjacent tooth — a wedge alone does not protect the full proximal surface
Clear polyester stripProtects neighbour from etchant, adhesive, composite; forms proximal contour; transmits light. Round sharp corners; extend beyond every margin
Definitive wedgePresses matrix to cervical margin, limits overhang, stabilises strip, slight separation, supports contact. Trim without injuring papilla
Contact formationMatrix and wedge assist but do not guarantee contact — adapt the first proximal increment deliberately; verify with floss after removal
Final contourPress strip against last enamel increment to reduce finishing; avoid flattening, over-bulking, or displacing the contact

Pre-wedge vs definitive wedge: separates/protects before prep vs adapts/stabilises matrix during restoration. Matrix vs wedge vs index: contains/shapes proximal surface vs seals/stabilises cervically vs reproduces planned palatal + incisal anatomy.

8. Adhesion, polymerisation shrinkage and curing WEEKS 2 · 4 · 6

Adhesive protocol

  • Protect neighbour; keep field dry and uncontaminated at every stage.
  • Etch only where intended; rinse thoroughly if phosphoric acid. Course/simulation protocol: 30 s enamel, 15 s dentin — clinically follow current manufacturer's instructions.
  • Dry enamel appropriately; do not desiccate dentin — moisture level depends on the system.
  • Active application, evaporate solvent, air-thin to prevent pooling, cure for specified time. Layer number per product.
  • Longer, shorter or improvised timings reduce performance. Contamination → weak bond → marginal defect, sensitivity, debonding.

Shrinkage and stress

ConceptMeaning
ShrinkageVolumetric contraction as monomers polymerise. Pre-gel flow relieves stress; post-gel, flow is lost and stress rises
Shrinkage stressDevelops when bonded surfaces restrain contraction. If it exceeds bond/tooth tolerance → debonding, enamel strain, marginal gap → staining, microleakage, sensitivity, marginal fracture, recurrent caries risk
C-factorRatio of bonded to unbonded surfaces. High ratio restricts flow and raises stress — Class I is more constrained than an open Class IV
Other variablesComposite volume and modulus, cavity compliance/geometry, adhesive performance, curing conditions, increment size, placement technique
"Maximum two bonded walls per increment" is a teaching heuristic, not the definition of C-factor or a universal rule.

Why increments

  • Better adaptation to walls and margins; smaller volume polymerised per cycle
  • Keeps each layer within validated curing depth; favourable light access
  • Builds dentin and enamel anatomy deliberately rather than carving a bulk mass
  • Reduces opposing-wall restraint where increment design allows

Lecture demonstrates conventional increments ≤ 2 mm — a course rule for the shown material; actual curing depth is product-specific and bulk-fill systems have their own validated protocols.

Light-curing — factors that determine adequate cure

Material and shade/opacity, increment thickness, light output and wavelength compatibility, clean tip, distance, angle (as perpendicular as access allows), exposure time, stability, access from additional aspects after matrix/index removal. Eye protection; never look at the light. Under-cure → voids, poor properties, wear, fracture.

9. Anatomical layering WEEKS 2 · 3 · 4 · 5

Optical logic: dentin/body composite = opacity, chroma, internal volume and form. Enamel composite = translucent outer shell and surface light behaviour. Excess body → flat/opaque. Excess enamel → grey. One thick opaque mass hides the substrate but loses depth.

Class III — three increments

  1. Proximal enamel shell against clear matrix: cervical seal, proximal wall, emergence, contact — cure
  2. Dentin/body core: opaque/chromatic; stop short of surface — cure
  3. Final enamel on the access surface (facial or palatal), feathered over bevel, shaped with strip — cure

A very small shallow prep may take one well-chosen increment — do not add layers for complexity.

Class IV — five stages after bonding

  1. Palatal enamel shell (index or supported matrix) — thin scaffold, must not consume space for body and facial layers
  2. Proximal wall / contact (strip + wedge): emergence, contact, embrasures, incisal corner
  3. Dentin/body core in controlled increments; mamelons/lobes if visible in reference tooth
  4. Optical effects only if required; keep opacity away from incisal edge
  5. Facial enamel: convexity, line angles, no over-contour; cure from extra aspects after index removal

Controlled contour. Build close to intended anatomy ("pre-finishing" concept — Paolone 2021). A small allowance for finishing is fine; bulky over-building is not protective — it lengthens finishing and risks margins, contact, line angles, enamel and occlusion.

10. Finishing and polishing WEEK 4 (+5, 6)

FinishingPolishing
PurposeRemoves gross excess; corrects contour, anatomy, margins, contact, occlusion. Removes meaningful material; leaves fine scratchesProgressively finer abrasives remove finishing scratches, reduce roughness, create enamel-like gloss with minimal material removal
Why bothCorrect shape but rough → plaque, stain, gingival irritation. Glossy but wrong shape → over-contoured, high, uncleanable. Polishing cannot fix an open margin, defective bond or uncontrolled caries

Composite — three-stage sequence

  1. Gross excess: flash, overhangs, occlusal interference — disc, diamond, multi-bladed carbide finishing bur
  2. Fine finishing: macro-contour, line angles, embrasures, ridges, grooves, fossae, texture — medium → fine → extra-fine
  3. Polish / gloss: extra-fine discs < 8 µm, rubber/silicone points, SiC brushes, felt with ≈1.5–0.5 µm paste

Abrasive rule: coarse → fine without skipping the grit that removes the previous scratches; light pressure, constant movement, cooling. A superficial shine does not prove deep scratches are gone — inspect wet and dry. Colour bands (gold/silver/red/yellow/brown/green) belong to the illustrated system only — confirm each manufacturer's grit code.

Timing (lecture): finish immediately; polish at 24–48 h. Course-specific — many modern systems allow same-visit polishing. Never dismiss with harmful interference, gross overhang, sharp edge or plaque-retentive excess.

SiteInstrument and technique
Anterior facialFlexible discs for broad convexity; fine/extra-fine tapered diamond or carbide for line angles and texture. Change angle through cervical, middle, incisal thirds — don't flatten the profile
Anterior cervicalScalpel blade to shave accessible flash, directed away from tissue and enamel
Anterior proximalAbrasive strips below/around contact, coarse → fine, short controlled strokes — excessive use opens or flattens the contact
Palatal concavitySmall flame, egg or rounded bur that fits without flattening marginal ridges or creating interference
Posterior occlusalAnatomy-preserving fine burs, then points, cups or brushes that enter grooves and fossae

Protect anatomy: establish contour during placement; finishing refines, it does not create. Check line angles from frontal and incisal views (inward = narrower, flattened = broad). Recheck floss after proximal work. Preserve functional contacts and marginal ridges; remove only interferences.

Amalgam finishing and polishing

  • Indication: most well-carved and condensed modern amalgams need no routine later finishing; do it when anatomy, contour, margins or texture need refinement. Less important for high-copper (less tarnish and marginal breakdown) than low-copper.
  • Timing: no additional finishing/polishing within 24 h — crystallisation incomplete. Gross excess, unsafe overhang or interference is still corrected at placement.
  • Finishing: low-speed green carborundum or white alumina stone → explorer check → large round finishing bur, light pressure (small round bur only to define grooves without removing contacts) → hold bur/stone ≈45° to the margin so unprepared tooth guides the instrument and limits amalgam removal → smooth, scratch-free before polishing.
  • Polishing: coarse rubber point at low speed with air-water to satin finish → wash between grits → medium → fine to high lustre (brown/medium/green order is product-specific). Alternative: brush or cup with flour of pumice slurry.
  • Heat: low speed, light intermittent pressure, movement, air-water cooling; > 60 °C can cause irreparable pulpal/restoration damage. A cloudy surface after overheating = surface mercury enrichment → ↑ corrosion, ↓ strength.
  • Do not erase contacts, grooves, fossae, ridges chasing polish. A strip between two contacting amalgams can eliminate the contact. Final: explorer margins, articulating paper, floss, clean surface.

12. Managing an existing restoration or fracture WEEKS 5 · 6

First question: why does it not match / what exactly is the defect? Clean and rehydrate, clarify the concern, diagnose whether it is surface stain/gloss, value/chroma/opacity, thickness, contour, texture, margin, disease — or a combination. Do not start by replacing.

OptionSuitable whenLimits / safeguards
Monitor & maintainAcceptable, asymptomatic, cleansable, inactive, stable, structurally insignificantRecord baseline and risk; define review; act on progression, disease, symptoms or patient concern
Refinish / repolishSuperficial stain, roughness, small flash/excess, gloss or texture mismatch, correctable contour; restoration otherwise soundPreserve contact, anatomy, thickness, margins. Cannot correct internal shade/opacity error, deep caries, widespread debonding
RepairLocalised chip, void, marginal defect, wear area or colour layer; sound bonded bulk remains and is accessibleMust leave a cleansable margin and durable bonding surface; correct the original cause
ReplaceExtensive shade/contour error, recurrent caries, widespread marginal failure, fracture, poor substrate, inadequate retention or remaining restorationEnlarges intervention and sacrifices tissue — diagnose and control the cause first; must be justified
Treat tooth / tissuesPulpal/periapical disease, significant tooth fracture, periodontal disease, trauma, unrestorable lossNever treat a restoration defect in isolation — urgent, VPT, endodontic, periodontal, surgical care follows diagnosis

Repair technique and silane

  • Confirm the remainder is worth keeping and the defect is localised and accessible.
  • Isolate, clean, remove weak/contaminated material, mechanically roughen/prepare a clean retentive finishable interface without unnecessary tooth removal.
  • Identify every exposed substrate — enamel, dentin, aged resin matrix, exposed filler, ceramic — surface treatment is substrate-specific.
  • Silane is not a universal stand-alone or mandatory step. Indicated when silica-containing filler or silica-based ceramic is exposed; follow the repair system. Silane couples to inorganic surfaces; adhesive bonds across the prepared interface — sequence depends on substrates and product.
  • Add composite, cure, finish, polish; restore shade, contact, emergence, thickness, occlusion; then address the risk that caused the defect.
  • Patient must understand longevity, cost and invasiveness of repair vs replacement.

Fractured incisor with fragment

Trauma assessment (history, soft tissue, mobility/displacement, percussion/palpation, sensibility baseline, occlusion, extent, radiographs; rule out root/alveolar injury) → fragment: keep hydrated, clean gently, check completeness, trial-fit without forcing → pulpal diagnosis: enamel–dentin fracture vs exposure; maturity; pulpal/periapical status — exposure ≠ automatic RCT; VPT may suit selected vital teeth → reattach if isolation, adaptation and bonding achievable; otherwise direct composite or other option → isolate, bond, stabilise, remove excess, cure, refine planned external reinforcement → verify incisal position, palatal contour, contacts, tissues; sensibility and radiographic follow-up — pulpal complications appear late

Reattachment reproduces original colour, texture, incisal anatomy and wear while conserving tissue; prognosis depends on injury severity, hydration, fit, substrate, adhesive control and loading.

Proximal space, dark colour, suspected caries

Diagnose (activity, extent, contact/papilla, restorability, pulp, periodontium, risk, occlusion; radiograph if indicated — do not assume it is only aesthetic) → control disease and inflammation; plan isolable, finishable, cleanable margins → direct composite with matrix/wedge for contact, emergence, cervical adaptation; alternatives only when indicated → verify margins, floss, colour, surface, occlusion; post-op radiograph for deep/hard-to-see proximal restorations — not automatic, cannot detect every gap

13. How anterior restorations fail WEEK 6

Definitions

TermMeaning
FailureUnacceptable biological, mechanical, functional or aesthetic outcome that requires intervention or stops the restoration serving its purpose — under stated criteria. Old or imperfect ≠ failed
SuccessMeets all stated clinical criteria without unacceptable defect or intervention
SurvivalRemains in service — may include a repaired restoration, depending on the study
DefectA finding; size, activity, symptoms, progression, cleansability and structural effect decide monitor / refinish / repair / replace
Failure eventStudy endpoint (repair, replacement, fracture, loss) — definitions differ; identify the criterion before comparing survival figures. The intervention threshold is itself part of the measured outcome
AFRAnnualised failure estimate within a study: AFR ≈ 1 − S1/y. Depends on follow-up, event definition, case mix, tooth type, size, operator, statistics. 5% AFR ≠ every patient has 5% risk per year, and ≠ lifetime prognosis

Four failure domains

Biological (secondary caries, pulpal/periapical disease, plaque-retentive margin, gingival inflammation) · Mechanical (composite/tooth fracture, debonding, marginal breakdown, wear) · Functional (high contact, excursive interference, altered guidance, open contact, food trapping) · Aesthetic (mismatch, discolouration, marginal stain, opacity error, visible line, poor shape/texture/gloss — an intact restoration can still be a valid failure in the anterior region).

Colour mismatch / discolourationShade after dehydration, wrong material/thickness, substrate show-through, roughness, diet, smoking, ageing of tooth or compositeSuperficial, internal, in the tooth, or form rather than colour?
Loss of anatomyWear, bruxism, nail biting, erosion, wrong material, insufficient thickness, over-finishingActive? Local or generalised? Alters guidance or space?
Secondary cariesCaries risk, plaque stagnation, defective contour/margin, diet, fluoride, dry mouth, timeActive and cavitated? Depth? Repairable after disease control?
Diagnostic cautions. Marginal stain is not a diagnosis — clean, dry, check continuity, texture, activity, symptoms, imaging only when indicated. Sensitivity is not proof of a failed bond — consider pulpal disease, exposed dentin, crack, trauma, occlusal overload, periodontal source, adjacent teeth. Complete debonding ≠ one defective product. Colour mismatch ≠ wrong tab alone.

Why failures happen — six factor levels

Separate background risk from the immediate mechanism (bruxism = patient risk; thin incisal extension = design; high protrusive contact = local loading).

Typical chains: contamination → weak bond → marginal defect/debonding · unsupported enamel → flex → fracture · thick increment / bad light position → under-cure → wear/fracture · missed excursive contact → incisal overload → chip/wear/debond · rough or over-contoured surface → plaque, stain, gingivitis → reintervention · automatic replacement of a small defect → tissue loss → larger restorative cycle.

Structured assessment of a suspected failure

History (onset, progression, trauma, pain triggers, previous repairs, habits, dry mouth, expectations) → clean and view wet/dry, magnification, facial/incisal/profile, compare with photographs → restoration and tooth (fracture, margin, contact, contour, wear, colour, cracks, unsupported enamel) → biology (caries activity, periodontium, pulp/periapical, sensibility, percussion) → function (centric, protrusive, lateral, guidance, wear facets, parafunction) → imaging for a defined question only → judge severity, activity, progression, cause, recurrence risk → least invasive suitable management + review

Patient communication

Composite is repairable and reliable, not permanent. Practical, risk-matched advice (no opening packaging with restored incisors, manage nail biting) without blame or unrealistic bans. When the problem is mainly aesthetic, replacing an intact restoration improves looks but removes tissue and enlarges the future intervention.
