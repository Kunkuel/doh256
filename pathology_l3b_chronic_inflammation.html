Chronic Inflammation

When the body cannot remove the injurious agent, inflammation cannot switch off. This lecture covers the immune system that drives the chronic response, the forms it takes, and its two most important expressions in the mouth.

02

Immunity and inflammation

Chronic inflammation cannot be understood without the immune system, because the two share their cells.

Definition

Immunity — a state of having sufficient biological defences to avoid infection, disease, or other unwanted biological invasion.

Type 1

Specific

Memory; humoral response to antigens. Targeted at a particular threat that has been encountered before.

Type 2

Non-specific

Inflammation — the response to an injurious stimulus, mediated by cytokines. No memory required.

There is considerable overlap between them. Critically, an immune response can trigger an inflammatory response — which is particularly the case in some chronic inflammatory diseases.

Why memory changes everything

No memory in place

First exposure

An inhaled bacterium that has never been inhaled before will likely cause tissue damage in the lungs. Both an immune response and an inflammatory response are activated.

Memory in place

Repeat exposure

An inhaled bacterium that has been previously inhaled may be quickly destroyed by an immune response without inducing much of an inflammatory response — so there is little tissue damage.

High yield

This pair is the entire rationale for vaccination: you install memory so that the specific response can neutralise the organism before the destructive, non-specific inflammatory response is needed.

Immunity is a double-edged sword

Level of immunityConsequenceExample
NonePrey to simple infections and tumoursHIV; immunosuppression
HyperactiveFatal immune-mediated diseasesBee stings (anaphylaxis)

Two arms of immunity

Arm 1

Cell mediated

Inflammation — neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, macrophages.
T cells and natural killer cells.

Arm 2

Humoral

B cells and antibodies.

Why does a second exposure to the same organism cause less tissue damage than the first?

Because memory is now in place. The specific immune response can recognise and destroy the organism rapidly, so the non-specific inflammatory response — which is what actually damages tissue — is barely called upon. On first exposure there is no memory, so both responses are activated and the inflammatory arm causes tissue damage.

03

Antigens and antibodies

The two halves of humoral immunity.

Antigen

Any substance that the body regards as foreign or potentially dangerous. They are usually proteins. The body can produce antibodies against them. In autoimmune disease the body can recognise autoantigens — its own molecules — as foreign.

Antibody

A special kind of blood protein synthesised in lymphoid tissue — by plasma cells, which are activated B lymphocytes — in response to the presence of an antigen. They circulate in the plasma to bind the antigen and render it harmless. Antibodies are categorised on their structure and function, and are made of immunoglobulins.

High yield

Two structural facts do most of the work: the heavy chain determines the antibody type (IgG, IgA and so on), and the Fc region binds to various cell receptors, causing opsonisation, cell lysis, and degranulation of mast cells.

Which part of the antibody determines its class, and which part actually recruits other immune cells?

The heavy chain determines the type or class (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE). The Fc region — fragment crystallisable — binds to cell receptors and thereby triggers opsonisation, cell lysis and mast cell degranulation. The Fab region, with its variable end, does the antigen binding.

04

The five immunoglobulins

One of a group of structurally related proteins that act as antibodies. Five classes with different functions.

ClassFunction
IgAMucosal surfaces — the antibody of secretions.
IgDAntigen receptors on B cells.
IgEBinds to allergens and triggers histamine release from mast cells.
IgGThe majority of antibody-based immunity against invading pathogens.
IgMThe early antibody, with high affinity for antigen, secreted before there is sufficient IgG to do the job.
Memory hook

A for mucosAl surfaces (saliva — directly relevant to oral health). D for the B cell Doorbell (receptor). E for Ellergy. G for the General workhorse — the bulk of immunity. M for iMmediate — the first one made.

Dental relevance

IgA is the dominant immunoglobulin in saliva, making it the first antibody defence at every mucosal surface in the mouth.

A blood test shows raised IgM but low IgG against an organism. Is this a recent or a long-standing infection?

Recent. IgM is the early antibody, secreted with high affinity for antigen before there is sufficient IgG to take over. A predominance of IgM with little IgG therefore indicates a current or very recent first exposure, rather than established or past immunity.

05

MHC — the signpost system

The Major Histocompatibility Complex is how a cell tells the immune system what is inside it.

  • A protein membrane complex that all self cells display
  • There are three types — class I, II and III
  • MHC proteins act as “signposts” that display fragmented pieces of an antigen on the host cell’s surface
  • The fragments may be self or non-self
  • If non-self, the host cell may acquire this antigen by engulfment (if a neutrophil or macrophage), or — if the host cell is infected by a bacterium, virus, or cancer — it may display the antigens on its surface with a Class I MHC molecule
  • Viruses and cancerous cells have a tendency to display unusual, non-self antigens on their surface. These non-self antigens initiate the specific immunity of the host’s body
The pairing you must know

CD4+ binds MHC class II (on antigen presenting cells). CD8+ binds MHC class I (on any infected body cell). Remember it by multiplication: 4 × 2 = 8, and 8 × 1 = 8.

Why must a cytotoxic T cell recognise MHC class I rather than class II?

Because class I is displayed by any infected body cell — a cell harbouring a virus or turning cancerous displays its abnormal antigens on class I. The cytotoxic (CD8+) cell's job is to kill such cells, so it must read the molecule that ordinary cells carry. Class II is restricted to antigen presenting cells and is read by CD4+ helper cells, whose job is to direct rather than kill.

06

T lymphocytes

Cellular immunity is mediated by T lymphocytes. Two subtypes, two very different jobs.

General properties

  • Each T cell is genetically programmed to recognise a specific cell-bound antigen, by means of an antigen-specific T cell receptor
  • Can only be activated by a surface-presented antigen — not by soluble ones
  • Two types, defined by their receptor: CD4+ and CD8+

Helper T cells (CD4+)

The master regulator

Helper T cells are the master regulator of the immune system. When activated they secrete cytokines, influencing the function of all other cells of the immune system, including other T cells, B cells, macrophages and natural killer cells.

  • HIV selectively destroys these cells
  • The symptoms of HIV show their importance — losing the regulator collapses the whole system
  • When defective, they cannot recognise antigens
  • Binds MHC class II on antigen presenting cells

Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+)

  • Primary function is acting as a cytotoxic cell; can also secrete cytokines
  • Release the cytotoxins perforin and granulysin
  • These form pores in the target cell’s plasma membrane, causing ions and water to flow into the target cell, which makes the cell expand and eventually lyse
  • Can also induce apoptosis
  • Binds MHC class I
Link to Lecture 2

Note the two killing routes. Pore formation causes ions and water to rush in until the cell lyses — this is the swelling-then-rupture pattern of necrosis. Alternatively the cell can be told to undergo apoptosis, which is tidy and provokes no inflammation. The same cell can trigger either death pathway from Lecture 2.

Why is HIV so devastating when it destroys only one cell type?

Because the cell it destroys is the CD4+ helper T cell — the master regulator of the immune system. Helper T cells secrete cytokines that influence the function of essentially every other immune cell: other T cells, B cells, macrophages and natural killer cells. Removing the regulator disables coordination across the entire system, which is why the symptoms of HIV demonstrate their importance so starkly.

07

Natural killer cells

A lymphocyte that kills without needing to be taught first.

  • A type of T lymphocyte
  • Has no T cell receptors and no surface cell immunoglobulins
  • Also called large granular lymphocytes
  • Able to lyse a variety of tumour cells, virally infected cells and some normal cells — without previous sensitisation
  • Has an Fc receptor for IgG, so it can lyse cells coated with IgG antibodies
  • Class I MHC molecules protect normal cells from being killed
The elegance of the system

Section 5 explained that virally infected and cancerous cells display abnormal antigens on MHC class I. Some such cells evade cytotoxic T cells by losing MHC class I entirely. That is exactly the loophole NK cells close: because class I protects normal cells, a cell that has shed its class I becomes an NK target. Losing your identity badge is itself the trigger.

Why does 'no previous sensitisation' make NK cells different from cytotoxic T cells?

A cytotoxic T cell is genetically programmed to recognise one specific antigen and must encounter it presented on MHC before it can act. An NK cell has no T cell receptor at all and can lyse tumour cells, virally infected cells and some normal cells immediately, without having met them before. It is therefore part of the rapid, non-specific defence rather than the specific, memory-based one.

08

B lymphocytes and plasma cells

The humoral arm: B cells become antibody factories.

Distribution

  • 10–20% circulate
  • The remainder reside in bone marrow, lymph nodes, spleen and tonsils

Activation

  1. The receptor
    On the surface of B cells there is an antigen receptor complex (IgM). Each B cell receptor has a unique antigen specificity.
  2. Binding
    B cells can be activated by soluble antigens — unlike T cells, which require surface-presented antigen. When an antigen binds the receptor, the B cell becomes activated.
  3. Transformation
    The activated B cell turns into a PLASMA CELL.
  4. Antibody production
    Plasma cells secrete antibodies, which are the mediators of humoral immunity.
  5. Amplification
    Activation also induces cell division, increasing the numbers of that specific B cell.
Key contrast

B cells can be activated by soluble antigens; T cells cannot. T cells can only be activated by a surface-presented antigen. This single difference explains why the humoral arm handles free-floating toxins and extracellular organisms, while the cellular arm handles what is hiding inside cells.

Why this matters in 3B

Plasma cells are one of the three defining cells of chronic inflammation. Seeing them in a section tells you that a humoral immune response has been running long enough for B cells to have been activated and transformed.

The single most important cell in chronic inflammation — and the most dangerous.

Why it is critically important

Function 2

Double-edged

The macrophage is a powerful defensive cell — but it can produce considerable damage if inappropriately activated. Read the table again: the same cell that kills microbes also dissolves matrix, recruits more inflammatory cells, and drives fibrosis. That is the engine of chronic tissue destruction.

Chronic inflammation is described as causing tissue destruction AND attempts at healing simultaneously. Which cell explains both, and how?

The macrophage. Its secreted products do both at once: reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide are toxic to cells and proteases are toxic to the extracellular matrix (destruction), while growth factors simultaneously stimulate fibrosis and new vessel formation (healing). Cytokines recruit still more cells, sustaining the process.

10

Chronic inflammation defined

The same goal as acute inflammation, but the body cannot achieve it.

Definition

Chronic inflammation is a prolonged inflammatory response lasting weeks or months, in which tissue damage continues while the body attempts to heal the injured part.

Why it happens: persistent irritation

  1. The body cannot remove or destroy the agent
    The stop condition from Lecture 3A is never met, so the mediators keep being produced.
  2. Features of the stimulus
    Some agents are inherently resistant — non-digestible, walled in, or able to survive inside macrophages.
  3. Immune system reactions
    The immune response itself may sustain the inflammation, as in autoimmune disease.

Two routes in

Route 1

Following acute inflammation

May be the result of acute inflammation that failed to resolve.

Route 2

De novo

May arise de novo — with no acute phase at all.

The defining paradox

“Tissue damage continues while the body attempts to heal.” In acute inflammation, damage and healing are sequential. In chronic inflammation they run simultaneously and indefinitely — which is why the histology shows destruction, fibrosis and new vessels all in the same field.

11

The cells — and the self-sustaining loop

Chronic inflammation is a cellular process. Three cells, plus fibroblasts.

Lymphocytes Plasma cells Macrophages Fibroblasts

It may also have an acute exacerbation superimposed on the chronic picture.

Lymphocytes in chronic inflammation

  • Emigrate using the same mechanisms described in acute inflammation (margination, adhesion, diapedesis, chemotaxis)
  • Have a reciprocal relationship to macrophages
  • Can be activated by contact with antigens
  • Activated lymphocytes produce lymphokines, which stimulate monocytes and macrophages
  • Cytokines from macrophages (monokines) in turn activate lymphocytes
  • This loop results in the persistence of chronic inflammation

Macrophages and monocytes

Key player in chronic inflammation. Two populations exist:

PopulationDetail
Tissue residentsDiffusely spread through connective tissue, and clustered in certain locations: liver (Kupffer cells), spleen, lymph nodes (sinus histiocytes), lungs (alveolar macrophages).
Mobile poolMonocytes in the circulation.
  • Blood pool half-life: 1 day (monocyte). Tissue life span: several months (macrophage)
  • Movement to tissue is governed by the same emigration process discussed in acute inflammation
  • Accumulate approximately 48 hours after the stimulus — which is exactly why acute inflammation lasts a maximum of about 2 days before the picture changes
  • Monocytes transform in tissues into macrophages; normal function is phagocytosis
  • Can be activated by cytokines, bacterial endotoxins, or T lymphocytes
  • Activation produces: increased cell size, increased levels of lysosomal enzymes, more active metabolism, and a greater ability to phagocytose and kill ingested microbes
  • Activated macrophages also secrete a wide range of biologically active products; if unchecked these cause tissue injury and fibrosis

What decides whether macrophages accumulate

Short-lived stimulus

They disperse

Macrophages either die off or move into the lymphatics and travel to the lymph nodes.

Long-term stimulus

They accumulate

  • Continued recruitment of monocytes
  • Local proliferation of macrophages
  • Immobilisation of macrophages
Acute inflammation is said to last a maximum of about two days. What cellular event explains that limit?

Macrophages accumulate approximately 48 hours after the stimulus. Up to that point the infiltrate is neutrophil-dominant, which is the acute picture. Once macrophages arrive in number — and, if the stimulus persists, begin the self-sustaining loop with lymphocytes — the histological picture becomes mononuclear, which is by definition chronic.

Chronic inflammation arising de novo

With no acute phase at all:

  1. Sterile foreign bodies
    Suture material, implants — non-digestible, so frustrated phagocytosis continues indefinitely.
  2. Asbestos
    A physically indestructible fibre.
  3. Some micro-organisms

Dental relevance

Suture material and implants are listed explicitly. Any material left in the tissues that cannot be digested will provoke a chronic response — which is why biocompatibility of dental materials matters, and why retained foreign bodies are removed.

Give one cause of chronic inflammation for each of: persistent infection, exogenous toxin, endogenous toxin, and autoimmunity.

Persistent infection — tubercle bacilli (tuberculosis) or Treponema pallidum (syphilis). Exogenous toxin — silica particles causing silicosis. Endogenous toxin — toxic plasma lipid causing atherosclerosis. Autoimmunity — rheumatoid arthritis or lupus erythematosus, where autoantigens set up a self-perpetuating immune reaction.

Histological features

Three things a pathologist looks for in a chronic lesion.

  1. Infiltration of mononuclear cells
    Macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells. Contrast with the neutrophil infiltrate of acute inflammation.
  2. Tissue destruction
    Induced by the inflammatory cells themselves — not only by the original agent.
  3. Attempts at healing by connective tissue replacement
    Of damaged tissue, via angiogenesis and fibrosis.
High yield

Point 2 is the one students miss. In chronic inflammation, a substantial share of the damage is caused by the host’s own inflammatory cells, not by the causative agent. The macrophage products from section 9 are the mechanism.

Serous and fibrous inflammation

Two forms distinguished by how badly the vessels leak — and therefore by what leaks out.

Serous inflammation

  • Marked by the outpouring of a thin fluid, derived either from the blood serum or from secretions of the cells lining the peritoneum, pleura or pericardium
  • A skin blister from a burn or a viral infection is a good example
  • Produces a serous exudate in body cavities:
    • Pleura → pleurisy
    • Pericardium → pericarditis — often caused by viruses; leads to exudate accumulation in the space between the two layers
    • Synovium → synovitis — often due to viruses or autoimmune responses

Fibrous inflammation

  • Generally a more severe injury with greater vascular permeability
  • Molecules of fibrin leak out and a fibrinous exudate develops
  • Often characteristic of inflammation in body cavities such as the pericardium or pleura
  • Fibrinous exudates may be removed by fibrinolysis and by macrophages → resolution
  • When fibrin is not fully removed, it may stimulate the ingrowth of fibroblasts and blood vessels, which organise it and cause scarring

Fibrosis is the outcome

Fibrous tissue with dense collagen deposition. Examples:

Rheumatoid arthritis Fibrous pericarditis Ankylosis of the TMJ
Dental relevance

Ankylosis of the temporomandibular joint is a direct consequence of fibrous inflammation. Dense collagen deposition within the joint restricts movement — a fibrotic end-stage of a chronic inflammatory process.

Worked example: pericarditis

Pericarditis is useful because, depending on severity, both serous and fibrous forms are possible.

  • Inflammation of the pericardium — the ‘Glad-Wrap’-like sac surrounding the heart
  • Can be caused by infection, autoimmune disease, trauma or malignancy

Pathology

  • Serous inflammation produces a straw-coloured fluid, often in large quantities, derived from the mesothelial cells lining the pericardial cavity
  • If chronic, the process can also become fibrous, leading to adhesions and constrictive pericarditis

Treatment

  • Due to the large accumulation of fluid the heart may have trouble beating effectively
  • This requires surgical drainage of the fluid
  • If it becomes fibrosed, life expectancy is greatly decreased
Two patients have pericarditis. One recovers fully; the other develops constrictive pericarditis. What determined the difference?

Whether the fibrinous exudate was removed. If fibrin is cleared by fibrinolysis and macrophages, the outcome is resolution. If it is not fully removed, it stimulates the ingrowth of fibroblasts and blood vessels which organise it into scar — producing adhesions and constrictive pericarditis, which greatly decreases life expectancy.

15

Suppurative inflammation

Pus, abscesses, and the two kinds of tract that drain them.

  • Characterised by large accumulations of pus, consisting of neutrophils, necrotic cells and oedema fluid
  • Certain organisms produce this — e.g. Staphylococci
  • Abscesses are focal, localised collections of purulent inflammatory tissue buried in a tissue, organ or confined space
  • Follows acute inflammation → abscess formation

The structure of an abscess

Draining tracts — a distinction worth getting right

TermDefinition
FistulaA non-epithelial lined tract.
SinusAn epithelial lined channel joining the abscess cavity to an epithelial surface. Allows drainage of the abscess — and therefore no pain.
Dental relevance

A patient with a chronically draining sinus from a periapical abscess is often pain-free, because the sinus continuously vents the pressure. The absence of pain does not mean the infection has resolved — it means it has found an escape route. This is why a symptomless sinus tract still requires treatment.

Why is an abscess difficult to treat with antibiotics alone?

Because defence cells are unable to reach the abscess — it is walled off by granulation tissue, with a necrotic centre that has no effective blood supply. Neither immune cells nor drugs delivered in the blood penetrate reliably. As Lecture 3A noted, it is the release of pus that allows organisation and healing to proceed, which is why drainage is the definitive treatment.

Ulcerative inflammation

Definition

An ulcer is a localised loss of epithelium, with exposure of the underlying connective tissue.

Causes

  1. Continuous physical or chemical injury
    Persistent trauma that prevents the epithelium re-forming.
  2. Secondary infection
    Colonisation of the exposed surface.
  3. Some specific bacteria
    Organisms that produce ulceration directly.
  4. Immune reactions
    Ulcerative colitis; skin diseases.
  5. Attempts at repair
    The healing process itself contributes to the appearance.

A common site outside the mouth is the oesophagus.

Dental relevance

Oral ulceration is one of the most common presentations in oral medicine, and this list is the framework for working through the differential. Section 18 expands it into a full classification of acquired causes.

17

Granulomatous inflammation

The most distinctive form of chronic inflammation — and the one with the most specific vocabulary.

Definition

Characterised by granuloma formation — a grain-like lump in the tissues: an aggregate of chronic inflammatory cells with tissue damage and destruction.

Do not confuse

Granuloma is not granulation tissue. Granulation tissue is the vascular scaffolding of healing (Lecture 3A). A granuloma is an aggregate of chronic inflammatory cells associated with tissue damage and destruction. The words are similar; the meanings are opposite.

What a granuloma actually is

  • A focal area of granulomatous inflammation
  • Consists of a microscopic aggregation of macrophages that are transformed into epithelium-like cells
  • Surrounded by a collar of mononuclear leukocytes — principally lymphocytes and some plasma cells

Macrophage differentiation

Form 1

Epithelioid cells

  • Resemble epithelial cells
  • Flat, elongated
  • Oval nucleus
  • Eosinophilic cytoplasm
  • Unable to phagocytose
  • Release inflammatory and immune mediators
Form 2

Giant cells

  • Very large
  • Multinucleate: >50 nuclei
  • Form where macrophages have difficulty destroying particles
  • Arise by fusion of macrophages — probably accidental

Two named giant cells

TypeDistinguishing feature
Langhans’ giant cellNuclei arranged in a horse-shoe shaped cluster
Foreign body giant cellNuclei scattered throughout the cell
Memory hook

Langhans’ → nuclei lined up in a horseshoe. Foreign body → nuclei flung about at random. Note that epithelioid cells lose the ability to phagocytose — they have given up eating in order to become secretory.

Formation of a granuloma

  1. Necrosis occurs (in some types)
    For example caseous necrosis in TB.
  2. The necrosis is walled in
    Surrounded by epithelioid cells, macrophages, and some giant cells.
  3. An outer zone forms
    Outside this, a region of lymphocytes and macrophages.
  4. The reaction is maintained
    Continued release of immune reaction modulators and toxins keeps the process going.
  5. Granulomata may coalesce
    They can become so large they coalesce, leading to considerable tissue destruction.

Diseases with granulomata

CategoryExamples
InfectiousMycobacterial — tuberculosis, leprosy · Mycotic (fungal) · Treponemal — syphilis · Parasitic
ChemicalTalc; asbestosis
AutoimmuneRheumatoid nodule; Crohn’s disease
Unknown / idiopathicSarcoidosis; orofacial granulomatosis
Dental relevance

Orofacial granulomatosis appears in the idiopathic category and presents with persistent lip or facial swelling. Crohn’s disease, also granulomatous, can produce oral manifestations too — so granulomatous inflammation is directly relevant to oral medicine practice.

Distinguish a granuloma from granulation tissue, and Langhans' from foreign body giant cells.

Granulation tissue is the vascular, fibroblastic scaffolding laid down during healing and repair. A granuloma is an aggregate of chronic inflammatory cells — macrophages transformed into epithelioid cells with a collar of lymphocytes — associated with tissue damage and destruction.

Langhans’ giant cells have nuclei in a horse-shoe shaped cluster; foreign body giant cells have nuclei scattered throughout the cell.

Acquired factors: a classification

A nine-part framework for working through the cause of an oral lesion. Learn it as a checklist.

4Immune diseasesBullous pemphigoid

This is a differential diagnosis scaffold. Faced with an oral lesion of unclear cause, walk the nine categories in order rather than jumping to the most familiar diagnosis. Categories 6 and 8 — neoplasia and iatrogenic injury — are the two most consequential to miss.

A patient presents with a persistent ulcer on the lateral tongue. Walk the nine categories.

1 Physical/chemical — chronic biting or a sharp cusp? 2 Infectious — HSV or other organism? 3 Inflammatory — an ongoing inflammatory process? 4 Immune — bullous pemphigoid or similar? 5 Nutritional/metabolic — B vitamin deficiency? 6 Neoplastic — squamous cell carcinoma, critical to exclude in a persistent lateral tongue ulcer. 7 Psychological — self-harm? 8 Iatrogenic — injury from a dental procedure, or trauma to a numb lip? 9 Idiopathic — e.g. Stevens–Johnson. The point of the checklist is that it forces category 6 to be considered rather than assumed away.

Oral example: periodontal disease

Everything in Lectures 3A and 3B, applied to the single most common inflammatory disease in dentistry.

Definition

Periodontal disease is a response by the body to plaque bacteria on the teeth at or below the gum line. This induces inflammation of either the gingiva or the periodontium — gingivitis or periodontitis. It is the second most common cause of tooth loss. Genetic or environmental factors can place people at higher risk.

How it starts

  1. Plaque bacteria accumulate
    At the interface of the tooth and gingiva.
  2. Bacteria release damaging substances
    Aerobic and anaerobic bacteria release metabolic acids, extracellular enzymes and LPS (lipopolysaccharide).
  3. Tissue is damaged
    These can directly or indirectly damage the sulcular epithelium and the underlying connective tissue.
  4. Mediators are released
    The body releases mediators to activate the inflammatory response.

Gingivitis — acute inflammation in the mouth

  • Acute inflammation is triggered: vessels dilate, permeability increases and leukocytes migrate — exactly the three tissue events of Lecture 3A
  • Neutrophils provide a barrier between the plaque mass and the crevicular epithelium
  • They phagocytose some bacteria, but this is not a critical mechanism in the control of plaque microorganisms

The cardinal signs, in the gingiva

Clinical findingCardinal sign
Red, swollen gingivaRubor and Tumor
Increase in intra-crevicular temperatureCalor
Pain on probingDolor
Papilla does not deflect food debrisFunctio laesa
High yield

That table is a perfect exam answer: it maps a routine clinical examination directly onto the five cardinal signs. Note especially functio laesa — the papilla’s function is to deflect food debris, and inflammation removes it.

Periodontitis — chronic inflammation in the mouth

  • Chronic inflammation of the supporting tissues of the teeth
  • Large infiltration of macrophages and lymphocytes — the mononuclear picture of section 13
  • Bacterial and host damage to the tissues — again, the host’s own cells contribute
  • The body has difficulty removing the stimulus, as it is technically outside the body — there is no blood supply directly to the infected site
Why periodontitis is the perfect model of chronic inflammation

Return to section 10: chronic inflammation occurs when the body cannot remove or destroy the agent. Plaque sits on a non-shedding, non-vascularised surface outside the body. The immune system literally cannot reach it. The stop condition can never be met, so the inflammation cannot switch off — and the host’s own response destroys the supporting tissues. This is also why mechanical plaque removal, not immunity or antibiotics, is the definitive treatment.

Periodontal abscesses

  • If the neutrophil influx is overwhelmed, an abscess can develop
  • An abscess results from the degranulation of many neutrophils in a confined area, attacking the host tissues and causing pronounced localised tissue damage
  • Blockage of the gingival crevicular orifice can also cause an abscess, as it prevents the flow of GCF (gingival crevicular fluid) from the apex of the sulcus to the mouth
Full circle

Note that the tissue damage in a periodontal abscess comes from neutrophil degranulation — the self-harm mechanism from Lecture 3A section 14. And the second cause is purely mechanical: block the drainage and pressure builds, exactly as with the sinus in section 15.

Why does periodontitis persist when the immune system is functioning normally?

Because the stimulus — plaque — sits on the tooth surface, which is technically outside the body with no blood supply directly to the infected site. The body therefore cannot remove or destroy the agent, which is the first cause of persistent irritation listed in section 10. Inflammation only switches off when the injurious agent is removed, so the response continues indefinitely and the host’s own macrophages and lymphocytes damage the supporting tissues.

Summary and glossary

Summary of inflammation

Point 1

Temporal and cellular overlap

Acute and chronic are not separate diseases but two ends of one continuum.

Point 2

Can resolve the injury

The response exists to destroy, dilute or isolate the agent and heal the damage.

Point 3

Can cause substantial tissue damage

Especially when the agent cannot be removed and the response cannot switch off.

Glossary

Antibody
Blood protein made by plasma cells in response to an antigen; binds it to render it harmless.
Antigen
Any substance the body regards as foreign or potentially dangerous; usually a protein.
Autoantigen
A self molecule recognised as foreign, as in autoimmune disease.
CD4+
Helper T cell; binds MHC class II; master regulator; destroyed by HIV.
CD8+
Cytotoxic T cell; binds MHC class I; releases perforin and granulysin.
Cytokine
Protein released by cells that enhances the immune response via surface receptors.
Epithelioid cell
Macrophage transformed into an epithelium-like cell; cannot phagocytose; secretes mediators.
Fab
Fragment antigen binding — the variable arms of an antibody.
Fc
Fragment crystallisable — binds cell receptors; causes opsonisation, lysis, mast cell degranulation.
Fistula
A non-epithelial lined tract.
Foreign body giant cell
Giant cell with nuclei scattered throughout.
Granuloma
Aggregate of chronic inflammatory cells; macrophages transformed into epithelioid cells with a lymphocyte collar.
Kupffer cells
Resident tissue macrophages of the liver.
Langhans’ giant cell
Giant cell with nuclei in a horse-shoe shaped cluster.
Lymphokine
Cytokine from an activated lymphocyte; stimulates monocytes and macrophages.
MHC
Major Histocompatibility Complex; membrane signposts displaying antigen fragments.
Monokine
Cytokine from a macrophage; activates lymphocytes.
Natural killer cell
Large granular lymphocyte; lyses targets without previous sensitisation.
Opsonisation
Coating of a target to make it easier to phagocytose.
Perforin
Cytotoxin forming pores in a target cell membrane, causing lysis.
Plasma cell
Activated B lymphocyte that secretes antibody.
Sinus
An epithelial lined channel joining an abscess cavity to an epithelial surface.
Sinus histiocytes
Resident tissue macrophages of the lymph nodes.
Vasculitis
Inflammation of the blood vessels.
