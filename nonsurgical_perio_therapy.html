1. Goals of periodontal therapy

  • Alter or eliminate the microbial aetiology and other contributing local and systemic factors.
  • Halt the progression of disease.
  • Return the dentition to a state of health, comfort, function and aesthetics.

Frame it this way

Everything in this lecture is a means to those three ends. Non-surgical therapy attacks the aetiology (plaque and plaque-retentive factors); surgery mainly buys access and pocket reduction where non-surgical means fall short; SPT protects the result.

Two timings to memorise

4–6 weeks = oral hygiene review. 3 months = periodontal reassessment. They are different appointments with different purposes — a classic short-answer question.

3. Systemic phase

  • Identify signs/symptoms of undiagnosed systemic disease and refer for medical evaluation when required.
  • Limit or modify dental treatment based on systemic findings.
  • Prevent emergencies in the dental practice.
  • Prevent serious post-operative complications following treatment.

4. Disease control phase

Also called the initial, hygienic or cause-related therapy phase. This is where non-surgical periodontal therapy lives.

  • Patient education, motivation and oral hygiene instruction.
  • Removal of hopeless teeth — do this early.
  • Removal of supragingival and subgingival deposits.
  • Correction or replacement of poorly fitting restorations and prosthetic devices; restoration or temporisation of carious lesions.
  • Possible use of antimicrobial agents.

Oral hygiene review — 4–6 weeks

Performed following completion of initial phase therapy. Assess:

  • Patient compliance, motivation and technique of plaque control.
  • Gingival tissue contour, colour and consistency.

Healing timelines

Gingivitis resolves in ~2 weeks after plaque removal. Periodontitis — it depends (on pocket depth, thoroughness of debridement, anatomy, host factors and self-performed plaque control).

5. Definitive phase

Precondition: the patient must be compliant, motivated and diligent with self-performed oral hygiene before proceeding. Includes periodontal surgery, dental implant surgery, endodontic therapy, and definitive restorative/prosthetic treatment.

Periodontal surgery

Objective: improve the prognosis for teeth and their replacements and/or improve aesthetics.

On inflamed tissues (treatment of periodontitis)

Purpose 1
Access
  • Increases access to root surfaces for debridement
  • Removes otherwise inaccessible plaque and calculus
Purpose 2
Pocket depth reduction
  • Resective: gingivectomy, apically displaced flap, periodontal flap ± osseous resection
  • Regenerative: biologics, bone grafts, membranes
Purpose 3
Correction of defects
  • Anatomic/morphologic hard- and soft-tissue defects
  • Usually resective, to facilitate plaque control

On non-inflamed tissues, absent periodontal pockets

  • Periodontal plastic / aesthetic surgery — create or widen attached keratinised gingiva (e.g. FGG, free gingival graft), cover exposed roots from recession, recreate lost papillae (e.g. CAF + CTG, coronally advanced flap with connective tissue graft).
  • Pre-prosthetic techniques — modify periodontal and neighbouring tissues to receive prosthetic replacements: surgical crown lengthening, alveolar ridge augmentation, vestibular deepening.

6. Maintenance / supportive periodontal therapy

Objectives AAP 2003

  • Prevent or minimise disease recurrence and progression.
  • Prevent or reduce tooth loss by monitoring the dentition and any prosthetic replacements.
  • Increase the probability of detecting and treating other oral diseases in a timely manner.

Periodontal maintenance may be temporarily discontinued and treatment reinstituted if recurrent disease is detected — more likely to occur if maintenance intervals are over-extended.

Three parts of every SPT visit

Part 1
Examination & planning
  • Examination, risk assessment, review diagnoses
  • Review or formulate a new treatment plan accommodating persisting/new local and systemic risk factors and unresponsive sites
Part 2
Treatment
  • Oral hygiene reinforcement
  • Debridement, prophylaxis
  • Preventive treatments (e.g. topical fluoride)
Part 3
Recall
  • Assign the next recall interval based on risk profile

7. Rationale for non-surgical periodontal therapy

  • Periodontal diseases are associated with dental plaque Löe et al. 1965; Theilade et al. 1966 and with plaque-retentive factors such as dental calculus Waerhaug 1952, 1955.
  • The goal is to disrupt dental plaque and remove plaque-retentive factors, in combination with effective self-performed plaque control, to achieve periodontal health.
  • In deep pockets (PPD > 5 mm), a reduction in supragingival plaque may not significantly influence the subgingival plaque Cercek 1983; Kho 1985; Beltrami 1987; Loos 1988.
  • Therefore supragingival cleaning alone is insufficient; it must be combined with professional instrumentation for significant clinical improvement.

The logical chain (worth writing out in an exam)

Plaque causes disease → calculus retains plaque → in shallow sites the patient can reach it, but beyond ~5 mm the toothbrush no longer influences the subgingival flora → hence professional subgingival instrumentation is required in addition to, not instead of, self-performed plaque control.

8. Terminology Kieser 1994

Subgingival debridement, scaling and root debridement (SRD) and scaling and root planing (SRP) are today used almost interchangeably, although they have inherently different objectives:

TermPrimary objective
Debridement & SRDDisruption and removal of dental plaque
SRPRemoval of plaque, calculus and contaminated cementum and dentine

The shift in terminology from SRP to SRD reflects the evidence in the next section: removing root substance is not the therapeutic goal.

9. Two overturned concepts

Removal of "contaminated" cementum

Old view (1970s–80s)

Extensive/aggressive root planing advocated to remove contaminated cementum, because endotoxin (LPS) was thought to be firmly attached and to penetrate into cementum Daly 1982; Jones & O'Leary 1978, affecting healing Aleo 1974, 1975.

Current view

Endotoxin is loosely bound and does not penetrate cementum Smart et al. 1990, and is easily removed by ultrasonic Smart 1990, hand instruments Cheetham 1988 or even brushing Nakib 1982 — without excessive cementum removal.

Root surface smoothness

  • Smoothness was advocated on the assumption that smooth roots were cleaner and less plaque-retentive Waerhaug 1956; Lindhe et al. 1984.
  • However, plaque retention, gingival inflammation and healing are independent of root surface texture Hunter et al. 1984.
  • Correlations do exist between subgingival calculus and the presence of root surface roughness — i.e. roughness is a marker of residual deposit, not the cause of disease.

10. Treatment approaches

Instrumentation modalities

  • Conventional (gold standard): scalers, curettes, ultrasonic instrumentation, or combinations of these.
  • Lasers: Nd:YAG — effective on plaque and soft tissue, inadequate calculus removal. Er:YAG — effective on plaque and calculus.
  • Photodynamic therapy (PDT): advocated as an adjunct to SRD; uses lasers at specific wavelengths to target microorganisms treated with a photosensitiser.

Verdict

There is currently insufficient evidence to recommend laser or photodynamic therapy as either a monotherapy or adjunct over conventional treatment modalities Cobb 2017.

Scheduling protocols

ProtocolWhat it involvesEvidence
Quadrant / sextant Completed over a number of appointments depending on extent and severity of disease Badersten et al. 1984
Full-mouth debridement Single-appointment full-mouth SRD No difference vs multi-visit quadrant SRD for PPD, CAL or patient discomfort. Benefit = fewer appointments Wennström et al. 2005
Single vs repeated instrumentation One pass vs repeated passes over surfaces No difference. Adequate outcomes achievable with OH + a single visit of SRD; repeat instrumentation not always required with skill
Full-mouth disinfection Full-mouth SRD within 24 h, CHX mouthwash 2× daily, tongue scraping, CHX tonsil spray, subgingival CHX irrigation 3× within 10 min, protocol for 8 days Showed superior results, but stringent protocol and no group has replicated the findings Quirynen et al. 1995

The take-home message

Different treatment approaches exist — however, the critical factors for successful non-surgical therapy are: thoroughness of root debridement, the patient's standard of self-performed oral hygiene, and compliance with professional SPT Badersten et al. 1984a.

Guiding quotation Sanz & Teughels 2008

Clinicians should choose the debridement modality according to the best available evidence, the needs and preferences of the patient, their own skills and experience, the logistic setting of the practice, and the cost-effectiveness of the therapy rendered.

11. Factors affecting the effectiveness of SRD

Operator variability

  • Trained periodontists leave less behind than periodontists in training or inexperienced dentists, and debride for 10–15% longer Brayer 1989; Kocher 1997.
  • Yet operator variability plays a minimal role in clinical research outcomes (PPD, CAL improvements) for non-surgical therapy Badersten et al. 1985.

Anatomical factors

Tooth type, tooth surface, CEJ, line angles, narrow interdental spaces, root concavities, resorption defects, areas around residual calculus, deep pockets and furcations Jones & O'Leary 1978; Breiniger 1987; Waerhaug 1978b; Dragoo 1992.

Powered vs hand instruments

  • Similar positive clinical and microbiological outcomes with powered and hand instruments Heitz-Mayfield & Lang 2013.
  • Modified (slim-design) ultrasonics are superior to hand instruments and to unmodified (thicker) ultrasonics for treating periodontal pockets Dragoo 1992.
  • Advantages of powered instruments Dragoo 1992: reduced treatment time, improved access, lavage effect, less damage to the root surface, less patient discomfort, less operator fatigue.

12. Evaluating SRD — effects on plaque and calculus

The effects of SRD are evaluated at three levels: microscopic (quantities of subgingival plaque and calculus), microbiological (subgingival plaque composition) and clinical (probing depth, clinical attachment level, bleeding on probing).

Established fact

SRD is often unable to render tooth surfaces free of plaque and calculus.

  • Waerhaug 1978 — following SRD, 90% of teeth had varying amounts of plaque and calculus remaining on one or more surfaces.
  • Caffesse et al. 1986 — the ability to remove plaque and calculus decreases as pocket depth increases.
Probing depthCalculus-free surfaces (Caffesse 1986)
1–3 mm86%
4–6 mm43%
> 6 mm32%

But — the crucial qualifier

Not all subgingival deposits need to be removed for healing to occur Nyman et al. 1988; Mombelli et al. 1995. This is why disruption (SRD) rather than root removal (aggressive SRP) is the modern aim.

13. Effects of SRD on microbiology

SRP causes a non-specific reduction of plaque. This produces a transient shift in flora composition — reduction of disease-associated species and an increase in health-associated species Petersilka et al. 2002.

StudyDesignFinding
Breiniger et al. 1987 SEM study Despite a high frequency of residual plaque after debridement, deposits were small, thin, rarely intact colonies of cocci/rods, with no complex microbial forms
Loos et al. 1988 Non-molar proximal sites vs grade II molar furcation sites after SRD Total microbial counts at non-molar sites fell by ≥ 10× at 1 week; molar furcation sites showed significantly less reduction
Haffajee et al. 1997 Clinical–microbiological correlation Clinical improvement correlated with a modest microbiota change — mainly reduced P. gingivalis, B. forsythus, T. denticola
Darby et al. 2001 Site-level analysis post-SRD Decreased P. intermedia, B. forsythus, T. denticola, P. gingivalis; A. actinomycetemcomitans was eliminated

Why this matters clinically

Microbiological shifts are transient. This highlights the importance of supragingival plaque control after cause-related therapy to prevent recolonisation of periodontal pockets, thereby allowing periodontal tissue healing.

14. Clinical effects of SRD

Probing depth reduction

  • Probing depth = the distance from the gingival margin to the bottom of the probeable crevice/pocket (where the probe tip stops).
  • Reduction results from a combination of gain in clinical attachment level and gingival recession.

Clinical attachment level — gain or loss

  • Clinical attachment loss (CAL) — the amount of attachment loss that has occurred relative to the CEJ (CEJ to base of pocket/crevice).
  • Clinical attachment level (CAL) — the distance between the attachment level and a reference point on the tooth (usually the CEJ).
  • SRD can result in loss or gain of clinical attachment level depending on initial probing depth.

Badersten et al. 1984 — three exam-critical statements

  • There is no magnitude of initial PPD at which non-surgical therapy is ineffective.
  • The greatest change in PPD reduction and CAL gain occurs within 1–3 months post-SRP for incisors and premolars Badersten 1984 and molars Loos 1989 — although healing and maturation of the periodontium may continue over the following 9–12 months.
  • Initially deep sites (7–12 mm) show more recession, greater attachment gain and more pocket reduction, converting after 24 months to moderate or shallow pockets (4–6 mm).

Expected outcomes at non-molar sites Cobb 1996

Initial probing depthMean reduction in PD (mm)CAL change (mm)
1–3 mm0.03 (negligible change)−0.34 (loss)
4–6 mm−1.29+0.55 (gain)
≥ 7 mm−2.16+1.29 (gain)

Following SRD, most studies have observed a loss of clinical attachment at initially shallow sites and gains at initially deeper sites. This is the evidence base for not instrumenting healthy shallow sulci.

Decreased bleeding on probing

  • BOP is an indicator of inflamed periodontal tissues; SRD disrupts plaque to establish healthy tissues (absence of bleeding).
  • Significant BOP reduction occurs over 1–3 months for moderate sites (4–6 mm) Badersten 1981 and over 3–9 months for severe sites Badersten 1984, 1985.
  • Resolution is more likely in shallower sites (1–3 mm) than deep sites (> 6 mm) Loos 1989; Badersten 1985.

15. Furcations and SRD

Many long-term studies have found that molars with furcation involvement have higher mortality rates:

StudyMolars with furcation involvement lostOverall tooth mortality
Hirschfeld & Wasserman 197831%7%
McFall 198257%10%

Furcation sites also respond less favourably to debridement than non-molar sites Loos et al. 1989:

  • Loos 1989 — proportion of sites losing probing attachment: molar furcation 25% vs non-molar 7% vs molar flat surfaces 10%.
  • → Furcation sites may represent compromised healing responses due to furcation anatomy and poor accessibility to instrumentation.

Anatomy limits both therapy and self-care

Anatomical factors — the fornix, concavities and irregularities — limit the efficacy of root debridement and subsequent plaque control Bower 1979; Waerhaug 1980; Nordland 1987; Loos 1988, 1989. Even where an initial complete debridement was accomplished, these factors may prevent adequate plaque control and lead to recolonisation by a microflora similar to that before therapy Magnusson et al. 1984.

16. Re-evaluation

Re-evaluation is essential to determine the response to treatment, ongoing risk, the need for further intervention, and enrolment in SPT.

  • Oral hygiene review at 4–6 weeks.
  • Periodontal review at 3 months.

Why 3 months? Badersten 1984; Loos 1989

Because the greatest improvement in clinical parameters (PPD, CAL, BOP) for premolars and incisors Badersten 1984 and for molars Loos 1989 occurs in the first 3 months following treatment. Reassessing earlier risks judging an incomplete response; much later wastes healing time.

Three possible outcomes of reassessment

Option 1
Repeat NSPT
  • Another round of non-surgical therapy at non-responsive sites
Option 2
Surgery
  • Periodontal surgery at non-responsive sites
Option 3
SPT
  • Enrol in supportive periodontal therapy with a risk-based recall

17. High-yield exam summary

Conclusion points from the lecture

  • NSPT aims at controlling the factors responsible for periodontal inflammation — patient education in oral hygiene, SRD, elimination of plaque-retentive factors, occlusal therapy.
  • Thorough disruption of dental plaque is required for success; removal of the root surface is not necessary.
  • Conventional therapy (scalers, curettes, ultrasonics) remains the gold standard against which other modalities are compared.
  • Outcomes are influenced by operator variability, anatomic factors, and powered vs hand instruments.
  • Microscopic, microbiological and clinical changes all occur following SRD.
  • Healing is measured clinically by PPD, CAL and BOP.
  • Re-evaluation is essential — response, ongoing risk, need for further intervention, enrolment in SPT.
