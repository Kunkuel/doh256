How to use this & what the examiners reward

The OSCE is not pure recall — it tests whether you can apply knowledge to a clinical scenario and justify your decisions. For every station, structure your answer as: (1) read the scenario → (2) classify the lesion → (3) state the management principle → (4) list the steps in order with a one-line "why" for each → (5) justify the material choice with reasoning/evidence.

Examiner instructions to obey: write the question number with every answer (missing numbers lose marks); use a pen; avoid abbreviations; answer all parts of multi-part stations; you cannot return to a station once you move on; only use the materials listed at your station.

01

ICDAS detection & ICCMS classification + management

ICDAS tells you what the lesion looks like (detection/severity). ICCMS takes that, adds the radiograph and the activity status, and tells you what to do about it (risk-based prevention vs. operative care). Diagnosis ≠ treatment.

The ICDAS visual codes (0–6) and ICCMS "merged" categories

Clean the surface, dry for 5 seconds, examine with good light and a ball-ended (WHO/CPI/PSR) probe — never a sharp explorer, which can cavitate a remineralisable lesion. ICCMS groups the six codes into four management categories.

CodeWhat you seeProbe / detail
0 — soundNo or questionable change after 5 s air-dryEnamel defects, wear & stains are also recorded as sound
1 — initialFirst visual change seen only after drying (white-spot / brown)No surface breakdown, no dentine shadow
2 — initialDistinct visual change when wet (white/brown)Still no breakdown, no shadow
3 — moderateLocalised enamel breakdown (micro-cavity), no visible dentineProbe drops into a narrow break in enamel
4 — moderateUnderlying dark dentine shadow ± enamel breakdownShadow shows through intact/broken enamel
5 — extensiveDistinct cavity with visible dentine, < half the surfaceProbe confirms it extends into dentine
6 — extensiveExtensive cavity, visible dentine, > half the surfaceInvolves >50% of the surface / tooth structure

Colour ≠ severity: it signals activity. Yellow/white, matt & rough → active · brown/black, shiny & hard → arrested.

Radiographic scoring (posterior surfaces): R0 → RC6

Radiographs estimate depth of demineralisation, not cavitation. The threshold at which a surface is reliably cavitated and dentine heavily infected is radiolucency deeper than the outer third of dentine — i.e. scores 4, 5, 6 (and sometimes 3 with fast progression). Two films over time are needed to prove progression.

Combining clinical + radiographic + activity → diagnosis

The clinical category and the radiographic category are read together. Most enamel-only lesions are invisible on film, so a clean radiograph never rules out an early lesion. Then judge activity, because active lesions are far more likely to progress.

Active lesion (likely to progress)

  • Enamel: whitish/yellowish, opaque, matt, loss of lustre, rough to a gentle probe.
  • Sits in a plaque-stagnation area (fissure entrance, near gingival margin, below/above contact point).
  • Often covered by thick plaque before cleaning.
  • Extensive: dentine feels soft or leathery on gentle probing.

Inactive / arrested (less likely to progress)

  • Enamel: whitish, brownish or black; shiny, hard, smooth.
  • On smooth surfaces, sits away from the gingival margin.
  • Not necessarily plaque-covered.
  • Extensive: dentine is shiny and hard on gentle probing.

ICCMS management — permanent teeth

Two management philosophies: NOC = Non-Operative Care (control the disease — fluoride, sealants, biofilm removal, diet) and TPOC = Tooth-Preserving Operative Care (minimally invasive restoration). Surgical/operative intervention is the last resort; sound surfaces and inactive lesions get risk-based prevention.

TPOC operative principles (say these when you justify "cut")

  • Cavity shape & extent is dictated by the spread of caries and the presence of infected/affected dentine — not a pre-set outline.
  • Remove soft, infected dentine from the pulpal aspect to avoid pulp exposure; it is acceptable to leave discoloured (affected) dentine pulpally.
  • If an active extensive lesion risks vital pulp exposure → stepwise / partial (selective) excavation; avoid exposure wherever possible.
  • Caries associated with restorations/sealants (CARS) & defective sealants → seal or repair the margin rather than replace where possible.
02

The caries process, risk assessment & prevention

Caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, multifactorial, dynamic disease — phasic demineralisation and remineralisation. Whether a lesion progresses or arrests is the net balance of those two processes, which is exactly what your preventive plan is trying to tip.

The chemistry that matters

  • Acidogenic bacteria + fermentable carbohydrate → organic acids → plaque pH falls → demineralisation.
  • Critical pH for hydroxyapatite ≈ 5.5; for fluorapatite ≈ 4.5 → fluoride makes enamel ~10× more acid-resistant.
  • Plaque behaves like a gel: it impedes diffusion of ions (H⁺, OH⁻, Ca²⁺, PO₄, HCO₃) so buffers can't reach the acid at the surface. Thicker plaque = more demineralisation.
  • More frequent sugar exposures = more time in the "danger zone" = more risk (frequency > quantity).

Three kinds of dentine (know the difference)

  • Infected — soft / wet / leathery, contains bacteria → remove.
  • Affected — firm / hard / dry, often stained, bacteria-free → can be left, especially pulpally.
  • Reactionary / sclerotic / tertiary — the pulp's defensive response; tertiary dentine is harder, brown (not caries).

Caries risk assessment (ICCMS uses the CAMBRA philosophy)

Risk is assessed at the patient level then combined with the presence of active lesions to predict the likelihood of new/progressing disease. Three tiers: low / moderate / high. The presence of any single high-risk factor classifies the patient as high risk.

Patient-level risk factors

  • Head & neck radiation
  • Dry mouth / hyposalivation (disease, medications, recreational drugs)
  • Inadequate oral hygiene
  • Deficient topical fluoride exposure
  • High frequency/amount of sugary drinks & snacks
  • Symptom-driven (not routine) dental attendance
  • Low socio-economic status / access barriers
  • For children: high caries experience in mother/caregiver

The clinician's informed "hunch" is also valid evidence. Special-care flags (radiation, hyposalivation, PUFA signs) warrant additional measures.

SDF — the minimally invasive workhorse

Silver diamine fluoride arrests caries (especially in primary teeth / where operative care isn't feasible): low cost, high coverage, non-operative. It stains the lesion black. Success still depends on diet control, patient compliance and follow-up.

03

Amalgam restorations & key clinical principles

Amalgam is held in by mechanical retention — it does not bond. That single fact drives the whole cavity design: undercuts, near-90° margins, no bevels, and adequate bulk so it doesn't fracture under load.

Advantages

  • Lower cost than most alternatives
  • Durable, especially posteriorly; extensive long-term evidence
  • Less technique-sensitive (tolerant of moisture)

Disadvantages

  • Non-adhesive → needs retentive prep (undercuts)
  • Removes sound tooth to create retention/resistance form
  • Can corrode; large restorations may crack remaining enamel/dentine
  • Not aesthetic; contains mercury

Mercury & the Minamata Convention (the safety question)

Amalgam is a safe, effective, well-researched material; only capsulated amalgam complying with ISO 24234:2015 should be used, and exposure is in the nanogram range with no established link to chronic/degenerative disease. The Minamata Convention requires a phase-down (not ban). Minimise use in children, pregnant/breastfeeding women and those with kidney disease. Practise mercury hygiene; spent capsules go to amalgam waste (ADA Policy 6.9 / 6.18). Do not remove sound amalgams for non-specific health complaints without informed consent.

Cavity preparation — the four "forms"

FormWhat it means for amalgam
OutlineConfined to pits/fissures; extend only to carious/defective tissue (conservative, but include susceptible fissures).
ResistanceFlat pulpal floor, rounded internal line angles (less stress concentration), adequate cusp support, no undermined enamel; enough bulk (≥1.5–2 mm) so amalgam resists occlusal fracture.
RetentionSlight occlusal convergence of walls (~20°); retentive undercuts placed into dentine; ≥0.5 mm into dentine for strength.
ConvenienceEnough access for caries removal, instrument placement, condensation & carving.

Margins: unsupported enamel removed; butt-joint cavosurface margins (~90°) — no bevels, because amalgam has no adhesion and thin feather-edges would fracture. For Class II: proximal box with no retentive undercuts on the box, Tofflemire retainer + matrix band + wooden wedge for a tight, gap-free gingival contact.

Placement sequence (mixing & condensing)

  1. Select & activate the capsuleCorrect alloy/spill size (clinic = SDI Permite 2-spill ECT); depress the plunger to break the seal between the alloy powder and the mercury.
  2. Triturate ~6 secondsMechanical mixing combines alloy + mercury into a homogeneous, plastic mass.
  3. Check the mixShake — a single cohesive "ball" that is plastic (not dry/crumbly, not runny) is correct. A poor mix gives a poorly adapted, weak restoration.
  4. Dispense into dappen dish, load carrier promptlyAmalgam sets fast; late additions won't unite with already-set material. (Empty capsule → amalgam waste.)
  5. Condense from the deepest point, in small incrementsFirm condensation adapts amalgam into line angles & undercuts and removes voids; slightly overpack for carving.
  6. Burnish → carve → check occlusion → finish/polishRecreate anatomy, marginal ridges & tight contacts; remove high spots; polishing (usually a later visit) reduces corrosion & plaque retention.

The GI lining under amalgam (a high-value mention)

A resin-modified GI lining (Fuji Bond LC) under amalgam seals dentinal tubules and offers fluoride release, enhancing resistance of dentine/enamel to caries. The clinic sequence: cure ~4 s, use a spoon excavator to remove any GI lining off the enamel cavity walls, then cure a further ~20 s.

Examiner's caveat: only mention it if you can justify why (dentine sealing, pulpal protection, fluoride). Material choice is still guided by cavity depth, remaining tooth structure and clinical presentation. If the question doesn't raise it, don't force it in.

04

Composite resin & adhesive concepts

This is the heart of the OSCE: the examiners want to see that you understand how the restoration bonds and how you place it. Composite bonds — so the prep is conservative — but it shrinks, and managing that shrinkage stress is what separates a good restoration from a failure.

What composite is made of

Organic matrix

Resin monomers: Bis-GMA, TEGDMA, UDMA, Bis-EMA. A blend balances viscosity vs. shrinkage — more fluid monomer = more shrinkage.

Inorganic filler

Silica, barium glass, zirconia. More filler → less shrinkage & better mechanics. Smaller particles → more surface area → more resin to wet → more shrinkage.

Coupling + initiators

Silane chemically couples filler to resin. Camphorquinone photo-initiator + tertiary amine co-initiator drive the cure; pigments for shade.

Classified by filler size (macro → micro → hybrid → micro/nano-hybrid → nanofill) and viscosity: conventional/packable (more viscous, up to ~85% filler) vs. flowable (more fluid, up to ~70% filler). Indications: direct & indirect restorations, repairs, sealants, bonding.

Polymerisation shrinkage, stress & the C-factor

Curing converts monomer → polymer; the gaps between chains create volumetric shrinkage. When the composite is bonded, that shrinkage can't happen freely → it generates shrinkage stress at the bonded interface. If interfacial tension > bond strength → failure (gap, debond, microleakage, marginal stain, secondary caries, cusp deflection, post-op sensitivity).

⚠ Precision the examiners look for

Incremental layering does not "reduce polymerisation shrinkage" — the total shrinkage is a material property. What it reduces is the C-factor and therefore the polymerisation stress, improving marginal adaptation. Say "stress / C-factor," not "shrinkage." (This was flagged by the examiner on last year's model answer.)

Adhesion: the foundation

Bonding began with Buonocore (1955) — acid-etching enamel. Adhesion to tooth works by: mechanical (resin tags interlock), adsorption (chemical bond to hydroxyapatite or type-I collagen), diffusion, or a combination.

Enamel (~96% mineral)

  • Etch 37% phosphoric acid · 30 s → dissolves prism peripheries → micro-retentions.
  • A "sludge" of dissolved mineral forms — must be washed off so adhesive can flow in.
  • No primer needed (no water in enamel; can be dried completely). Bond is mostly hydrophobic (Bis-GMA + a little HEMA).
  • Bonding to enamel is stronger and more durable than to dentine → keep margins on enamel.

Dentine (mineral + collagen + water + tubules)

  • Cutting creates a smear layer (organic + inorganic debris).
  • Etch 37% · 15 s exposes a network of collagen fibrils + water.
  • Collagen is like spaghetti — over-dry it and it collapses (loss of retention; if over-dried you must cut a little further and re-etch). Leave dentine moist/shiny.
  • The hybrid layer (resin interdiffused with collagen) is the key to dentine adhesion; resin tags seal tubules and reduce post-op sensitivity.

The four systems — classify by strategy and steps, not just "generation"

Your lecturer classifies adhesives the modern way — by how they treat the smear layer (etch-and-rinse vs self-etch) and the number of bottles/steps. "Generations" are a looser, secondary label.

SystemGeneration*Bottles / stepsBest at

2-step etch-and-rinse5thEtch · (Primer+Bond combined)Simpler; enamel & dentine
2-step self-etch6thSelf-etch primer · Bond (separate)Best for dentine (mild, chemical bond)

Step-by-step techniques — every step, and why you do it

All four laid out below. The steps are in bold; the reason for each is underneath. Notice the through-line: etch-and-rinse removes the smear layer (rinse needed, dentine kept moist), while self-etch modifies it (no rinse, dentine not over-dried) — and self-etch always needs a selective enamel etch because its own acid is too weak on enamel.

① Three-step etch-and-rinse 4th gen · separate etch + primer + bond
  1. Etch — 37% phosphoric acid: enamel 30 s, then dentine for the last 15 s → rinse 30 sWhy: the acid dissolves enamel-prism peripheries (micro-porosity for resin tags) and strips the dentine smear layer to expose a collagen mesh. Rinsing washes off the dissolved-mineral "sludge" so adhesive can flow in. Dentine is etched for less time so the demineralised zone isn't deeper than the resin can infiltrate.
  2. Dry selectively — enamel fully dry (frosty), dentine left moist/shinyWhy: enamel has no water, so it can be dried hard. Dentine collagen is like wet spaghetti — over-drying collapses it and the resin can't get in (lost bond). Blot with absorbent paper, not cotton (cotton sheds fibres into the collagen).
  3. Primer — separate bottle, active application (rub in) 20 s, then gently air-dryWhy: the bifunctional primer carries a solvent (ethanol/acetone) that chases out residual water and an amphiphilic monomer that wets and envelops the collagen, building the hybrid layer. Active rubbing drives it into the tubules; air-drying evaporates the solvent.
  4. Bond — passive application 10 s, remove excess, light-cure 10–20 sWhy: the hydrophobic bonding resin co-polymerises with the primer and provides the surface the composite links to. Apply passively (no scrubbing) to avoid trapping air bubbles; curing locks the whole assembly together.

When to use / strengths: the gold standard and best for enamel; keeping primer and bond in separate bottles keeps the bond layer uniformly hydrophobic → most durable. Trade-off: most steps, most time, most technique-sensitive.

② Two-step etch-and-rinse 5th gen · etch, then primer + bond combined
  1. Etch — enamel 30 s + dentine 15 s → rinse 30 s → dry (enamel dry, dentine moist)Why: identical etching rationale to the 3-step — open enamel and expose dentine collagen, then rinse off the sludge and keep dentine moist so the collagen stays expanded.
  2. Adhesive (primer + bond in one bottle) — 1st coat active 20 s, 2nd coat active 20 sWhy: primer and bond are pre-mixed, so two scrubbed coats are used to be sure the monomers fully infiltrate the collagen and reach an adequate film thickness (one coat is often too thin).
  3. Gently air-dry → light-cure 10–20 sWhy: air-drying evaporates solvent so no bubbles are trapped, then light-curing polymerises the layer.

When to use / strengths: fewer steps than the 3-step, works on enamel and dentine. Trade-off: the combined bottle is more hydrophilic than a dedicated bond, so long-term durability is a notch below the 3-step.

③ Two-step self-etch 6th gen · self-etch primer, then separate bond
  1. Selective enamel etch — 37% acid on enamel only, 30 s → rinse 30 s → dryWhy: self-etch primers are too mild to properly etch enamel, so enamel is etched conventionally for a reliable margin. Dentine is deliberately left alone.
  2. Self-etch primer (acidic monomers) — active 20 s, then gently air-dryWhy: the acidic monomer etches and primes dentine in one step without rinsing — it modifies (doesn't remove) the smear layer and demineralises only ~0.5–1 µm, exactly the depth it then infiltrates, so there is no exposed, uninfiltrated collagen. Solvent evaporates on air-drying.
  3. Bond — passive 10 s, remove excess, light-cure 10–20 sWhy: the separate hydrophobic bond seals the primed surface (kept hydrophobic for durability) and gives the composite its bonding substrate.

When to use / strengths: best for dentine — mild acidity + monomers like 10-MDP form a true chemical bond to hydroxyapatite, and because dentine is never rinsed/over-dried the collagen can't collapse. Low post-op sensitivity. Trade-off: still needs the extra selective-etch step for good enamel bonding.

④ One-step self-etch 7th gen · all-in-one (etch + primer + bond)
  1. Selective enamel etch — 30 s → rinse 30 s → dryWhy: same reason as the 6th gen — the all-in-one's acid is weak on enamel, so etch enamel separately for a durable margin (some products skip this, at the cost of enamel bond strength).
  2. All-in-one adhesive — 1st coat active 20 s, 2nd coat active 20 sWhy: etchant, primer and bond are in a single bottle, applied to dentine without rinsing. Two scrubbed coats improve infiltration and film thickness because a single coat is very thin.
  3. Gently air-dry → remove excess → light-cure 10–20 sWhy: evaporate the solvent and leave a thin even film, then polymerise.

When to use / strengths: fastest and simplest, fewest steps. Trade-off: all the hydrophilic components stay together, so it absorbs water → more prone to hydrolytic degradation and forms a thinner, weaker hybrid layer; the least durable of the four.

Self-etch acidity & the star monomer (10-MDP)

AcidityHybrid layerBond type
Ultra-mild (pH > 2.5)~300 nmChemical bond to HA
Mild (pH ≈ 2)~500 nm — all collagen coveredChemical bond to HA
Strong (pH ≤ 1)~4 µm (100× ultra-mild)Micromechanical only — risk collagen not fully infiltrated

10-MDP is the common acidic monomer: it forms a stable chemical bond to hydroxyapatite, envelops and protects collagen, and resists degradation → why mild self-etch is so good on dentine.

Which adhesive is "best"? It depends on the substrate

Best for ENAMELBest for DENTINE
Etch-and-rinse → micromechanical retentionMild self-etch → chemical bond to HA
Best: 3-step, ethanol/water solvent (acetone is unstable), primer in a separate bottleBest: 2-step, mild acidity, selective enamel etch with phosphoric acid, primer in a separate bottle

Why separate bottles last longer: the bond layer stays uniformly hydrophobic. Mixing primer (solvent + hydrophilic monomers) into the bonding layer speeds degradation. Degradation routes: hydrolysis (water trapped in the adhesive) and endogenous MMPs (enzymes released on etching that digest any exposed, uninfiltrated collagen). Practical longevity tips: keep margins on enamel, maximise enamel to bond to, good operator technique; shake the bottle, keep it closed, avoid light exposure before use.

05

Vital Pulp Therapy — concepts & indications

The whole aim is to keep the pulp alive. VPT preserves the dentine–pulp complex by sealing the advancing lesion away from the oral environment so the pulp can heal and lay down tertiary (reactionary/reparative) dentine. It is less invasive than root canal treatment.

Indications

  • Deep & very deep caries in a vital, restorable tooth
  • Reversible pulpitis of mature teeth; pulpitis of immature/deciduous teeth
  • Pulp exposure with controlled bleeding (for direct cap)
  • Asymptomatic or mild transient symptoms; pre-op radiograph shows no radicular pathology
  • Patient able to return for review

Contraindications

  • Spontaneous, persistent pain (irreversible pulpitis likely)
  • Necrotic pulp; purulent exudate
  • Uncontrolled bleeding from the exposure
  • Sinus tract or apical pathology

Depth tells you what protection you need

Indirect pulp cap — technique

  1. Rubber dam — alwaysIsolation prevents salivary contamination and is essential to the coronal seal that makes VPT work ("vital & seal").
  2. Selective caries removalRemove unsupported enamel (high-speed diamond) and all peripheral caries (slow-speed round carbide or spoon excavator); remove soft infected dentine on pulpal/axial walls with a spoon, no pressure, leaving deep caries over the pulp to avoid exposure.
  3. Clean debris with water/saline (CHX optional); dry gentlyDon't desiccate deep dentine.
  4. Place bioactive material on the deepest pulpal/axial wall onlyApply at the deepest point near the pulp — not on the gingival wall (no pulp beneath it). Keep it thin & localised so it doesn't compromise the seal/retention.
  5. Cover & sealCaOH₂ (Dycal) → conventional GIC base; or MTA/Biodentine → RM-GI lining. The cover protects the cap and seals against the acidic restorative steps.
  6. Definitive restoration — same appointment if possibleSuccess is directly related to a good coronal seal. If you run out of time, at least seal the prep.
  7. Follow-upReview at 6 & 12 months then yearly to 4 years; the tooth should respond within normal limits to pulp sensibility testing.

Capping materials

Calcium hydroxide (Dycal / Life)

  • High alkalinity → antibacterial; stimulates fibroblasts & promotes tertiary dentine.
  • But: forms an incomplete barrier with tunnel defects; dimensionally unstable → dissolves → microleakage & bacterial ingress.
  • ~70% success over 5 years; must be covered by GI so it doesn't dissolve.

Calcium silicate cements (MTA / Biodentine)

  • Biocompatible & bioactive (mineral deposition); excellent seal; dimensionally stable; high compressive strength.
  • MTA can discolour hard tissue (radiopacifier).
  • Biodentine = 80% tricalcium silicate + 15% CaCO₃ + zirconia radiopacifier; releases Ca(OH)₂, stimulates mineralisation, bonds micromechanically (shear bond ≈ GIC).

Evidence to quote (VPT material choice)

Hydraulic calcium silicates show superior histological & clinical outcomes vs. calcium hydroxide for an exposed pulp. Resin-based materials / dentine-bonding agents on an exposed pulp are now contraindicated (cytotoxicity, no dentine bridge, poor outcomes). For a non-exposed deep lesion (IPC), long-term studies show calcium silicate cements and conventional GIC perform similarly. The AAE notes CaOH₂/GIC/resin trail in success (range ~43–92%).

06

Clinical decision-making & justification

The recurring exam task: "select your material and justify it with evidence." There's rarely one right answer — there's a defensible answer. Frame it around cavity depth, remaining tooth structure, lesion activity & cavitation, caries risk, moisture control, esthetics and the patient.

Choosing a restorative material — the argument for each

MaterialJustify it when…Key caveat
Composite resinConservative prep wanted; esthetics matter; good isolation possible; bonds (preserves tooth); repairable; elastic modulus near tooth.Technique- & moisture-sensitive; polymerisation stress; weaker in large stress-bearing situations than amalgam.
AmalgamLarge posterior load-bearing cavity; poor moisture control; poor-access / hard-to-isolate; durable & economical; less technique-sensitive.Needs ≥1.5–2 mm bulk to resist occlusal fracture; non-adhesive (retentive prep); not esthetic; mercury phase-down.
GIC / RM-GIRoot/Class V; high caries risk (fluoride release); moisture-tolerant; chemical bond to dentine; provisional/temporary restorations; deep-lesion base.Lower wear resistance & strength than composite/amalgam — often interim or non-load-bearing.

⚠ Eugenol (ZOE / IRM) under composite

Eugenol inhibits free-radical polymerisation of resin → lower degree of conversion, poor bonding, microleakage, weaker restoration. Never place ZOE/IRM under or adjacent to a planned composite. Use GIC/RM-GI instead. (IRM is fine under amalgam or as a stand-alone temporary.)

The RA3 "grey zone"

A lesion that is clinically moderate but radiographically RA3 (outer ⅓ dentine) is the one ICCMS decision left to clinical judgement: NOC or TPOC. If non-cavitated & arrestable in a low-risk, compliant patient → control non-operatively. If cavitated, active, hard-to-clean, or high-risk → restore.

Evidence you can name-drop

  • Pitts et al. 2014 — ICDAS/ICCMS framework & staged management.
  • Young et al. 2015 — lesion activity (arrested / remineralised / non-cavitated) guiding management.
  • Da Rosa Rodolpho 2022 — posterior composites surviving up to 33 years; marginal degradation/staining is not the same as secondary caries (don't replace serviceable restorations on appearance alone).
  • AAE position statement on VPT — calcium silicate cements outperform CaOH₂ for exposed pulps.
  • Minamata Convention / local ADA policy — amalgam phase-down & vulnerable groups.

Last year's paper — corrected model answers

These are the 2025 station tasks (paraphrased) with clean model answers. Where last year's student answer (the red text) went wrong, it's flagged — those are exactly the traps to avoid.

Station 1 — RA3 cavitated outer-third dentine, no symptoms, vital: choose & justify a material

Cavitated lesion, outer third of dentine (RA3), asymptomatic, vital tooth. Pick your restorative material and justify with evidence.

This is the locally modifiable call (clinically moderate / RA3). Because it is cavitated, the lesion can't be cleaned and non-operative control alone won't arrest it → TPOC. Then justify the material:

  • Composite (preferred for most accessible cavities): conservative bonded prep, esthetic, repairable, modulus near tooth — provided isolation is good.
  • Amalgam: defensible if it's a large/load-bearing or hard-to-isolate Class II in a patient with poor oral hygiene — but you must have ≥1.5–2 mm bulk so it won't fracture under occlusal load.
  • GIC: justify for high caries risk / poor moisture control / interim restoration (fluoride release, moisture tolerance).

Anchor it with activity assessment (Young 2015) and the ICDAS/ICCMS framework (Pitts 2014).

Station 2 — Class II ready for amalgam: what to do & why before restoring

Rubber dam, matrix & wedge in place; amalgam capsule, mixer, dappen dish in front of you. What do you do and why before starting?

  1. Select & activate the capsuleDepress the plunger to break the internal seal between alloy powder and mercury.
  2. Triturate ~6 sMechanically combine into a homogeneous plastic mass.
  3. Check the mix (shake)A single cohesive ball = correct; crumbly/dry or runny = re-do.
  4. Dispense to dappen dish, load carrier, apply promptly & condense from the deepest pointFast set means it must be placed quickly; condensation adapts it into the prep.

Amalgam retention is mechanical (cavity design & undercuts) — it does not "bond" or "adhere" to the cavity, and trituration does not make it "wet enough to adhere." Don't describe amalgam as adhesive.

Station 3 — Non-cavitated occlusal ICDAS code 3: ICCMS/ICDAS approach & steps

Incipient, non-cavitated occlusal lesion, code 3. Materials available include acid, primer, bond, liner, resin sealant, composite, GIC, light.

Manage non-operatively first. A non-cavitated lesion is remineralisable: place a resin-based fissure sealant (after cleaning/etching the fissure) ± topical fluoride, control diet, and review. Move to minimally invasive operative care only if it progresses (active after the watch period) or cavitates.

⚠ Trap from last year

The big error was jumping straight to cutting a cavity and a full adhesive restoration. A non-cavitated code 3 lesion is a sealant + prevention case, not a restoration.

Station 4 — Mid-depth lesion (RB4) ready for composite: steps & why

Mid-depth lesion (RB4, middle third of dentine) and prepared cavity, for resin composite. Acid, primer, bond, liner, CaOH₂, GIC, IRM, composite, light available.

  1. Pulpal protection only if approaching the pulpRB4 is moderate (middle ⅓). A liner/base (RM-GI such as Vitrebond) is placed only over the deepest dentine if it's close to the pulp; a true mid-depth lesion often needs no cap — over-lining wastes bonding area.
  2. Etch enamel 30 s + dentine 15 s → rinse → dry (enamel dry, dentine moist, absorbent paper)Demineralise & expose collagen; don't desiccate.
  3. Primer (active 20 s) → air-dry → bond (passive 10 s) → light-cureForm the hybrid layer; evaporate solvent; even bond film.
  4. Composite — incremental (≤2 mm), cure each layerLowers the C-factor → reduces shrinkage stress & improves marginal adaptation.

⚠ Trap from last year

Two things: (1) applying CaOH₂ to an RB4 (middle-third) lesion is usually over-treatment — calcium hydroxide capping is for deep/very deep lesions, not moderate ones; and (2) say incremental placement reduces stress/C-factor, not "polymerisation shrinkage."

Station 5 — Deep lesion (young patient, pain to sweets/cold): IPC + restoration

Deep lesion (RB5/RC5) in a young patient with pain to sweets/cold (reversible pulpitis). List the steps for indirect pulp cap + final restoration.

This is the examiner's own model sequence:

  1. Rubber dam (already placed — note it)Isolation = seal integrity.
  2. Calcium hydroxide cement on the deepest dentineAlkalinity → antibacterial, stimulates pulpal defence & tertiary dentine.
  3. GIC over the CaOH₂Protects/seals the cap so the acidic restorative steps don't irritate the pulp.
  4. Selective enamel etch 30 s → rinse → dry (enamel dry)Self-etch approach on the deep dentine; etch enamel conventionally for retention.
  5. Primer active 20 s → air-dry → bond 10 s → light-cure 10–20 sHybrid layer + even bond film.
  6. Incremental composite, cure each increment 20–40 sReduces C-factor → less stress, better margins.

If asked for a provisional with GIC instead: rubber dam → selective caries removal → CaOH₂ on deepest dentine → bulk GIC (fluoride-releasing, protective) → contour/occlusion → allow GIC to set before removing the matrix. Note the manual scores a cavitated inner-third lesion as RC5, not RB5.

Station 6 — Da Rosa Rodolpho 2022: dentine-sealing technique & would you change it?

(a) What dentine-sealing technique was used before these restorations (excluding IPC)? (b) Would you do anything different — support with evidence?

(a) Dentine sealing = applying the adhesive system to form the hybrid layer that seals the dentine (an etch-and-rinse adhesive in that long-term cohort) before placing composite — i.e. immediate dentine sealing rather than leaving cut dentine exposed.

(b) Defensible improvements with evidence: for deep, non-exposed dentine use a biologically based material (hydraulic calcium silicate) or condition dentine with 10% polyacrylic acid before a GIC liner (removes smear-layer contaminants, leaves smear plugs). Avoid resin/dentine-bonding agents directly on an exposed pulp (cytotoxic, no dentine bridge — Krifka 2012; De Souza Costa 2000). For exposed pulps, calcium silicate cements (MTA) outperform CaOH₂; for non-exposed deep lesions, CSC and GIC perform similarly long-term.

Fact-check of your group's "244 OSCE info" notes

Your friends' adhesion notes are broadly correct and well-organised — the etch times, the hybrid-layer logic, and the four techniques all hold up. A few points to correct or sharpen before you commit them to memory:

① Generation labels are wrong for the self-etch systems

The notes label both "2-step self-etch" and "1-step self-etch" as (6th gen). Correct mapping: 2-step self-etch = 6th, 1-step self-etch (all-in-one) = 7th, universal/multi-mode = 8th. Safest in the exam: describe them as "two-step self-etch" / "one-step self-etch," which your lecturer uses.

② "Dentine: self-etch, 2-step / Enamel: rinse-and-etch, 3-step"

The principle is right (self-etch suits dentine; etch-and-rinse suits enamel) but the "2-step/3-step" tag attached there is muddled. Step-count describes the system, not the substrate. State it as: etch-and-rinse = best for enamel (micromechanical); mild self-etch = best for dentine (chemical bond to HA).

③ Light-cure time

The notes say "20 s light cure" throughout. Your lecture material gives the adhesive cure as 10–20 s (per manufacturer); composite increments are typically 20–40 s. Either is acceptable if you say "per manufacturer's instructions."

④ Etch order & "air dry desiccation" on enamel

For etch-and-rinse, etch enamel first (30 s) then dentine (15 s) so total dentine contact is only 15 s, then rinse. Drying enamel fully (desiccation) is correct; never desiccate dentine — keep it moist/shiny. The notes are right that enamel is dried and dentine kept moist; just don't let "air dry desiccation" bleed onto dentine.

⑤ One small mislabel in the Week 9 notes

A single all-in-one bottle (etch + primer + bond) is a one-step self-etch — the Week 9 notes briefly call it "one-step etch-and-rinse." Everything else in that section is correct.

Everything else — primer = bifunctional bridging molecule, hydrophilic end into collagen / hydrophobic end to the bond, hybrid layer definition, scrub/penetration logic, the reason we etch (demineralise + expose collagen) — matches the lecture material. Good notes.

Rapid revision — the numbers & one-liners

Etch & bond timings

  • Enamel etch 30 s · dentine 15 s · rinse 30 s
  • Primer active 20 s · bond passive 10 s
  • Adhesive cure 10–20 s · composite increment 20–40 s
  • Increments ≤ 2 mm, ≤ 2 walls; bulk-fill ≤ 4–5 mm

Critical pH

  • Hydroxyapatite ≈ 5.5
  • Fluorapatite ≈ 4.5 (≈10× more acid-resistant)
  • Self-etch: ultra-mild >2.5 · mild ≈2 · strong ≤1

Radiographic scores

  • RA1/RA2 = enamel · RA3 = outer ⅓ dentine → initial
  • RB4 = middle ⅓ dentine → moderate
  • RC5 = inner ⅓ (cavitated) · RC6 = into pulp → extensive

Amalgam essentials

  • Non-adhesive → undercuts, butt joint ~90°, no bevels
  • Bulk ≥ 1.5–2 mm; walls converge ~20°
  • Triturate 6 s; condense from deepest point

Recall extremes

  • High-risk child → as short as 3 months
  • Low-risk adult → up to 2 years
  • F⁻ varnish: 2×/yr (mod) → 4×/yr (high)

Don't-mix-them-up

  • Infected (soft, bacteria) → remove · Affected (firm, sterile) → can stay
  • Incremental reduces stress / C-factor, not shrinkage
  • ZOE never under composite
  • CaOH₂ capping = deep/very deep only, not RB4
