Session 1 · Foundations of restorative dentistry

1Caries process & dentine zones

Caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, multifactorial, dynamic disease. It causes phasic demineralisation and remineralisation, and the lesion progresses or arrests according to the net balance.

Acidogenic bacteria + fermentable carbohydrate → organic acids → plaque pH falls Critical pH: hydroxyapatite 5.5 · fluorapatite 4.5 (~10× more acid-resistant) Sugar frequency matters more than amount Plaque acts as a gel: buffers can't diffuse in, so thicker plaque means more demineralisation
DentineFeelBacteriaAction
InfectedSoft, wet, leatheryYesRemove (but see selective removal in deep lesions)
AffectedFirm or hard, dry, flaky, often stainedNoCan be left, especially over the pulp
Reactionary / sclerotic / tertiaryHard, brownNoPulp's defence. Not caries
Enamel lesion (surface → deep)
  1. Surface zone: outer 20–50 µm, looks intact; demin/remin happens here
  2. Body: most demineralised
  3. Dark zone
  4. Translucent zone: deepest, at the advancing front
Dentine lesion (cavity → pulp)
  1. Decomposed (infected)
  2. Bacterial invasion (infected)
  3. Demineralisation: collagen intact, few bacteria (affected)
  4. Sclerotic: tubules mineralised (reactive)
  5. → Tertiary dentine laid down at the pulp

Spread: through enamel along the prisms → laterally along the EDJ → toward the pulp along the tubules.

2ICDAS / ICCMS, risk & caries management

Diagnosis ≠ treatment. ICDAS describes the lesion; ICCMS adds the radiograph, activity and patient risk to decide management. Clean, dry for 5 s, good light, ball-ended probe (a sharp explorer can cavitate a lesion that could remineralise). ICDAS can't be used on teeth with enamel defects (e.g. amelogenesis imperfecta).

Risk & prevention
  • Risk factors: radiation, dry mouth, poor OH, low fluoride, frequent sugar, symptom-driven attendance, low SES. One high-risk factor = high risk
  • Fluoride varnish 2×/yr (moderate) → 4×/yr (high); high-F toothpaste; diet counselling
  • Recall: 3 months (high-risk child) up to 2 years (low-risk adult)
SDF & selective removal
  • SDF arrests caries (esp. primary teeth): cheap, wide coverage, stains black; still needs diet control and follow-up
  • Selective removal: periphery to hard dentine (for the seal); over the pulp, leave firm or soft dentine in a deep lesion to avoid exposure
  • Hall crown: selected primary molars only

3Tooth sensitivity

The dentine and pulp work as one unit: odontoblast processes (Tomes fibres) run in the tubules. Normally no dentine is exposed. Hydrodynamic theory (Brännström): a stimulus causes rapid fluid movement in open tubules, which distorts odontoblasts and nerve endings → short, sharp pain. Triggers: cold, air, brushing, sweets, cutting and air-drying.

Where dentine gets exposed
  • Cervical region (most susceptible): a gap CEJ exposes dentine once the gingiva recedes (vs overlap or butt joint)
  • Recession, NCCL, caries, cavity preps, failed restorations, fractures and cracks
Management
  • Diagnose first: exclude caries, cracks, failed restorations and pulpitis (lingering or spontaneous pain is not sensitivity)
  • Remove the cause (brushing, acids)
  • Non-cavitated: surface deposition: high-F varnish (Duraphat), CPP-ACP (Tooth Mousse; milk-protein allergy); KNO₃ 5% toothpaste calms the nerve; in office: oxalates, glutaraldehyde/HEMA, bioactive glass
  • Cavitated: dentine sealing or restoration

Tooth loss without bacteria. These lesions are increasingly common (more teeth kept, more acid). They are usually multifactorial.

Treatment ladder
  1. Control the cause alone (diet, brushing, splint, GERD referral)
  2. Cause control + desensitiser
  3. Cause control + restoration

Restore when: dentine exposed and not remineralising, uncontrolled sensitivity, lost form, structural risk (deep lesion near pulp), aesthetics. Saliva flow and buffering strongly affect how fast erosion progresses.

Material choice
  • GIC: chemical bond to dentine, F⁻ release, less post-op sensitivity, tolerates moisture → subgingival margin, poor isolation, high caries risk or dry mouth
  • Composite: best aesthetics, polish and wear; needs good isolation and bonding
  • Deep lesion: GIC liner + composite (sandwich)
  • Sclerotic dentine bonds poorly: roughen with a bur. Selective enamel etch reduced marginal staining (Peumans 2010). Bevel enamel only

5Amalgam

Material
  • Ag–Sn–Cu alloy + Hg. γ = unreacted Ag–Sn · γ1 = Ag–Hg matrix · γ2 = Sn–Hg (weak, corrodes). High-copper alloys remove γ2 (form η Cu–Sn instead)
  • Zinc + moisture → delayed expansion
  • Clinic: SDI Permite C, high-Cu admixed (spherical + lathe-cut); corrosion products seal the margins
  • Pros: cheap, durable, less technique-sensitive. Cons: non-adhesive, needs undercuts, corrodes, cracks teeth, not aesthetic
Preparation (no bond → mechanical)
  • Outline: follow the caries only
  • Resistance: flat floor, rounded internal angles, bulk ≥ 1.5–2 mm, no undermined enamel
  • Retention: slight occlusal convergence + small undercuts (bur ≤ 20°)
  • Margins: butt joint ~90°, no bevel (thin amalgam fractures)
  • Class II: matrix + wedge for a tight contact with no overhang
Step by stepBefore placing amalgam: Class II ready, with dam, matrix and wedge on
  1. Check the field: matrix tight and stable, wedge seals the gingival margin, cavity clean and dry (RMGI liner such as Fuji Bond LC if indicated)Once mixed, working time is short. A loose matrix or wedge gives an overhang or open contact
  2. Select the capsule (SDI Permite, 2-spill) for cavity size; check expiry and that it's intactPre-dosed capsules give the correct alloy-to-mercury ratio (Minamata: capsules only)
  3. Activate: press the capsule on a hard surface until the plunger is flush with the bodyBreaks the seal between alloy powder and mercury so they can mix
  4. Triturate in the amalgamator for ~6 s (lecture value; SDI's instructions give 8 ± 1 s for 1–3 spill on the Ultramat 2)Mechanically coats the alloy particles with Hg to make a homogeneous, plastic mass
  5. Check the mix: shake the capsule and feel a single "pebble" (lecture), meaning one cohesive, plastic ball (not dry and crumbly, not wet and runny)A bad mix gives a weak, poorly adapted, porous, corrosion-prone restoration. Discard it rather than guessing a new time
  6. Open the capsule, dispense into the dappen dish; used capsule goes to amalgam wasteMercury hygiene and safe disposal
  7. Load the carrier and start immediately, condensing from the deepest point (proximal box first) in small incrementsAmalgam sets fast and fresh material won't bond to set material. Condensing adapts it to the walls and removes voids
  8. Overpack → burnish → carve (marginal ridge, anatomy) → remove matrix carefully → check occlusionRemoves the Hg-rich surface; correct form; no high spots
Trap: amalgam does not bond or adhere. Retention is mechanical. Never write that trituration makes it "wet enough to adhere".
Finishing & polishing
  • Not within 24 h (still crystallising); fix gross excess and high spots at placement
  • Green or white stone → finishing bur held at ~45° to the margin → coarse → fine rubber points (or pumice)
  • Low speed, light pressure, water cooling: > 60 °C damages pulp and amalgam (cloudy surface = Hg brought to the surface)
ADA Policy 6.18 / Minamata
  • Safe and effective. Capsules only; bulk mercury prohibited
  • Phase-down, not a ban. ADA: minimise in children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, kidney disease. Minamata: strongly discourages it in deciduous teeth, under 15s and pregnancy
  • Capsules must meet ISO 24234 (now the 2021 edition; your lecture notes say 2015)
  • Don't remove sound amalgam for vague health complaints

6Adhesion: etch & bond systems

Adhesion = mechanical (resin tags), adsorption (chemical to HA or collagen), diffusion, or a mix. Buonocore (1955) introduced acid-etching of enamel.

Enamel ~96% mineral by weight (Week 9 lecture slide says 90%), no water
  • 37% H₃PO₄ 30 s dissolves prism peripheries → micro-porosity → resin tags (micromechanical)
  • Rinse off the "sludge"; dry fully (frosty)
  • Strongest, most durable bond → keep margins on enamel
Dentine collagen + water + tubules + smear layer
  • Etch 15 s only: removes smear layer, exposes collagen mesh
  • Keep moist/shiny. Over-drying collapses collagen ("wet spaghetti"); then cut a little deeper and re-etch. Blot with absorbent paper, not cotton.
  • Hybrid layer = resin interdiffused with collagen (main bond); resin tags seal tubules → less post-op sensitivity

① Three-step etch-and-rinse

4th gen · etch → primer → bond (separate)
  1. Etch 37% H₃PO₄: enamel 30 s, then dentine for the last 15 sMicro-porosity in enamel; strip smear layer and expose collagen. Dentine gets less time so demineralisation isn't deeper than resin can infiltrate
  2. Rinse 30 sRemove acid and dissolved-mineral sludge so adhesive can flow in
  3. Dry selectively: enamel frosty, dentine moist (blot)Enamel has no water; over-dried dentine collagen collapses → no infiltration → lost bond
  4. Primer: active (rub) 20 s → gentle air-dryBifunctional primer: hydrophilic end wets collagen, hydrophobic end links to bond. Solvent chases water and is evaporated → builds the hybrid layer
  5. Bond: passive 10 s → remove excessHydrophobic resin co-polymerises with primer; passive so no air bubbles; even film without pooling
  6. Light-cure 10–20 sLocks the hybrid layer and gives the surface the composite bonds to
Why choose it: best enamel bond. The separate hydrophobic bond layer makes it the most durable. Cost: most steps, most technique-sensitive. On enamel-only bonding, primer isn't needed (no water to displace).

② Two-step etch-and-rinse

5th gen · etch → (primer + bond)
  1. Etch enamel 30 s + dentine 15 s → rinse 30 s → enamel dry, dentine moistSame reasoning as the 3-step
  2. Combined adhesive: coat 1 active 20 s, coat 2 active 20 sPrimer and bond are pre-mixed. Two scrubbed coats give full collagen infiltration and enough film thickness (one coat is too thin)
  3. Gentle air-dry → light-cure 10–20 sEvaporate solvent (no bubbles or porosity), then polymerise
Trade-off: combined bottle is more hydrophilic → absorbs water → slightly less durable than the 3-step.

③ Two-step self-etch

6th gen · selective enamel etch → SE primer → bond
  1. Selective enamel etch: 37% on enamel only, 30 s → rinse → drySelf-etch acid is too mild for enamel. Phosphoric acid gives a reliable enamel margin while dentine is left untouched
  2. Self-etch primer (acidic monomers): active 20 s → gentle air-dry. Do not rinse.Etches and primes in one step. Modifies (doesn't remove) the smear layer, and demineralises only as deep as it infiltrates (~0.5–1 µm) → no exposed, unprotected collagen
  3. Bond: passive 10 s → remove excess → cure 10–20 sSeparate hydrophobic bond seals the primed surface and stays durable
Why choose it: best for dentine. Mild pH (≈2) + 10-MDP gives a chemical bond to hydroxyapatite; no rinsing or over-drying, so collagen can't collapse; low post-op sensitivity. Use it for deep cavities and IPC.

④ One-step self-etch

7th gen · selective enamel etch → all-in-one
  1. Selective enamel etch 30 s → rinse → dryAll-in-one acid is weak on enamel
  2. All-in-one adhesive: coat 1 active 20 s, coat 2 active 20 sEtch, prime and bond from one bottle, no rinse. Two coats for infiltration and thickness
  3. Gentle air-dry → remove excess → cure 10–20 sRemove solvent, leave a thin even film, polymerise
Trade-off: fastest, but all hydrophilic components stay together → water uptake → hydrolytic degradation and a thinner, weaker hybrid layer. Least durable.

⑤ Universal + selective enamel etch

CSU preferred ("modified 6th gen"), also used when replacing amalgam
  1. Selective enamel etch 35–37%, 30 s → rinse → dryBest enamel bond without phosphoric acid on dentine
  2. Universal adhesive: active 20 s × 2 coats (course handbook). The clinic sheet for Scotchbond Universal says one 20 s scrub → 5 s gentle dry → 10 s cure, so follow the productSelf-etch mode on dentine; active scrubbing improves infiltration
  3. Air-dry to remove solvent → light-cure 10–20 sSolvent left behind weakens the interface

7Composite resin

Composition
  • Resin matrix: Bis-GMA, TEGDMA, UDMA, Bis-EMA (more fluid monomer → more shrinkage)
  • Filler: barium glass, silica, zirconia. More filler → less shrinkage. Smaller particles → more surface area → more resin → more shrinkage
  • Silane couples filler to resin · camphorquinone + tertiary amine start the cure · pigments
Classification
  • Filler size: macro → micro → hybrid → micro/nanohybrid → nanofill
  • Viscosity: conventional (up to ~85% filler) vs flowable (up to ~70%)
  • Placement: incremental vs bulk-fill (4–5 mm, more translucent); pre-heated 60–70 °C option
  • Clinic: Gradia Direct (microfilled, sticky, very translucent) · Filtek Supreme XTE (nanofilled, easier handling)
Incremental technique
  • Increments ≤ 2 mm, oblique, touching ≤ 2 walls; one increment per cusp; cure each one (time per manufacturer, typically 20–40 s)
  • Why: full cure through each layer (depth of cure ~1.5–2 mm) + lowers the C-factor per layer → less polymerisation stress → better margins, fewer gaps, less cusp deflection or sensitivity. Also builds anatomy layer by layer.
  • Class II: build the proximal wall first with 2+ oblique increments, which turns it into a Class I
C-factor = bonded ÷ unbonded surfaces
  • Class I box: 5 bonded : 1 free → C = 5 (worst)
  • Class IV is open → low C-factor
  • If shrinkage stress > bond strength → gap, debonding, marginal stain, microleakage, secondary caries, cusp deflection, enamel cracks, post-op sensitivity
Light curing Output and wavelength suited to the product, clean tip, as close and perpendicular as possible, held still, full time, extra cure from other aspects after the matrix comes off. Eye protection. Under-curing → poor properties, wear, fracture.
Step by stepClass I composite, RB4 (mid-depth), rubber dam on
  1. Shade chosen earlier on the hydrated tooth; confirm the dam seals and clean the cavity (rinse, dry)Dehydrated teeth look lighter. Saliva or blood contamination ruins the bond
  2. Pulp protection: none routinely for RB4. RMGI (Vitrebond) only over the deepest dentine if it's close to the pulp, never on enamel margins. No CaOH, no ZOE/IRMMid-depth doesn't need a cap. Lining wastes bonding area. Eugenol inhibits resin polymerisation
  3. Name the system: 3-step etch-and-rinse. 37% H₃PO₄: enamel 30 s, then dentine 15 sEnamel micro-porosity; expose dentine collagen; shorter on dentine to avoid over-demineralising
  4. Rinse 30 s; enamel dry and frosty, dentine moist (blot)Remove sludge; prevent collagen collapse
  5. Primer active 20 s → air-dry → bond passive 10 s → thin → cure 10–20 sForms the hybrid layer; solvent evaporated; even bond film
  6. Composite in oblique increments ≤ 2 mm, ≤ 2 walls each, cure each per manufacturer (typically 20–40 s)Complete cure; lower C-factor and stress (Class I C = 5); better margins
  7. Shape the final occlusal increment (cusps, fissures) before curingLess finishing later; correct anatomy
  8. Remove dam → check centric and excursive occlusion with articulating paper → adjustHigh spots cause fracture, pain and wear
  9. Finish (fine diamond or multi-fluted bur), then polish with rubber points or brushes (course: 24–48 h)Smooth surface means less plaque and stain, and longer life
  10. OHI, diet and fluoride; recallThe restoration treats the lesion, not the disease

Also defensible: 2-step self-etch with selective enamel etch. Name one system and stick to it; don't mix the steps. Composite virtues: adhesion, aesthetics, conservative prep, repairable, modulus close to dentine. Limits: technique- and moisture-sensitive, shrinkage, needs light everywhere.

8Vital pulp therapy

Aim: keep the pulp alive by sealing the lesion off from the mouth ("vital & seal") so the pulp heals and lays down tertiary dentine. Less invasive than RCT. Pulp injury comes from caries, cutting, heat, pressure and toxic materials (monomers, acids, eugenol).

  • Necrosis, pus, sinus tract, apical lesion
  • Uncontrolled bleeding from an exposure

10Repair vs replace

"If in doubt, take it out" has become "take it out only as a last resort". Every replacement makes the cavity bigger (the tooth death cycle). Material is rarely the main cause of failure; patient, operator, cavity size and site dominate. Posterior failures are mainly secondary caries and fracture; anterior failures are mainly aesthetics and fracture.

OptionWhen
Do nothing / monitorVery small deficiency, marginal stain with no clinical effect, patient not bothered
RepolishRoughness, dullness, small excess; margins intact
RepairPartial replacement of no more than 50% of the restoration. One localised defect: marginal stain the patient dislikes, small gap or food trap, small chip or fracture, colour correction
ReplaceGeneralised failure, bulk fracture, caries recurring under a large part, deep interface staining, failed previous repairs, 2 or more repair indications on the same restoration
Amalgam
  • You can't repair amalgam with amalgam: no chemical bond between old and new, and you'd need bulk and retention, which isn't conservative. Repair it with composite instead.
  • Marginal degradation and secondary caries = two problems → replace. Lecturer favours composite, because amalgam causes cracks over time (molars OR 8.79; amalgam-restored OR 4.81) and secondary caries is easier to treat than a crack.
  • Repolish amalgam only when margins are intact and the surface is rough.

Replacing amalgam with composite

  1. Rubber damMoisture control and protection from amalgam particles
  2. Remove with a high-speed diamond and plenty of water (plus suction)Less heat and mercury vapour
  3. Clean off amalgam residue; leave the pigmented (hard) dentine and mask itStain isn't caries, so don't over-cut
  4. Selective enamel etch 35–37%Strong enamel bond
  5. Universal adhesive, active application → air → cureSelf-etch mode on dentine
  6. Incremental compositeCure and stress control

Composite repair

  1. Isolate (rubber dam)Clean interface
  2. Roughen the old composite (diamond bur or air abrasion)Removes the aged surface; micromechanical retention; raises surface energy and wettability
  3. 37% H₃PO₄ (enamel 30 s, dentine 15 s) → rinse → dryOn old composite it only cleans; it etches the tooth
  4. Silane on the old composite, 60 s → air-dryChemically couples to exposed filler particles
  5. Adhesive: bond on the resin, enamel and dentine (primer only if dentine is exposed) → cure 20 sBonds to the resin matrix and the tooth; intermediate layer
  6. Incremental composite → occlusion → finish and polishRestore form and function

Fresh, uncontaminated composite bonds through the oxygen-inhibited layer, so it needs no surface treatment.

11Loupes & ergonomics

Choosing
  • 2.5–3× magnification for students (Prof Bryant)
  • TTL (through-the-lens): wider field, well balanced · flip-up: adjustable declination, cheaper
  • Galilean: light, wide field, long depth of field, cheaper, easier to adapt · Prismatic: stronger and sharper but heavy
  • Check your eyesight first; must be able to take a light (needed for endo and fixed pros)
Set-up & use
  • Working distance 35–42 cm; back straight, neck tilted 10–15°, elbows 90°
  • Set IPD (interpupillary distance) and declination angle (ergo TTL is pre-angled for upright posture)
  • Higher magnification → smaller field of view and depth of field → rigid posture
  • Infection control: don't touch loupes with gloved hands
Session 2 · Anterior & aesthetic restorative dentistry

12Anterior aesthetics & caries

Record every lesion on four axes: site · cavitated or not · severity (initial / moderate / extensive) · activity. Severity and activity are independent. A single radiograph gives depth, not activity. Restoring the lesion doesn't cure the disease.

ElementWhat to assess and reproduce
Shape / formOutline, incisal edge, line angles, width-to-length; square, triangular or oval
Symmetry & proportionContralateral tooth, midline, gingival levels. Golden proportion 1.618 : 1 : 0.618 (central : lateral : canine apparent width) is a guide, not a rule
Position & alignmentRotation, inclination, overjet/overbite, spacing; may need ortho or perio first
Surface textureLobes, perikymata, depressions; too little looks flat, too much holds stain
ColourValue first, then chroma, then hue; varies from cervical to incisal
TranslucencyEnamel translucent (incisal most), dentine opaque and chromatic
Illusions by contour
  • Narrower: line angles closer together
  • Wider: line angles and depressions further apart
  • Shorter: emphasise horizontal texture; move the gingival height of contour incisally
  • Longer: emphasise vertical form
Restorative options
  • Direct composite: conservative, repairable, one visit, cheaper; technique-sensitive
  • Indirect (ceramic, CAD-CAM): extensive loss or major colour/form change; more reduction, cost and visits
  • Interdisciplinary: ortho for position, perio for gingival levels
  • Size alone doesn't force an indirect restoration

13Shade selection

  • When: before preparation and before the rubber dam, on a clean, hydrated tooth. Dehydration raises value and opacity → the restoration ends up too light.
  • Light: consistent daylight or cool artificial light; neutral surroundings; quick glances, rest your eyes.
  • Order: value → chroma → hue. Map zones (cervical/body dentine, facial enamel, incisal translucency) rather than matching one tab to the whole tooth.
  • Cured button trial: place unbonded composite (no etch or bond), cure it (curing changes colour), compare, flick it off.
  • Layered case: pick a more opaque dentine/body shade + a more translucent enamel shade. A dark substrate needs planned masking, not just more opacity.
  • Electronic devices (e.g. EasyShade): calibrate, hold flat and still on the area.

14Class III

Proximal surface of an incisor or canine with the incisal angle intact (involving the angle makes it Class IV). Recreates the proximal surface, contact, embrasures and lingual contour.

AccessWhen
Palatal / lingualUsually preferred: keeps facial enamel. Not if it removes more sound tissue
FacialLesion is facial, facial enamel undermined, or large lesion. Careful bevel, shade and polish
Strictly proximalVery small lesion after separation; high risk to the neighbour
Preparation
  • Smallest bur; follow the lesion, no routine undercuts
  • Remove soft dentine and unsupported enamel; dark but hard dentine can stay (colour isn't the endpoint)
  • Bevel: facial enamel, 45°, 0.5–1.2 mm (Class III) → more bond area, blended margin. Never on dentine or cementum
Layering (3 increments)
  1. Proximal enamel shell against the clear strip (seal, contact)
  2. Dentine/body core, stopping short of the surface
  3. Final enamel on the access surface, feathered over the bevel
Sequence Diagnose → LA → record occlusion → shade (hydrated, cured trial) → rubber dam → pre-wedge / fender → conservative access and excavation → remove unsupported enamel, bevel → clear strip + wedge past all margins → etch & bond → shell → body → enamel (cure each) → finish, floss, check occlusion after removing the dam.

15Class IV & trauma

Proximal surface plus the incisal angle or edge (caries, trauma or a lost restoration). Adds incisal form, optical depth, fracture resistance and anterior guidance.

Silicone index (palatal guide)
  • From a wax-up, a 3D-printed replica or the intact pre-op tooth
  • Captures palatal + incisal contour; trial-seat it before etching
  • After bonding: thin enamel increment into the index → seat → cure → check the shell
  • No index? Clear matrix + wedge + finger support
Sequence Diagnose and consent → LA → record occlusion → shade (enamel + body) → plan the form → rubber dam → protect the neighbour → defect-centred prep → 45° facial bevel 0.5–2 mm → etch & bond → palatal shell → proximal wall → body (mamelons if present) → facial enamel → extra cure → finish, polish, occlusion (protrusive too).

Fractured incisor Trauma assessment first (history, mobility, sensibility, radiographs; rule out root or alveolar fracture). Keep any fragment hydrated and trial-fit it; reattach if it fits and you can isolate and bond. Pulp exposure ≠ automatic RCT: VPT may suit a vital tooth. Review sensibility and radiographs over time; pulp complications can appear late.

The matrix and wedge help, but they don't guarantee a contact. Adapt the first proximal increment deliberately and check with floss.

17Finishing & polishing

Finishing vs polishing
  • Finishing sets contour, margins, contact and occlusion (removes material)
  • Polishing removes scratches coarse → fine for an enamel-like gloss
  • Polishing can't fix an open margin, a bad bond or caries
  • Course rule: finish composite immediately, polish at 24–48 h · amalgam: nothing extra within 24 h
Composite sequence
  1. Gross excess: discs, diamonds, multi-fluted carbide burs
  2. Fine finishing: contour, line angles, embrasures, texture
  3. Polish: extra-fine discs (< 8 µm), rubber points, SiC brushes, felt + paste (1.5–0.5 µm)

Don't skip grits; light pressure, keep moving, keep cool. Scalpel blade for cervical flash; abrasive strips for proximal surfaces (don't open the contact).

Acceptance check Margins sealed and smooth (no flash, ledge or gap) · contact passes floss without shredding · form, line angles and incisal edge match · occlusion checked in centric and excursions after the dam is off · gloss matches enamel · patient can clean it.

18Failure & longevity

Definitions
  • Failure: unacceptable outcome needing intervention. Old or imperfect ≠ failed
  • Success: meets all criteria with no intervention · Survival: still in service, may include a repair
  • Marginal stain ≠ secondary caries; sensitivity ≠ proof of a failed bond
Evidence
  • Demarco 2017: AFR anterior 1–5%, posterior 1–3%. Posterior failures = secondary caries + fracture; anterior = aesthetics
  • Collares 2017: 72,196 anterior composites; AFR ~4.6% at 5 y; varies by dentist (2–11%); higher risk in young children, over-65s and central incisors
  • Da Rosa Rodolpho 2022: posterior composites up to 33 years
Failure modeLikely causes
FractureTrauma, parafunction, thin or unsupported material, voids, under-cure, heavy occlusion
Loss of adhesionContamination, poor adhesive handling, under-cure, poor substrate
Marginal stainFinishing damage, wear, poor adaptation, no bevel, bond degradation, caries
Colour mismatchShade taken on a dry tooth, wrong opacity or thickness, dark substrate, ageing, diet, smoking
Loss of anatomyWear, bruxism, nail biting, erosion, over-finishing
Secondary cariesHigh caries risk, plaque stagnation, defective margins

Prevention: correct technique (increments), right material, rubber dam, evidence-based practice. Composite is repairable, not permanent; give practical advice (no opening packets with your front teeth, manage nail biting).

19Clinical decision-making

What drives a decision
  • Clinical factors: disease status, symptoms, history and future risk
  • Non-clinical factors: SES, insurance, patient and clinician characteristics
  • Education + preference + experience + expected progression + patient and economic factors = decision
  • Errors in diagnosis and planning harm patients
In practice
  • Watch for bias: general and mental health, social and behavioural factors, age
  • Involve the patient: better understanding and relationship
  • Systemic phase first (perio, OH) before any restorative or aesthetic work
  • Exam: caries, restorations, fractures, defects, bite, parafunction, perio. Check substrate quality before bonding; use silane for composite-to-composite repair

20Direct composite veneers

A layer of tooth-coloured material on the facial surface to fix localised or generalised defects and intrinsic discolouration. It can be direct composite, processed composite, porcelain or pressed ceramic.

Indications & pros
  • Change shape or colour; close diastemas; patient wants a conservative option
  • Cheaper than indirect, immediate result, minimally invasive, easily repaired
Limitations
  • Weakened tooth structure
  • Parafunction (no space: open the bite posteriorly first)
  • Periodontal disease
  • Excessive buccal inclination
  • Severe discolouration (composite can't mask it)

Planning: age, face, tooth form, occlusion, adjacent teeth, previous work, the patient's expectations. Sequence: diagnosis and plan → LA → material selection → rubber dam → prep → restoration (etch and bond per manufacturer, layered) → finishing and polishing.

Preparation steps
  1. Cervical orientation groove, diamond at 45° to the long axis, following the cervical contour
  2. Vertical depth grooves (bur diameter guides the depth)
  3. Central cervico-incisal groove across the 3 planes
  4. Connect the grooves
  5. Extend the cervical groove into the proximals (keep the contacts)
  6. Margin slightly subgingival (~0.3 mm; not in the sim clinic)
  7. Finish the preparation

Reduction depends on the substrate colour and the tooth's position in the arch. Enamel removal is irreversible.

21Diastema

A space between teeth, most often between the maxillary centrals. It isn't automatically disease. Don't close a diastema without knowing the cause. Check ortho needs (open bite, arch and tooth dimensions), fix the cause, then build for longevity.

Causes
  • Prominent labial frenum
  • Congenitally missing or undersized (peg) teeth
  • Interarch tooth-size discrepancy (Bolton)
  • Supernumerary teeth (e.g. mesiodens)
  • Heredity · tongue thrusting
  • Periodontal disease (migration) · posterior bite collapse
Options
  • Observe: developmental spacing (children's spaces can close)
  • Treat the cause: perio, habits, frenum
  • Orthodontics: move and redistribute the space when adding material would make the teeth too wide
  • Direct composite additions, or veneers / combined treatment
  • Decide on aetiology, aesthetics, expectations and cost
Additive composite closure Plan widths, symmetry and the midline (don't just widen both centrals) → hydrated shade → isolate → protect the neighbours, clear matrix → etch & bond → build proximal additions in increments → cure → refine emergence, line angles and contact → polish → floss and occlusion → review.

Avoid over-wide incisors, a bulky cervical ledge, an open contact, uncleanable embrasures or bonding the teeth together. The lecture groups spaces as < 2 mm, 2–6 mm and > 6 mm, but size alone doesn't decide the treatment.

22Tooth bleaching

Mechanism HP or CP penetrates enamel and dentine and oxidises organic pigment molecules.
  • Extrinsic stain → cleaning or paste. Intrinsic → bleaching
  • Classified as vital vs non-vital and in-office vs at-home
  • Indications: ageing, trauma (still vital), medication, calcific metamorphosis, brown fluorosis (white fluorosis responds poorly); also before or after restorations to match shades
  • Contraindications: heavy tobacco or alcohol use; caries or open cavities
  • Success depends on diagnosis of the cause, treatment plan, technique, instructions and patient cooperation
  • Restorations don't bleach, so do restorative shade work after bleaching
Agents
  • Home: 10–20% CP (10–15% recommended) or 4–10% HP
  • In-office: 30–35% HP
In-office prophy → initial shade → gingival barrier → bleach → monitor → final shade.
Fully dentist-controlled and fast, but costly, unpredictable, risk of soft-tissue damage and sensitivity.
Home initial shade → impression or scan → custom trays (vacuum-formed), fit and instructions → monitor → final shade.
Low concentration, cheap, few side effects, but relies on compliance and takes longer. Wipe off excess gel; no drinking or rinsing during wear; remove trays for meals and hygiene.

23ADA Policy 2.2.8: whitening by non-dentists

High yield Causes of pulpal damage or irreversible injury (policy §3.4):
  • Incorrect heat or other energy (plasma arc light, Class 4 laser) → pulpal (nerve) injury
  • Incorrect use → burns to adjacent soft tissues
  • No protective eyewear with intense light or lasers → irreversible eye injury
  • Protective gel, gingival barriers and rinsing are protective steps, not risks
PointContent
Position summaryFor public safety, only registered dental practitioners trained and competent in whitening should use or supply products with > 6% hydrogen peroxide (or equivalent)
ThresholdsOnly dentists may supply HP > 6% or CP > 18%. CP ≈ ⅓ its strength as HP, so 18% CP ≈ 6% HP
Poisons StandardSchedule 5 ("Caution"): HP 3–6% or CP 9–18% can be sold OTC with warnings. Schedule 10: HP > 6% or CP > 18% is prohibited except for use by dental practitioners
Before treatmentComprehensive exam by a dentist; treatment must be justified; risks and costs explained; documented informed consent; realistic expectations
Hazards (§3.3)HP > 5% is a hazardous substance (WorkSafe). Skin, eye or mucosa contact → irritation or burns. Ingestion → oesophagus or stomach irritation, bleeding. Percolation into the pulp (faster through exposed dentine or enamel cracks) → pulpal inflammation
Energy (§3.4–3.5)Heat or light misuse → pulp damage and soft-tissue burns; no eyewear → irreversible eye injury (AS/NZS 4173:2018). Class 4 lasers need a state licence in WA, Qld, Vic and Tas
Safety (§3.9)Safe and effective under a dentist. Most common side effects: transient sensitivity and soft-tissue irritation
Why not salonsNon-dentists (salons, kiosks, mobile services) can't assess whether it's safe, choose a technique, or meet regulated standards. Online products can exceed legal limits; marketing drives unrealistic expectations and overuse. ACCC monitors advertising and supply; regulators should educate the public
