1Etch & bond: the four systems

Enamel ~96% mineral by weight (Week 9 lecture slide says 90%), no water
  • 37% H₃PO₄ 30 s dissolves prism peripheries → micro-porosity → resin tags (micromechanical)
  • Rinse off the "sludge"; dry fully (frosty)
  • Strongest, most durable bond → keep margins on enamel
Dentine collagen + water + tubules + smear layer
  • Etch 15 s only: removes smear layer, exposes collagen mesh
  • Keep moist/shiny. Over-drying collapses collagen ("wet spaghetti"). Blot with absorbent paper, not cotton.
  • Hybrid layer = resin interdiffused with collagen (main bond); resin tags seal tubules → less post-op sensitivity

*The clinic materials sheet labels Scotchbond Universal "6th gen" (and calls CSU's selective-etch method "modified 6th gen"). Generation labels vary between sources. In written answers, describe the strategy and number of steps (for example "two-step self-etch").

① Three-step etch-and-rinse

4th gen · etch → primer → bond (separate)
  1. Etch 37% H₃PO₄: enamel 30 s, then dentine for the last 15 sMicro-porosity in enamel; strip smear layer and expose collagen. Dentine gets less time so demineralisation isn't deeper than resin can infiltrate
  2. Rinse 30 sRemove acid and dissolved-mineral sludge so adhesive can flow in
  3. Dry selectively: enamel frosty, dentine moist (blot)Enamel has no water; over-dried dentine collagen collapses → no infiltration → lost bond
  4. Primer: active (rub) 20 s → gentle air-dryBifunctional primer: hydrophilic end wets collagen, hydrophobic end links to bond. Solvent chases water and is evaporated → builds the hybrid layer
  5. Bond: passive 10 s → remove excessHydrophobic resin co-polymerises with primer; passive so no air bubbles; even film without pooling
  6. Light-cure 10–20 sLocks the hybrid layer and gives the surface the composite bonds to
Why choose it: best enamel bond. The separate hydrophobic bond layer makes it the most durable. Cost: most steps, most technique-sensitive.

② Two-step etch-and-rinse

5th gen · etch → (primer + bond)
  1. Etch enamel 30 s + dentine 15 s → rinse 30 s → enamel dry, dentine moistSame reasoning as the 3-step
  2. Combined adhesive: coat 1 active 20 s, coat 2 active 20 sPrimer and bond are pre-mixed. Two scrubbed coats give full collagen infiltration and enough film thickness (one coat is too thin)
  3. Gentle air-dry → light-cure 10–20 sEvaporate solvent (no bubbles or porosity), then polymerise
Trade-off: combined bottle is more hydrophilic → absorbs water → slightly less durable than the 3-step.

③ Two-step self-etch

6th gen · selective enamel etch → SE primer → bond
  1. Selective enamel etch: 37% on enamel only, 30 s → rinse → drySelf-etch acid is too mild for enamel. Phosphoric acid gives a reliable enamel margin while dentine is left untouched
  2. Self-etch primer (acidic monomers): active 20 s → gentle air-dry. Do not rinse.Etches and primes in one step. Modifies (doesn't remove) the smear layer, and demineralises only as deep as it infiltrates → no exposed, unprotected collagen
  3. Bond: passive 10 s → remove excess → cure 10–20 sSeparate hydrophobic bond seals the primed surface and stays durable
Why choose it: best for dentine. Mild pH (≈2) + 10-MDP gives a chemical bond to hydroxyapatite; no rinsing or over-drying, so collagen can't collapse; low post-op sensitivity. Use it for deep cavities and IPC.

④ One-step self-etch

7th gen · selective enamel etch → all-in-one
  1. Selective enamel etch 30 s → rinse → dryAll-in-one acid is weak on enamel
  2. All-in-one adhesive: coat 1 active 20 s, coat 2 active 20 sEtch, prime and bond from one bottle, no rinse. Two coats for infiltration and thickness
  3. Gentle air-dry → remove excess → cure 10–20 sRemove solvent, leave a thin even film, polymerise
Trade-off: fastest, but all hydrophilic components stay together → water uptake → hydrolytic degradation and a thinner, weaker hybrid layer. Least durable.

⑤ Universal + selective enamel etch

CSU preferred ("modified 6th gen"), also used when replacing amalgam
  1. Selective enamel etch 35–37%, 30 s → rinse → dryBest enamel bond without phosphoric acid on dentine
  2. Universal adhesive: active 20 s × 2 coats (course handbook). The clinic sheet for Scotchbond Universal says one 20 s scrub → 5 s gentle dry → 10 s cure, so follow the productSelf-etch mode on dentine; active scrubbing improves infiltration
  3. Air-dry to remove solvent → light-cure 10–20 sSolvent left behind weakens the interface
Traps: 1-step self-etch is not "one-step etch-and-rinse". Never rinse a self-etch primer. Never phosphoric-etch dentine in a self-etch protocol. Durability falls because of hydrolysis (trapped water) and MMPs (enzymes released on etching that digest exposed, uninfiltrated collagen).

2Composite placement (Q6 model)

Incremental technique
  • Increments ≤ 2 mm, oblique, touching ≤ 2 walls; cure each one (time per manufacturer, typically 20–40 s)
  • Why: full cure through each layer (light penetration) + lowers the C-factor per layer → less polymerisation stress → better marginal adaptation, fewer gaps and less cusp deflection or sensitivity. Also lets you build anatomy layer by layer.
  • Bulk-fill (4–5 mm) only for products made for it
C-factor = bonded ÷ unbonded surfaces
  • Class I box: 5 bonded : 1 free → C = 5 (worst)
  • Class IV is open → low C-factor
  • Shrinkage is a material property; increments reduce the stress, not the total shrinkage
Model answerQ6: Class I on 26, RB4 (mid-depth), rubber dam on
  1. Shade chosen earlier on the hydrated tooth; confirm the dam seals and clean the cavity (rinse, dry)Dehydrated teeth look lighter. Saliva or blood contamination ruins the bond
  2. Pulp protection: none routinely for RB4. RMGI (Vitrebond) only over the deepest dentine if it's close to the pulp, never on enamel margins. No CaOH, no ZOE/IRMMid-depth doesn't need a cap. Lining wastes bonding area. Eugenol inhibits resin polymerisation
  3. Name the system: 3-step etch-and-rinse. 37% H₃PO₄: enamel 30 s, then dentine 15 sEnamel micro-porosity; expose dentine collagen; shorter on dentine to avoid over-demineralising
  4. Rinse 30 s; enamel dry and frosty, dentine moist (blot)Remove sludge; prevent collagen collapse
  5. Primer active 20 s → air-dry → bond passive 10 s → thin → cure 10–20 sForms the hybrid layer; solvent evaporated; even bond film
  6. Composite in oblique increments ≤ 2 mm, ≤ 2 walls each, cure each per manufacturer (typically 20–40 s)Complete cure; lower C-factor and stress (Class I C = 5); better margins
  7. Shape the final occlusal increment (cusps, fissures) before curingLess finishing later; correct anatomy
  8. Remove dam → check centric and excursive occlusion with articulating paper → adjustHigh spots cause fracture, pain and wear
  9. Finish (fine diamond or multi-fluted bur), then polish with rubber points or brushes (course: 24–48 h)Smooth surface means less plaque and stain, and longer life
  10. OHI, diet and fluoride; recallThe restoration treats the lesion, not the disease

Also defensible: 2-step self-etch with selective enamel etch. Name one system and stick to it; don't mix the steps.

3Vital pulp therapy

Aim: keep the pulp alive by sealing the lesion off from the mouth ("vital & seal") so the pulp heals and lays down tertiary dentine. Less invasive than RCT.

DepthDefinitionProtection
SuperficialAt the DEJAdhesive + composite; RMGI liner (Fuji Bond LC) under amalgam
Shallow0.5–1 mm below the DEJ
MediumAbout half-way through dentine
DeepMore than half-way, RDT > 0.5 mmVPT (pulp capping)

Indications
  • Deep or very deep caries, vital and restorable tooth
  • Reversible pulpitis (provoked, short-lived pain, e.g. to sweets or cold)
  • No radicular pathology on the radiograph
  • Immature or deciduous teeth (root development continues)
Contraindications
  • Spontaneous or lingering pain (irreversible pulpitis)
  • Necrosis, pus, sinus tract, apical lesion
  • Uncontrolled bleeding from an exposure
ProcedureWhat it is
Indirect pulp capSelective caries removal, no exposure; deep caries left over the pulp and sealed
Direct pulp capExposure with controlled bleeding → calcium silicate (MTA or Biodentine) directly on the pulp → seal
Partial or full pulpotomyRemove part or all of the coronal pulp; the rest stays vital
PulpectomyWhole pulp removed. Not VPT
Model answerQ5: very deep caries, young patient, IPC then composite
  1. Confirm reversible pulpitis (provoked, non-lingering), positive sensibility, no periapical pathology → IPC indicated. Give LAVPT only works on a pulp that can heal
  2. Rubber dam: alwaysAsepsis and moisture control. Success depends on the coronal seal
  3. Remove unsupported enamel (high-speed diamond)Access, and sound margins that will seal
  4. Remove all peripheral caries (slow-speed round carbide or spoon) down to hard dentineA sound periphery gives a bacteria-tight seal
  5. On the pulpal and axial walls, remove only soft infected dentine with a spoon, no pressure; leave deep caries over the pulpAvoids exposure; sealed residual bacteria become inactive
  6. Clean with water or saline (CHX optional); dry gently, don't desiccateRemove debris without damaging deep dentine
  7. CaOH (Life/Dycal): thin layer on the deepest pulpal/axial point only, not the gingival wallpH 11–13 is antibacterial and stimulates tertiary dentine. There's no pulp under the gingival wall, so keep that area for bonding
  8. Cover with a conventional GIC base (don't condition over the CaOH). The VPT lecture says conventional GIC; the clinic sheet for Life says "seal with RMGI", so either is defensible if you justify itCaOH dissolves and is weak. GIC protects it, seals, releases F⁻ and shields the pulp from acid in later steps
  9. 2-step self-etch: selective enamel etch 30 s → rinse → dry → SE primer active 20 s → air → bond passive 10 s → cure 10–20 sNo phosphoric acid near the pulp. Mild self-etch is best on dentine (chemical bond); the enamel etch keeps the margins strong
  10. Incremental composite ≤ 2 mm, cure eachFull cure; lower C-factor and stress → margins stay sealed
  11. Remove dam, check occlusion, finish and polish. Restore definitively the same visit (if out of time, at least seal)The coronal seal decides success
  12. Review at 6 and 12 months, then yearly up to 4 years: symptoms, sensibility, radiographConfirm the pulp is vital and healthy, with no apical change

Alternative pathway: MTA or Biodentine on the deepest point + RMGI (light-cure GI) lining. Apply the conditioner last to keep its contact with the bioceramic short.

Calcium hydroxide (Life / Dycal)Calcium silicate (MTA / Biodentine)

Traps: CaOH on an RB4 (mid-depth) lesion is over-treatment. Ledermix is not indicated (no RCT evidence). Resin or bonding agent directly on an exposed pulp is contraindicated (cytotoxic, no dentine bridge). Brown, hard tertiary dentine is not caries.

4Amalgam (Q4 model)

Model answerQ4: Class II ready; dam, matrix and wedge on; capsule, mixer and dappen dish available
  1. Check the field: matrix tight and stable, wedge seals the gingival margin, cavity clean and dry (RMGI liner such as Fuji Bond LC if indicated)Once mixed, working time is short. A loose matrix or wedge gives an overhang or open contact
  2. Select the capsule (SDI Permite, 2-spill) for cavity size; check expiry and that it's intactPre-dosed capsules give the correct alloy-to-mercury ratio (Minamata: capsules only)
  3. Activate: press the capsule on a hard surface until the plunger is flush with the bodyBreaks the seal between alloy powder and mercury so they can mix
  4. Triturate in the amalgamator for ~6 s (lecture value; SDI's instructions give 8 ± 1 s for 1–3 spill on the Ultramat 2)Mechanically coats the alloy particles with Hg to make a homogeneous, plastic mass
  5. Check the mix: shake the capsule and feel a single "pebble" (lecture), meaning one cohesive, plastic ball (not dry and crumbly, not wet and runny)A bad mix gives a weak, poorly adapted, porous, corrosion-prone restoration. Discard it rather than guessing a new time
  6. Open the capsule, dispense into the dappen dish; used capsule goes to amalgam wasteMercury hygiene and safe disposal
  7. Load the carrier and start immediately, condensing from the deepest point (proximal box first) in small incrementsAmalgam sets fast and fresh material won't bond to set material. Condensing adapts it to the walls and removes voids

If asked to finish the restoration: overpack → burnish → carve (marginal ridge, anatomy) → remove matrix carefully → check occlusion → finish and polish after 24 h or more.

Trap: amalgam does not bond or adhere. Retention is mechanical: slight occlusal convergence of the walls plus small retentive undercuts (OSCE notes: bur angled ≤ 20°). Never write that trituration makes it "wet enough to adhere".
Prep principles
  • Butt-joint margins about 90°, no bevels (thin amalgam fractures)
  • Bulk at least 1.5–2 mm; flat floor; rounded internal angles
  • High-copper alloy removes the weak, corrosion-prone γ2 phase
ADA Policy 6.18 / Minamata
  • Safe and effective. Capsules only; bulk mercury prohibited
  • Phase-down, not a ban. ADA: minimise in children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, kidney disease. Minamata: strongly discourages it in deciduous teeth, under 15s and pregnancy
  • Capsules must meet ISO 24234 (now the 2021 edition; your lecture notes say 2015)
  • Don't remove sound amalgam for vague health complaints

5Repair vs replace

"If in doubt, take it out" has become "take it out only as a last resort". Choose the least invasive option that fixes the diagnosed problem.

OptionWhen
Do nothing / monitorVery small deficiency, marginal stain with no clinical effect, patient not bothered
RepolishRoughness, dullness, small excess; margins intact
RepairPartial replacement of no more than 50% of the restoration. One localised defect: marginal stain the patient dislikes, small gap or food trap, small chip or fracture, colour correction
ReplaceGeneralised failure, bulk fracture, caries recurring under a large part, deep interface staining, failed previous repairs, 2 or more repair indications on the same restoration
Amalgam (Q1)
  • You can't repair amalgam with amalgam: no chemical bond between old and new, and you'd need bulk and retention, which isn't conservative. Repair it with composite instead.
  • Marginal degradation and secondary caries = two problems → replace. Lecturer favours composite, because amalgam causes cracks over time (molars OR 8.79; amalgam-restored OR 4.81) and secondary caries is easier to treat than a crack.
  • Repolish amalgam only when margins are intact and the surface is rough.

Replacing amalgam with composite

  1. Rubber damMoisture control and protection from amalgam particles
  2. Remove with a high-speed diamond and plenty of water (plus suction)Less heat and mercury vapour
  3. Clean off amalgam residue; leave the pigmented (hard) dentine and mask itStain isn't caries, so don't over-cut
  4. Selective enamel etch 35–37%Strong enamel bond
  5. Universal adhesive, active application → air → cureSelf-etch mode on dentine
  6. Incremental compositeCure and stress control

Composite repair

  1. Isolate (rubber dam)Clean interface
  2. Roughen the old composite (diamond bur or air abrasion)Removes the aged surface; micromechanical retention; raises surface energy and wettability
  3. 37% H₃PO₄ (enamel 30 s, dentine 15 s) → rinse → dryOn old composite it only cleans; it etches the tooth
  4. Silane on the old composite, 60 s → air-dryChemically couples to exposed filler particles
  5. Adhesive: bond on the resin, enamel and dentine (primer only if dentine is exposed) → cure 20 sBonds to the resin matrix and the tooth; intermediate layer
  6. Incremental composite → occlusion → finish and polishRestore form and function

Fresh, uncontaminated composite bonds through the oxygen-inhibited layer, so it needs no surface treatment.

6Whitening + ADA Policy 2.2.8

Q2 answer Risks of pulpal damage or irreversible injury (policy §3.4):
  • ✅ a Incorrect heat or other energy (plasma arc light, Class 4 laser) → pulpal (nerve) injury
  • ✅ b Incorrect use → burns to adjacent soft tissues
  • ❌ c Protective gel and rinsing prevent injury
  • ✅ d No protective eyewear with intense light or lasers → irreversible eye injury

Policy 2.2.8: what to know

ThresholdsOnly dentists may supply HP > 6% or CP > 18%. CP ≈ ⅓ its strength as HP, so 18% CP ≈ 6% HP

Bleaching basics (Dr Weber)

Mechanism HP or CP penetrates enamel and dentine and oxidises organic pigment molecules.
  • Extrinsic stain → cleaning or paste. Intrinsic → bleaching
  • Indications: ageing, trauma (still vital), medication, calcific metamorphosis, brown fluorosis (white fluorosis responds poorly); also before or after restorations to match shades
  • Contraindications: heavy tobacco or alcohol use; caries or open cavities
  • Restorations don't bleach, so do restorative shade work after bleaching
Agents
  • Home: 10–20% CP (10–15% recommended) or 4–10% HP
  • In-office: 30–35% HP
In-office prophy → initial shade → gingival barrier → bleach → monitor → final shade.
Fully dentist-controlled and fast, but costly, unpredictable, risk of soft-tissue damage and sensitivity.
Home initial shade → impression or scan → custom trays (vacuum-formed), fit and instructions → monitor → final shade.
Low concentration, cheap, few side effects, but relies on compliance and takes longer. Wipe off excess gel; no drinking or rinsing during wear; remove trays for meals and hygiene.

7Anterior & S2 topics (compressed)

Shade, aesthetics and Class III / IV
  • Shade: clean, hydrated tooth, before the dam (dehydration makes it look lighter and more opaque). Judge value first, then chroma, then hue. Test with a cured, unbonded composite button.
  • Six aesthetic elements: shape, symmetry and proportion (golden 1.618 : 1 : 0.618), position and alignment, surface texture, colour, translucency (incisal edge is most translucent).
  • Illusions: line angles closer together look narrower; further apart look wider. Horizontal texture makes a tooth look shorter.
  • Class III = anterior proximal, incisal angle intact. Palatal access is preferred (keeps facial enamel) but isn't mandatory. Class IV adds the incisal angle or edge.
  • Bevel: facial enamel only, 45°, 0.5–2 mm: more enamel bonding area and a blended margin. Don't bevel dentine or cementum.
  • Isolation: rubber dam, pre-wedge, clear polyester strip and wedge to protect the neighbour; silicone index for the palatal shell in Class IV.
  • Layering: palatal enamel shell → proximal wall and contact → opaque dentine/body core → (effects) → translucent facial enamel. Cure each layer.
  • Finish vs polish: finishing fixes contour, margins and occlusion; polishing removes scratches (coarse to fine). Course rule: finish immediately, polish at 24–48 h.
Direct composite veneers and diastema
  • Veneer indications: shape or colour change, closing diastemas, patient wants conservative treatment. Pros: cheaper, immediate, minimally invasive, repairable.
  • Limits: weakened tooth, parafunction, periodontal disease, excessive buccal inclination, severe discolouration (composite can't mask it).
  • Prep depth: 0.5–0.75 mm mid-facial, tapering to 0.3–0.5 mm at the cervical; margin about 0.3 mm subgingival (not in sim clinic); keep contacts; facial only.
  • Prep steps: cervical groove at 45° → vertical depth grooves → central cervico-incisal groove (3 planes) → connect grooves → extend into the proximals → cervical margin → finish.
  • Sequence: Dx/Tx plan → LA → material → rubber dam → prep → restore (etch and bond per manufacturer) → finish and polish.
  • Diastema causes: frenum, missing teeth, small teeth, Bolton discrepancy, supernumeraries, heredity, tongue thrust, perio disease, posterior bite collapse. Don't close it without knowing the cause; check ortho needs first.
Failure, longevity and clinical decision-making
  • Anterior failures: mainly aesthetic (colour mismatch, marginal stain), debonding, fracture. Posterior: secondary caries and fracture.
  • AFR (Demarco 2017): anterior 1–5%, posterior 1–3%. Collares 2017: 72,196 anterior composites, AFR about 4.6% at 5 years, varies by dentist (2–11%).
  • Causes: trauma, poor prep, wrong material, patient risk. Prevention: correct technique (increments), material choice, rubber dam, evidence-based practice.
  • Marginal stain ≠ secondary caries. Stain alone doesn't justify replacing a restoration.
  • Decision-making = clinical factors (disease, symptoms, risk) + non-clinical factors (SES, insurance, patient and clinician characteristics). Watch for bias. Complete the systemic phase first (perio, OH) before aesthetic work. Shared decisions build a better relationship.
