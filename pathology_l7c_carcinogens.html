Carcinogens of Oral Cancer

Aetiological agents, mechanisms and prevention in lip, oral cavity and pharyngeal cancer. The neoplasia framework applied to the tissue you examine every day.

01

A multifactorial disease

Four pathways, all converging on the same epithelium.

Lip, oral cavity and pharyngeal cancers (LOPC) arise from cumulative carcinogen exposure across several independent and interacting pathways.

Pathway 1

Behavioural

Tobacco (smoked and smokeless), alcohol, betel quid.

Pathway 2

Infectious / biological

HPV 16/18, the oral microbiome.

Pathway 3

Environmental / occupational

UV radiation, dusts, chemical exposures.

Pathway 4

Host & lifestyle

Age, genetics, diet, oral hygiene.

The word that matters is cumulative

No single agent causes oral cancer. Exposures accumulate and interact — which is why the tobacco-plus-alcohol figure later in this lecture is so much larger than either alone, and why prevention has to address several habits rather than one.

Multistep carcinogenesis, applied

This is L7B's model in a specific tissue. Each exposure contributes initiating or promoting events; the epithelium accumulates the alterations; and the dysplasia grading from L7A is how we measure how far along that path a given lesion has travelled.

02

Tobacco smoking

The dominant carcinogen, and how it does the damage.

  • Cigarette smoke contains 60+ carcinogens; tobacco-specific nitrosamines are the most significant
  • Carcinogens form DNA adducts and drive p53 overexpression in premalignant and malignant oral epithelium

Key carcinogen classes

Tobacco-specific nitrosamines Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons Aromatic amines
DNA adducts → p53

An adduct is a carcinogen chemically bound to DNA, distorting it. If the adduct is not repaired before replication, it becomes a permanent mutation. p53 — the guardian from L7B — responds to that damage, and its overexpression in premalignant epithelium is a marker that the damage response is engaged. Once p53 itself is mutated, the brake is gone.

Dose, duration and mortality

Current smokers — overall risk3.58×
   oral cavity3.39×
   pharyngeal4.24×
Mortality — current smokers7.86×
Mortality — former smokers1.61× — still elevated
Effect of 7 years of smokingRoughly doubles risk (RR 2.01)
Duration matters more than intensity

Risk rises disproportionately with intensity, not linearly — but duration matters most. Just 7 years of smoking roughly doubles risk. For counselling, that means years smoked is the more important number to ask about, and stopping earlier matters more than cutting down.

03

Smokeless tobacco and betel quid

Not a safer alternative.

Form 1

Smokeless tobacco

  • Naswar, gutka, khaini, zarda, toombak, shammah
  • Nicotine content varies 0.8–76 mg/g
  • Contains PAHs, nitrosamines and radioactive elements
Form 2

Betel quid / areca nut

  • Areca nut + slaked lime ± tobacco, wrapped in betel leaf
  • Risk is independent of added tobacco — the areca nut itself is carcinogenic
Two points that get missed

Areca nut carries risk independent of tobacco. A patient who chews quid without tobacco is not safe, and saying so is a common error.

Nicotine content varies nearly hundredfold across smokeless products, so “smokeless tobacco” is not one exposure — ask which product.

Why the dentist sees this first

These products are held in the buccal sulcus for long periods, so the carcinogen sits in direct, prolonged contact with a fixed area of mucosa. The resulting lesions are therefore site-specific and visible on routine examination.

04

Alcohol

Carcinogen and cofactor — and the synergy is the point.

  1. Ethanol becomes acetaldehyde
    Via alcohol dehydrogenase. Acetaldehyde is a known carcinogen.
  2. Acetaldehyde acts as a tumour promoter
    Consistent with the promotion stage from L7B.
  3. Clearance varies genetically
    ~44% of Japanese carry an inactive ALDH2 variant, prolonging exposure to acetaldehyde.
Synergy, not addition

Combined tobacco + alcohol use raises oral cancer risk roughly 30-fold — far more than adding the two separate risks. Alcohol acts as a solvent, increasing mucosal permeability to tobacco carcinogens, while also generating its own. The two habits multiply rather than sum.

A host factor you can name

The ALDH2 variant is a concrete example of L7A's “constitutional factors” and L7B's host susceptibility: identical alcohol intake produces different acetaldehyde exposure depending on an inherited enzyme variant.

Why is combined smoking and drinking so much worse than either alone?

Because the effect is synergistic, not additive — roughly 30-fold combined. Alcohol contributes its own carcinogen (acetaldehyde, produced by alcohol dehydrogenase and acting as a tumour promoter) and also increases mucosal permeability, so more tobacco carcinogen reaches the epithelium. Genetic variation compounds it: an inactive ALDH2 variant prolongs acetaldehyde exposure.

05

E-cigarettes

An emerging exposure, assessed against Bradford Hill.

Assessed against the Bradford Hill criteria of causation (Raj et al., 2020):

FindingDetail
Lower toxicant loadSubstantially fewer carcinogens than combustible smoke — but not zero
DNA strand breaksOccur in oral cell lines regardless of nicotine content
Gene dysregulationReported in exposed oral tissue
“Fewer carcinogens” is not “safe”

The two findings to hold together: the toxicant load is lower than combustible tobacco, and DNA strand breaks occur regardless of nicotine content — so the damage is not attributable to nicotine alone. Long-term outcome data do not yet exist, which is exactly why the lecture frames this against causation criteria rather than asserting a conclusion.

06

HPV

A viral carcinogen — and the only one with a vaccine.

  • HPV-16 (then HPV-18) is the dominant oncogenic type in the head and neck
  • Viral E6 inhibits p53; E7 binds and inactivates pRb — disabling two key tumour-suppressor checkpoints
  • Tonsil and base-of-tongue tissue are preferentially infected
Prevention is unique here

HPV is the only major carcinogen in this lecture with a preventive vaccine. Every other agent is addressed by avoidance or cessation; this one can be immunised against.

Three lectures meeting

L7B gave the mechanism (E6/E7 as viral oncogenes disabling p53 and pRb). Lecture 6 gave the epidemiology (40–80% of oropharyngeal cancers HPV-positive, 90% of those HPV-16, base of tongue and tonsils, little role in the oral cavity proper). This lecture puts them together as a preventable exposure.

07

Environmental and occupational carcinogens

UV, dusts and chemicals.

Physical

UV radiation

Principal cause of lower lip cancer; higher risk in fishermen, farmers and outdoor workers.

Particulate

Occupational dusts

Wood, cement, coal and asbestos dust — wood dust raises oropharyngeal risk.

Chemical

Chemical exposures

Formaldehyde, chromium (leather dust) and metal particles linked to increased risk.

Lower lip is a sun cancer

UV is the principal cause of lower lip cancer — which is why lip lesions follow a different risk profile from intra-oral ones, why the lower lip is affected rather than the upper (sun angle), and why sun protection appears in the prevention list.

Ask about work

Occupation is easy to omit from a dental history but appears twice here — outdoor work for lip cancer, and wood, cement, coal, asbestos, formaldehyde and chromium exposure for oropharyngeal risk. It is a single question that changes a patient's risk category.

08

Diet, microbiome and host susceptibility

Why identical exposures give different outcomes.

Modifiable

Diet & the oral microbiome

High red/processed meat and low fruit and vegetable intake raises risk; Mediterranean-pattern diets are protective. The oral microbiome contributes as a biological pathway.

Inherited

Genetic & familial susceptibility

Polymorphisms in P450 / xenobiotic enzymes affect carcinogen metabolism and DNA repair capacity.

The host decides what an exposure costs

Carcinogens must be metabolically activated and the resulting damage repaired. P450 polymorphisms change the first; DNA repair capacity changes the second. Two people with identical smoking histories can therefore carry quite different risk — which is L7B's genomic-instability theme at population scale.

Epigenetics and socioeconomic drivers

  • Environmental, genetic and epigenetic inputs, plus ageing, converge on epigenetic regulation
  • Mechanisms: DNA methylation, histone modification, and post-transcriptional gene silencing by microRNAs
  • In low- and middle-income settings, risk habits, poor oral health literacy, low education and limited awareness compound biological risk
The same three epigenetic mechanisms as L7B

Methylation, the histone code, and microRNA silencing — identical to the list in Neoplasia Part 2. Here they are the point at which lifestyle, environment and ageing all converge on gene expression.

09

Combined risk, and reversing it

The numbers worth carrying, including the good news.

FactorEffect
Heavy smoking + heavy alcohol~30× combined, vs neither habit
Poor oral hygieneRelative risk for smokers reaches 6.44 — it lets tobacco carcinogens linger in the mouth, intensifying exposure
Geography (Oceania)Well above the global average
HPV-positive smokersRelative risk 3.28 vs 1.35
Oral hygiene is a carcinogen-exposure variable

This is the most directly actionable finding in the lecture for a dentist. Poor oral hygiene lets tobacco carcinogens linger in the mouth, intensifying exposure — so hygiene advice is not only about caries and periodontal disease. It reduces the contact time between carcinogen and epithelium.

Quitting works

Excess risk falls within years of stopping and approaches baseline by about 20 years smoke-free. Mortality in former smokers (1.61×) is far below current smokers (7.86×) though still above never-smokers. That is a concrete, encouraging number to give a patient who thinks the damage is already done.

10

Prevention

Three tiers, each targeting a different point.

TierMeasures
PrimaryTobacco/alcohol cessation, HPV vaccination, sun protection, dietary modification
SecondaryRoutine dental screening, visual/tactile exam, biomarkers, chemoprevention in leukoplakia
TertiaryStructured follow-up, rehabilitation, and management of field change
Secondary prevention is the dental role

Routine dental screening with visual and tactile examination is listed as a secondary prevention measure. Every recall appointment is an opportunistic screening for a cancer whose survival depends heavily on stage at detection — and the patient is already in the chair.

Field change

The tertiary tier names field change: the whole exposed epithelium has been subjected to the carcinogen, not just the visible lesion. Excising one tumour does not remove the risk from surrounding mucosa, which is why structured follow-up is required and why second primaries are common.

Key takeaways

  1. Tobacco is dominant
    Risk scales with both intensity and duration of use.
  2. Alcohol multiplies it
    Combined smoking and heavy alcohol raises risk roughly 30-fold — synergistic, not additive.
  3. HPV is uniquely preventable
    The only major carcinogen here with a vaccine.
  4. Quitting works
    Excess risk falls within years and approaches baseline by ~20 years smoke-free.
Why does poor oral hygiene raise oral cancer risk in a smoker, and what does that mean for your advice?

Because it lets tobacco carcinogens linger in the mouth, intensifying exposure — relative risk for smokers reaches 6.44. Contact time between carcinogen and epithelium is itself a risk variable. Practically, hygiene instruction for a smoker is carcinogen-exposure reduction as well as caries and periodontal prevention, and it is worth framing it that way to the patient alongside cessation advice.

Summary and glossary

The carcinogens lecture on one screen.

The through-line

  1. Cumulative, not single
    Four pathways — behavioural, infectious, environmental, host — interacting.
  2. Tobacco dominates
    60+ carcinogens, nitrosamines chief; DNA adducts and p53 overexpression; duration matters most.
  3. Smokeless forms are not safe
    Areca nut carries risk independent of added tobacco.
  4. Alcohol multiplies tobacco
    Acetaldehyde as promoter; ~30× combined; ALDH2 variation.
  5. HPV disables two checkpoints
    E6 on p53, E7 on pRb — and is vaccine preventable.
  6. UV causes lower lip cancer
    Outdoor occupations at higher risk.
  7. Hygiene changes exposure
    Poor hygiene lets carcinogens linger; RR 6.44 in smokers.
  8. Risk is reversible
    Approaching baseline by ~20 years smoke-free.

Glossary

LOPC
Lip, oral cavity and pharyngeal cancer.
DNA adduct
A carcinogen chemically bound to DNA; becomes a permanent mutation if unrepaired before replication.
Tobacco-specific nitrosamines
The most significant carcinogen class in cigarette smoke.
Betel quid
Areca nut + slaked lime ± tobacco, wrapped in betel leaf; carcinogenic independent of tobacco.
Acetaldehyde
Carcinogenic metabolite of ethanol produced by alcohol dehydrogenase; a tumour promoter.
ALDH2
Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2; an inactive variant carried by ~44% of Japanese prolongs acetaldehyde exposure.
Synergistic
Combined effect greater than the sum of the separate effects — as with tobacco and alcohol.
Bradford Hill criteria
A framework for assessing whether an association is causal.
E6 / E7
HPV oncogenes; E6 inhibits p53, E7 binds and inactivates pRb.
Xenobiotic enzymes
Enzymes such as the P450 family that metabolise foreign compounds, including carcinogens.
Field change
Carcinogen exposure of an entire epithelial field, so risk persists in mucosa surrounding an excised tumour.
Chemoprevention
Use of agents to prevent progression of a premalignant lesion such as leukoplakia.
