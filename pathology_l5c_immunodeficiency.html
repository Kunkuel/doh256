Immunodeficiency

Immunopathology Part 3. Parts 1 and 2 covered an immune system doing too much. This one covers it doing too little — the primary defects, and the secondary immunodeficiency you are most likely to see first in the mouth.

01

Immune deficiency: the two categories

Defects in the immune system — born with, or acquired.

Primary

Intrinsic defect

An intrinsic defect in the immune system itself.

Secondary

Consequence of something else

Due to another underlying condition — infection, malignancy, drugs, malnutrition.

How they present — the four words

Patients usually present with infections that are severe, persistent, unusual and recurrent. Any one of those on its own may mean nothing; together they are the signal that the immune system, not the organism, is the problem.

You have already met secondary immunodeficiency

Part 2's transplant patient on immunosuppressants is a secondary immunodeficiency — and showed the consequences: CMV, opportunistic fungal infection, and a 40× malignancy rate. This section covers the primary defects, and then the most important secondary cause worldwide.

02

The four primary categories

Named by which arm of the immune system has failed.

1

Primary antibody deficiency

The humoral arm — B cells and immunoglobulin.

2

Primary cell-mediated defects

The T cell arm.

3

Primary defects in phagocyte function

Neutrophils and macrophages — covered in detail next.

4

Primary complement deficiency

The complement cascade.

Mapping back to Lecture 3B

These four categories are simply the four effector arms of the immune system you built in Lecture 3B, each with its own failure mode. Knowing which arm has failed predicts which organisms cause trouble: antibody and complement defects favour encapsulated bacteria, T cell defects favour viruses and fungi, and phagocyte defects favour staphylococci and fungi.

03

Primary defects in phagocyte function

Neutropenia or neutrophil dysfunction — and a distinctive clinical picture.

The defect is either neutropenia (too few) or neutrophil dysfunction (enough, but not working).

Clinical features of the resulting disease

  1. Recurrent and persistent infections
    The general marker of immunodeficiency.
  2. Severe infection but minimal clinical features
    Because the clinical features of infection are the neutrophil response. Without neutrophils there is little pus, little redness and little swelling — the infection is severe but looks unimpressive.
  3. Poorly responsive to antibiotics
    Antibiotics slow organisms; neutrophils remove them. Without the second half, the first is not enough.
  4. Commonly staphylococcal
    The characteristic organism.
  5. Involve skin and mucous membranes
    The barrier surfaces.
  6. Complicated by suppurative lymphadenopathy
    Infection tracking to the draining nodes.
The trap in point 2

Severe infection but minimal clinical features. This is the most dangerous thing about phagocyte defects: the usual signs that tell you an infection is serious are produced by the very cells that are missing. A neutropenic patient with a quiet-looking swelling may be critically ill.

04

Chronic granulomatous disease

One enzyme fails, and phagocytes can no longer kill.

The defect

Failure to produce high concentrations of toxic oxygen radicals during the respiratory burst accompanying phagocyte activation, because of failure to produce a functional NADPH oxidase enzyme.

The phagocyte can still find and engulf the organism — it simply cannot kill it. Bacteria survive inside the cell, and the immune system walls them off instead, which is where the disease gets its name.

Presentation

Typically presents in the first 3 months of life as severe skin sepsis caused by Staphylococcus aureus, or fungal infections with Candida albicans or Aspergillus fumigatus.

Complications

Regional lymphadenopathy Hepatosplenomegaly Hepatic abscesses Osteomyelitis

Affected organs show multiple abscesses and non-caseating giant cell granulomas.

Why granulomas, and why non-caseating

Lecture 3B defined a granuloma as the response to an agent the body cannot destroy. Here the agent is not inherently indestructible — the phagocyte is defective, so the organism persists anyway and the same walling-off response follows. The granulomas are non-caseating, which distinguishes them from the caseating granulomas of tuberculosis.

Why does a failure of NADPH oxidase produce granulomas?

Because NADPH oxidase generates the toxic oxygen radicals of the respiratory burst that phagocytes use to kill ingested organisms. Without it, organisms are engulfed but survive inside the phagocyte. Persistent, undestroyable organisms are exactly the stimulus for granuloma formation — the body walls off what it cannot eliminate. In CGD these are non-caseating giant cell granulomas, alongside multiple abscesses.

05

HIV and AIDS: the scale of it

The most important secondary immunodeficiency.

Acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) is caused by the retroviruses human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) types 1 and 2.

HIV-positive people (end of 2015)25,313
Unaware of their status10%
New diagnoses made late29%
Why those two percentages matter to you

10% unaware and 29% diagnosed late together mean that a proportion of patients in your chair have undiagnosed HIV. That is the argument both for universal standard precautions and for taking oral manifestations seriously — the mouth is frequently where undiagnosed infection first shows.

06

Transmission

Routes, and the number for needlestick.

  1. Sexual intercourse
    The dominant route worldwide.
  2. Sharing contaminated needles
    Intravenous drug use; also therapeutic procedures through re-use of contaminated equipment.
  3. Receipt of infected material
    Blood, blood products, donated organs, semen.
  4. Occupational exposure
    Healthcare workers, via needle-stick injuries or blood splashes — 0.3% seroconversion.
  5. Vertical transmission
    Almost all new childhood infections are mother-to-child — before or during childbirth, or through breastfeeding.
0.3%

The seroconversion rate after a needle-stick injury from an HIV-positive source is 0.3%. Low, but not zero, and it is the number quoted in occupational exposure protocols. Report every sharps injury — post-exposure prophylaxis is time-critical.

07

Pathogenesis

How the virus gets in, and why it cannot be cleared.

  1. Entry via gp120
    HIV enters susceptible cells by binding the viral envelope glycoprotein gp120 to mainly the CD4 molecule — that is, helper T cells.
  2. Co-receptors
    CXCR4 and CCR5 chemokine receptors — found on macrophages, glial cells of the central nervous system, and epithelial cells of the gut.
  3. Extraordinary replication and mutation
    HIV replicates at 109–1010 new virions per day, producing up to 108 new mutants per day.
Why destroying CD4+ cells is catastrophic

Lecture 3B established the helper T cell as the coordinator — it licenses B cells to make antibody and activates macrophages. Removing it does not disable one arm of immunity; it disables the thing that directs all of them. That is why AIDS produces such a broad range of opportunistic infections rather than a single characteristic one.

08

The clinical spectrum: WHO stages

Four stages, from a glandular-fever-like illness to AIDS.

StageFeatures
WHO Stage 1Transient, acute glandular fever-like illness. Occurs in 10–20% of patients within a few weeks of infection, and precedes seroconversion
WHO Stage 2Asymptomatic
WHO Stage 3Asymptomatic persistent generalised lymphadenopathy — enlarged nodes in 2 or more extra-inguinal sites persisting for at least 3 months
WHO Stage 4AIDS-related complex, neurological disease, opportunistic infections and tumours — below
Stage 1 precedes seroconversion

The initial illness happens before antibodies appear. A patient tested during this window may be antibody-negative while highly infectious. This is the single most important point about stage 1 — a negative test does not exclude very recent infection.

Stage 4 in detail

AIDS-related complex (in some seropositive people):

Unexplained lymphadenopathy Diarrhoea Night sweats Oral candidiasis Weight loss

HIV is neurotropic — localising selectively in nervous tissue:

  • At seroconversion: acute septic meningitis, encephalopathy, myelopathy and neuropathy.
  • Later: chronic meningitis, cerebral lymphoma, encephalopathy and dementia — in 70% of AIDS patients.

Tumours — both virally driven

Tumour 1

Kaposi’s sarcoma

Caused by Human Herpesvirus 8.

Tumour 2

Non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma

Caused by Epstein–Barr virus.

The pattern behind both

Both AIDS-defining tumours are caused by viruses — HHV-8 and EBV. A healthy immune system keeps these oncogenic viruses in check; remove immune surveillance and the tumours emerge. This is the same principle as the 40× malignancy rate in transplant patients from Part 2, and the same two tumour types appear in both settings.

Kaposi's sarcoma in the mouth

Kaposi's sarcoma has a characteristic oral presentation, classically on the palate and gingiva, as purple macules progressing to nodules. In an undiagnosed patient it may be the presenting sign of AIDS.

10

Oral manifestations

Why the dentist is often the first to know.

The lecture devotes several slides to oral disease in HIV, and for good reason: with 10% of HIV-positive people unaware of their status and 29% diagnosed late, the mouth is frequently where the immunodeficiency first becomes visible.

Fungal

Erythematous candidosis

Oral candidosis is also listed among the AIDS-related complex features.

Viral (EBV)

Hairy leukoplakia

A white lesion, classically on the lateral tongue, that does not rub off.

Viral (HSV)

Herpes simplex infection

More severe, more persistent and more extensive than in an immunocompetent patient.

Bacterial

Necrotising ulcerative periodontitis

Rapid, painful destruction of periodontal tissues.

The four to recognise

Erythematous candidosis, hairy leukoplakia, herpes simplex infection and necrotising ulcerative periodontitis. Encountering these together, or any of them in an unexpectedly severe form in a young patient, warrants thinking about underlying immunodeficiency.

Read them as the categories from earlier

Fungal, viral, viral, bacterial — these are the opportunistic infections of the previous section, presenting in the tissue you examine every day. The same loss of CD4+ coordination that permits Pneumocystis in the lung permits Candida in the mouth.

A young patient presents with severe necrotising ulcerative periodontitis and a white lesion on the lateral tongue that does not rub off. What should you be thinking?

Both are recognised oral manifestations of HIV infection — necrotising ulcerative periodontitis, and hairy leukoplakia (EBV-driven, classically on the lateral tongue, and not removable by rubbing, which distinguishes it from pseudomembranous candidosis). Given that 10% of HIV-positive people are unaware of their status and 29% of diagnoses are made late, this combination in a young patient warrants sensitive discussion and referral for testing.

Management

Suppression, not cure — and why.

Treatment is a combination of different therapeutic drugs, called antiretroviral therapy (ART) or highly-active antiretroviral therapy (HAART).

Why a vaccine has not worked

Obstacle 1

A poor immunogen

The HIV envelope is fragile and a poor immunogen.

Obstacle 2

No stable target

High mutation rate makes it difficult to find a stable epitope to vaccinate against.

Where the drugs act

ClassAction
Entry inhibitorsBind CCR5 — blocking the co-receptor
Fusion inhibitorsBlock HIV–cell membrane fusion
Reverse transcriptase inhibitorsInhibit viral replication
Protease inhibitorsPrevent assembly of new virions
Why combination therapy

With up to 108 new mutants per day, resistance to any single drug arises almost immediately. Attacking four different points in the life cycle at once means a virion would need several simultaneous mutations to escape — which is why HAART works where monotherapy failed.

Why is HIV treated with a combination of drugs rather than a single agent?

Because HIV replicates at 109–1010 virions per day, generating up to 108 new mutants per day. That mutation rate means resistance to any single agent emerges rapidly. Combination therapy blocks several separate stages — entry (CCR5), fusion, reverse transcription and viral assembly — so escape would require multiple simultaneous mutations.

12

Summary and glossary

Part 3, and the end of Lecture 5.

The through-line

  1. Primary is intrinsic, secondary is consequential
    Both present with severe, persistent, unusual and recurrent infections.
  2. Four primary categories
    Antibody, cell-mediated, phagocyte, complement — the four arms of the immune system.
  3. Phagocyte defects hide themselves
    Severe infection with minimal clinical features, because the features are the neutrophil response.
  4. CGD is a killing defect, not a finding defect
    No NADPH oxidase, no respiratory burst — hence non-caseating granulomas.
  5. HIV targets the coordinator
    gp120 to CD4, with CXCR4/CCR5 co-receptors.
  6. Its mutation rate defeats both immunity and monotherapy
    Hence no vaccine, and combination HAART.
  7. The tumours are viral
    Kaposi's sarcoma (HHV-8) and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (EBV) — immune surveillance removed.
  8. The mouth shows it early
    Candidosis, hairy leukoplakia, HSV, necrotising ulcerative periodontitis.

Glossary

Primary immunodeficiency
An intrinsic defect in the immune system.
Secondary immunodeficiency
Immunodeficiency due to another underlying condition.
Neutropenia
Deficiency of neutrophils.
Respiratory burst
Production of toxic oxygen radicals by activated phagocytes to kill ingested organisms.
NADPH oxidase
The enzyme generating the respiratory burst; non-functional in chronic granulomatous disease.
Chronic granulomatous disease
Primary phagocyte defect causing severe staphylococcal and fungal infection, abscesses and non-caseating giant cell granulomas.
Suppurative lymphadenopathy
Pus-forming enlargement of draining lymph nodes.
HIV
Human immunodeficiency virus, types 1 and 2; a retrovirus.
gp120
The viral envelope glycoprotein binding the CD4 molecule.
CXCR4 / CCR5
Chemokine co-receptors used by HIV for cell entry.
Seroconversion
The appearance of detectable antibody; the WHO Stage 1 illness precedes it.
Persistent generalised lymphadenopathy
Enlarged nodes at 2 or more extra-inguinal sites for at least 3 months (WHO Stage 3).
Neurotropic
Localising selectively in nervous tissue.
AIDS-related complex
Lymphadenopathy, diarrhoea, night sweats, oral candidiasis and weight loss.
Kaposi’s sarcoma
AIDS-defining tumour caused by Human Herpesvirus 8.
Hairy leukoplakia
EBV-associated white lesion, classically of the lateral tongue, that does not rub off.
ART / HAART
Antiretroviral therapy; combination therapy blocking several stages of the viral life cycle.
