01

Autoimmunity defined

A response, a disease — and the gap between them.

Two definitions, kept apart

Autoimmunity: an immune response against a self (auto)-antigen or antigens.

Autoimmune disease: tissue damage or disturbed physiological function resulting from an autoimmune response.

The distinction that gets examined

Autoimmune responses can occur without resulting disease. Finding an autoantibody does not mean the patient has an autoimmune disease — it means they have an autoimmune response. This is exactly why a positive ANA alone cannot diagnose SLE, as Part 1 covered.

02

Proving that autoimmunity caused the disease

Four steps — an association is not a cause.

The lecture asks how we know autoimmunity causes an autoimmune disease, rather than merely accompanying it. The answer is a chain of four requirements:

  1. Demonstrate immunological reactivity to a self-antigen
    Show the response exists.
  2. Characterise or isolate the inciting autoantigen
    Identify precisely what is being attacked.
  3. Induce reactivity in an experimental animal
    By immunisation against the same antigen.
  4. Show the same pathological changes in that animal
    Changes similar or identical to those found in human disease, in the appropriate organs or tissues of an actively sensitised animal.
Why this structure is familiar

This is Koch's postulates applied to autoimmunity: find the agent, isolate it, reproduce the disease with it, recover the same disease. The logic of proving causation is the same whether the agent is a microbe or the patient's own immune response.

03

Patterns of autoimmune disease

Organ-specific or not — and the answer follows the antigen.

Pattern 1

Organ-specific

Single organ affected, commonly an endocrine gland. The autoantibody may target a cell surface or an intracellular molecule.

Pattern 2

Non-organ-specific

Multiple organs affected, because the self-antigen is widely distributed in the body — particularly intracellular molecules involved in transcription and translation of the genetic code.

SLE affects skin, joints, kidneys, heart and blood. What does that tell you about its target antigen?

That it is widely distributed — SLE is non-organ-specific. Its autoantigens are intracellular molecules involved in transcription and translation (nuclear antigens, dsDNA, Sm), which are present in essentially every nucleated cell. A disease cannot be confined to one organ if its target is in all of them.

Ankylosing spondylitis is the named exception to the female predominance. If a question asks which autoimmune disease is more common in men, that is the answer.

05

Aetiology: genetic factors

Usually many genes — and MHC above all.

Autoimmunity usually involves multiple genes, but some single-gene defects involve defects in apoptosis. The strongest and best characterised associations involve alleles of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC).

Why MHC, and why apoptosis

Both make sense mechanistically. MHC determines which peptides get presented to T cells, so an MHC allele that happens to present a self-peptide well is a direct route to autoimmunity. Apoptosis is how self-reactive lymphocytes are normally deleted — if that deletion fails, the cells survive. One controls what is shown; the other controls who is allowed to respond.

Connecting to Part 1

SLE gave the concrete numbers: over 100 genetic loci, each contributing a small increased risk, with 25% concordance in monozygotic twins. That is the signature of polygenic susceptibility — genes load the gun, but three quarters of identical twin pairs are discordant, so something environmental pulls the trigger.

06

Aetiology: environmental factors

Hormones, infection, drugs, ultraviolet radiation.

Hormones

  • Females more likely to be affected than males.
  • The peak age of onset is within the reproductive years — implicating oestrogens.
  • Removal of the ovaries in animal models of SLE inhibits onset — the experimental evidence that the association is causal.

Infection

Molecular mimicry

A microbial antigen sufficiently resembles a self antigen that the response raised against one also attacks the other. Infection of an organ may also cause up-regulation of co-stimulatory molecules and altered patterns of antigen breakdown and presentation.

Drugs

  • A drug–self-molecule complex has a structural similarity to self and hence allows bypass of peripheral tolerance.
  • Some drugs bind directly to the peptide-containing groove in MHC molecules, with a direct capacity to induce abnormal T-cell responses.
  • HLA-DR2 is associated with penicillamine-induced myasthenia gravis, whereas DR3 is associated with nephritis.
  • Genetic variation in drug metabolism matters: slow acetylators are prone to SLE.

Ultraviolet radiation

UV radiation can modify self-antigens, enhancing their immunogenicity, or lead to apoptotic death of cells within skin. Apoptosis is associated with cell surface expression of autoantigens usually found only within cells — which are then able to bind related autoantibodies and trigger tissue damage.

Why photosensitivity is a lupus feature

This is the mechanism behind it. UV drives keratinocyte apoptosis, and apoptosis externalises normally intracellular nuclear antigens onto the cell surface, where circulating anti-nuclear antibodies can reach them. In a non-organ-specific disease whose antigens are intracellular, sunlight is effectively a way of putting the target on display.

Why does sun exposure trigger a lupus flare?

UV radiation causes apoptotic death of skin cells, and apoptosis is associated with cell surface expression of autoantigens that are normally only found inside cells — exactly the intracellular nuclear antigens SLE targets. Once displayed on the surface they can bind circulating autoantibodies and trigger tissue damage. UV can also modify self-antigens directly, enhancing their immunogenicity.

07

Mechanisms of tissue damage

The same effector arms as Part 1, pointed inwards.

Route 1

Antibody or immune complexes

Types II and III from Part 1, with a self antigen.

Route 2

CD4+ T-cell-mediated

Activation of macrophages or cytotoxic T cells — type IV.

Route 3

A combination

Most real diseases use more than one.

Autoantibodies can bind to the functional sites of self-antigens such as hormone receptors, neurotransmitter receptors and plasma proteins — the receptor-interference mechanism from type II, which is how Graves disease and myasthenia gravis work.

There is no new machinery here

Autoimmunity does not have its own effector mechanism. It uses the four hypersensitivity types from Part 1; the only difference is that the antigen is self. If you can classify a hypersensitivity reaction, you can classify an autoimmune disease.

08

Sjögren syndrome

The autoimmune disease you will diagnose from the dental chair.

CD4+ T cell reactions against unknown antigens in the ductal epithelial cells of the exocrine glands. Most patients with primary Sjögren syndrome have autoantibodies to the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) antigens SS-A (Ro) and SS-B (La).

Two antibodies, two names each

SS-A = Ro and SS-B = La. Both names appear in reports and in questions, so learn the pairs together.

Why this one is yours

The target is the ductal epithelium of the exocrine glands — which means the salivary glands. Destruction of salivary tissue produces xerostomia, and xerostomia produces rampant cervical caries, candidosis and difficulty wearing dentures. The dentist frequently sees the consequences before the diagnosis is made.

Mechanism check

Note that Sjögren is a CD4+ T cell reaction — a type IV mechanism — even though it also produces characteristic autoantibodies. The antibodies are the diagnostic marker; the T cells do the damage. That combination is the “combination of mechanisms” route from the previous section.

09

Treatment of autoimmune disease

Two options, and neither is a cure.

Option 1

Replacement of organ function

Accept the destruction and replace what was lost — thyroxine in Hashimoto's, insulin in type 1 diabetes, saliva substitutes in Sjögren.

Option 2

Immune suppression

Damp down the response itself, at the cost of suppressing wanted immunity along with unwanted.

The cost of option 2

Immunosuppression is the same intervention used to keep a transplant alive, and it carries the same complications — infection and malignancy. Those are set out in detail later in this lecture, and they apply just as much to the patient on immunosuppressants for rheumatoid arthritis as to the transplant recipient.

Transplantation: the vocabulary

Five definitions that the rest of the section depends on.

Haplotype
A group of genes within an organism that was inherited together from a single parent.
Allele
A variant form of a gene.
Allogeneic
Between genetically dissimilar individuals.
Autologous
One part of one’s body to another.
Xenogeneic
Across a species barrier (e.g. pigs to humans).
Reading the prefixes

Auto- self, allo- another of the same species, xeno- another species. The rejection risk rises in exactly that order, because the degree of genetic difference does.

11

MHC and HLA

The molecules that make a graft foreign.

  • Cell surface glycoproteins of two basic types: class I and class II.
  • Human MHC antigens are also known as human leucocyte antigens (HLAs).
  • Extensive genetic polymorphism and co-dominant expression — which is precisely why matching two unrelated people is so difficult.
ClassFound on
MHC IMost nucleated cells
MHC IIAntigen-presenting cells
MHC IIIC2 and C4 complement components
MHC III is the odd one out

Classes I and II are cell-surface presenting molecules. Class III is not a presenting molecule at all — it encodes complement components C2 and C4. It is grouped with them because the genes sit in the same region of chromosome 6, not because it does the same job.

From Lecture 3B

You already know what I and II do: class I presents to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (every nucleated cell can be inspected and killed), and class II presents to CD4+ helper T cells (only professional APCs). Graft rejection uses both, in that order.

12

How a graft is rejected

Two phases: recognition, then attack.

Phase 1

Afferent — initiation / sensitisation

Allorecognition: MHC molecules on the dendritic cells of the donor are recognised by the recipient’s CD4+ T cells. This can occur in the graft or in recipient lymphoid organs.

Phase 2

Effector

CD4+ T cells enter the graft and recruit effector cells using IL-2 and IFN-γ. The recruits are macrophages, CD8+ T cells, NK cells and B cells. Their targets are the endothelium and the parenchymal cells.

The endothelium is the target that matters

Attacking the graft's endothelium destroys its blood supply. That is why rejection presents as infarction and progressive ischaemic failure rather than as diffuse inflammation — and it is what the three rejection patterns in the next section all have in common.

13

Hyperacute, acute and chronic rejection

Three timeframes, three mechanisms, three outcomes.

The pattern to hold

Hyperacute is antibody that was already there — hence minutes. Acute is T cells that had to be primed — hence days to weeks, and hence reversible by immunosuppression. Chronic is not immunological at all, which is exactly why immunosuppression fails against it.

A transplanted kidney fails over several years despite full immunosuppression. Which rejection is this, and why does treatment not help?

Chronic rejection, occurring months to years after transplantation, with slowly progressive renal failure and hypertension. It is not thought to be immunologically mediated — so immunosuppressive therapy, which works by damping the immune response, has nothing to act on and does not reverse it.

Complications of immunosuppression

The price of keeping the graft.

ComplicationDetail
Susceptibility to infectionViral infections, mainly CMV; opportunistic fungal infections, e.g. Cryptococcus
Malignancy40× higher incidence — particularly Kaposi’s sarcoma and lymphoma
Acute myocardial infarctionDue to hypertension, hypertriglyceridaemia, or insulin-resistant diabetes aggravated by steroids
40× is not a typo

A forty-fold increase in malignancy is one of the starkest numbers in the unit. It demonstrates how much cancer the immune system is silently preventing in a healthy person — immune surveillance is invisible until it is removed.

Dental implications

An immunosuppressed patient is at higher risk of oral candidosis, herpetic and CMV infection, delayed healing after extraction, and oral malignancy. Both Kaposi's sarcoma and lymphoma have recognised oral presentations — and both reappear in Part 3 under HIV, for the same reason.

15

Bone marrow transplantation and GvHD

The graft rejects the host.

Haemopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Graft rejection is common, and the complications are: failure of engraftment, infection, and graft-versus-host disease (GvHD).

Graft-versus-host disease

The transplanted immune cells recognise the recipient as foreign and attack. In a solid organ graft the host attacks the graft; in HSCT the graft is an immune system, so the direction reverses.

Oral chronic GvHD

Mucosa

Oral mucosal disease

Erythema, lichenoid lesions, ulcerations and mucoceles.

Glands

Salivary gland dysfunction

With dry mouth.

Sclerosis

Oral sclerosis

With limited mouth opening.

Why the mouth is a key site

Chronic GvHD has a characteristic oral triad — lichenoid mucosal change, salivary hypofunction and sclerosis restricting opening. The dentist may be the first to recognise it, and mouth opening is easy to measure and track over time.

How does GvHD differ in direction from ordinary graft rejection, and why does that happen in bone marrow transplants specifically?

In ordinary rejection the host's immune system attacks the graft. In GvHD the graft attacks the host. This happens in haemopoietic stem cell transplantation because the tissue being transplanted is an immune system — the donor's immunocompetent cells recognise the recipient's tissues as foreign. In the mouth it produces lichenoid lesions and ulceration, salivary dysfunction with dry mouth, and sclerosis limiting mouth opening.

Summary and glossary

Part 2 on one screen.

The through-line

  1. Autoimmunity is a response; autoimmune disease is damage
    A response can exist without disease.
  2. The antigen's distribution sets the disease's distribution
    Organ-specific vs non-organ-specific.
  3. Genes load, environment triggers
    MHC alleles and apoptosis defects; hormones, infection, drugs, UV.
  4. No new effector mechanisms
    Autoimmune damage uses the Part 1 hypersensitivity types.
  5. Sjögren is the dental one
    CD4+ attack on exocrine ductal epithelium; SS-A (Ro) and SS-B (La).
  6. Rejection comes in three timeframes
    Antibody, then T cell, then non-immunological — only the middle one responds to treatment.
  7. Immunosuppression costs infection and cancer
    40× malignancy; CMV and fungal infection.
  8. In HSCT the direction reverses
    GvHD, with a recognisable oral triad.

Glossary

Autoimmunity
An immune response against a self-antigen or antigens.
Autoimmune disease
Tissue damage or disturbed physiological function resulting from an autoimmune response.
Organ-specific
Autoimmune disease affecting a single organ, commonly an endocrine gland.
Non-organ-specific
Autoimmune disease affecting multiple organs, targeting a widely distributed antigen.
Molecular mimicry
Structural similarity between a microbial and a self antigen, so a response to one attacks the other.
Peripheral tolerance
The mechanisms preventing self-reactive lymphocytes acting outside the thymus; bypassed by some drug–self complexes.
Slow acetylator
A drug-metabolism phenotype prone to drug-induced SLE.
SS-A (Ro) / SS-B (La)
Ribonucleoprotein autoantigens in primary Sjögren syndrome.
Haplotype
A group of genes inherited together from one parent.
Allele
A variant form of a gene.
Allogeneic
Between genetically dissimilar individuals of the same species.
Autologous
From one part of one’s own body to another.
Xenogeneic
Across a species barrier.
HLA
Human leucocyte antigen — the human MHC.
Allorecognition
Recognition of donor MHC on donor dendritic cells by recipient CD4+ T cells.
Hyperacute rejection
Minutes to hours; circulating cytotoxic antibody against donor MHC class I; the kidney must be removed.
Acute rejection
Days to weeks; CD8+ T cell infiltration; reversible with immunosuppression.
Chronic rejection
Months to years; not thought to be immunologically mediated; unresponsive to immunosuppression.
HSCT
Haemopoietic stem cell transplantation.
GvHD
Graft-versus-host disease — donor immune cells attacking recipient tissues.
