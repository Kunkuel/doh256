Acute Inflammation

Inflammation is the connective tissue’s response to injury — a reaction of blood vessels with accumulation of fluid and leukocytes. This lecture works through the vascular and cellular machinery that produces it, and where it can go next.

01

Vocabulary you need first

The lecture opens by testing terms from Lectures 1 and 2. Cover the right-hand column and recite them.

Pathology
The study of disease and its causes.
Aetiology
The cause of a disease.
Hypoxia
A condition in which tissues are deprived of an adequate supply of oxygen.
Homeostasis
The ability of a living organism to adjust its internal environment to maintain a stable equilibrium.
Necrosis
Unprogrammed cell death.
Apoptosis
Programmed cell death.

Tissue and injury terms

Parenchyma
The functional part of an organ, as opposed to supporting tissue — e.g. a neuron.
Stroma
The tissue structure of an organ that serves to support it — e.g. connective tissue.
Dystrophic calcification
Mineralisation of soft tissues occurring without a systemic mineral imbalance.
Caseous
Cheesy (in appearance) — the classic necrosis of tuberculosis.
Endothelium
A thin layer of flat epithelial cells that lines the lymph and blood vessels.
Infarct
An area of dead tissue caused by a loss of blood supply.
Why this matters

Parenchyma vs stroma is the distinction the whole of repair rests on. Parenchymal cells can sometimes regenerate; stroma is what repairs by laying down collagen. Get these two straight now and section 17 becomes easy.

Define dystrophic calcification, and say what makes it 'dystrophic'.

Mineralisation of soft tissue that occurs without any systemic mineral imbalance — calcium levels in the blood are normal. It is 'dystrophic' because the calcium deposits in tissue that is already damaged or dying, rather than because there is too much calcium in the body.

03

Why inflammation exists

The logic that makes everything else in this lecture follow.

  1. A stimulus injures cells
    An injurious stimulus can damage parenchymal or stromal cells.
  2. The same stimulus provokes the connective tissue
    That very same stimulus triggers a complex reaction in the surrounding connective tissue.
  3. That reaction is inflammation
    Inflammation is the connective tissue's response to injury.

What the response has to achieve

To resolve an injury, the body must:

  • Destroy, dilute or isolate the injurious agent
  • Heal or replace the damaged tissue
The goal

To rid the organism of both the initial cause of the injury and its consequences. Inflammation is therefore closely linked with repair — they are two halves of one process.

If it fails

Where inflammation is defective, infections never heal and wounds become permanent festering sores. Inflammation is unpleasant, but its absence is far worse.

Inflammation causes pain, swelling and loss of function. Why is it still described as protective?

Because its purpose is to destroy, dilute or isolate the injurious agent and to heal or replace damaged tissue. The signs we experience as unpleasant are side effects of the vascular and cellular machinery doing that job. Where the response is defective, infections never resolve and wounds persist as festering sores.

04

What inflammation is

A precise definition, and three properties worth memorising.

Definition

Inflammation is the connective tissue response to injury: a reaction of the blood vessels, with accumulation of fluid and leukocytes in the tissues. It involves many cells, vessels, and intra- and extracellular components. It is a protective response — but it can be harmful.

Three properties to remember

Property 1

Stereotyped

Fundamentally the same reaction regardless of the tissue type and the cause of injury.

Property 2

Overlapping

Consists of a group of responses that overlap and are inter-related — not a tidy sequence.

Property 3

Shared with immunity

Immune cells and inflammatory cells are the same cells, and the two responses may overlap.

Carry this forward

Property 3 is the bridge into Lecture 3B. Because the cells are shared, an immune response can trigger an inflammatory response — which is exactly what happens in many chronic inflammatory diseases.

05

The inflammatory cells

Leukocytes are the workforce. Know which cell does what, and which arrives first.

General facts about leukocytes

Job

Three functions

  • Defend against invasion by pathogens
  • Identify and destroy foreign/abnormal cells
  • Clean up debris
Behaviour

Seek out and destroy

Highly mobile — they travel to the site of injury rather than waiting for it to come to them.

Numbers

~7 million per mL

Of blood, on average. Two-thirds are neutrophils. Produced generally from the bone marrow.

Polymorphonuclear cells (granulocytes)

CellRole
NeutrophilsPhagocytotic specialists · first-line defenders · scavenge and clean up debris. Two-thirds of all circulating leukocytes.
EosinophilsNormally associated with allergic conditions; defence in parasitic infections (worms). Red staining on histology.
BasophilsThe least common and least understood. Similar to mast cells — they store histamine and heparin.

Mononuclear cells (agranulocytes)

CellRole
MonocytesPrecursor phagocytes. In tissue they enlarge and mature into macrophages. Can last for months to years — the amount digested determines the life span.
LymphocytesTwo types. B lymphocytes produce antibodies and prime antigens for destruction. T lymphocytes are involved in cell-mediated immunity (cancer cells, virus-invaded cells).
High yield

Neutrophil = acute. Macrophage and lymphocyte = chronic. Which cell dominates a histological section is how a pathologist dates the inflammation. This single fact links Lectures 3A and 3B.

A biopsy shows a dense infiltrate of neutrophils. Roughly how old is this process, and what would you expect if it were months old?

Neutrophil-dominant means acute — the process is recent (minutes to a couple of days). If it were months old you would expect a mononuclear infiltrate instead: macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells, together with fibroblasts laying down collagen.

06

Exudate, transudate, oedema and pus

Four fluid terms that are constantly confused. The difference between the first two is protein.

TermDefinitionExample
ExudateInflammatory extravascular fluid with a high protein content. Requires a significant alteration to the permeability of vessels.Inflammation
TransudateFluid with a low protein content — an ultrafiltrate of blood plasma. Due to hydrostatic imbalance across the endothelium, with normal vessel permeability.Left-sided cardiac failure
OedemaAn excess of fluid in the interstitial spaces — this can be either an exudate or a transudate.Either mechanism
PusA purulent exudate — inflammatory exudate rich in leukocytes (mainly neutrophils) and cellular debris.Abscess
Starling’s law

Normally the osmotic pressure in the blood almost exactly balances the hydrostatic pressure forcing fluid out. When blood pressure substantially exceeds osmotic pressure the balance tips and fluid accumulates — for example pulmonary oedema caused by increased left-sided heart failure.

A patient has pleural fluid with a low protein content. Is this inflammatory? What is the likely mechanism?

Low protein means it is a transudate, so it is not inflammatory. Vessel permeability is normal; the fluid is an ultrafiltrate of plasma driven out by a hydrostatic imbalance across the endothelium — classically cardiac failure. An inflammatory effusion would be an exudate, with high protein, because permeability itself is altered.

07

Acute vs chronic

Inflammation divides into two — but the boundary is blurred, not sharp.

 Acute inflammatory responseChronic inflammatory response
Timing of onsetThe immediate response to an injuryA delayed response
DurationLasts minutes to a maximum of 2 daysA prolonged response — weeks to months
Dominant cellsNeutrophilsLymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages
High yield

There is a temporal and cellular overlap between the two. They are not separate diseases but two ends of one continuum — a chronic lesion can have an acute exacerbation, and an acute process can slide into chronicity.

08

Starting and stopping

Control is exerted by mediators at both ends of the process.

Switching on

How does the body know to involve inflammation?

Mediators — either singly, in combination, or as a sequence — can stimulate and amplify the response. Sources include necrotic cells and mast cells.

Switching off

How does the body know how to stop?

When the injurious agent is removed and the mediators are either displaced or inhibited.

Link to chronic inflammation

Read the stop condition carefully: inflammation ends only when the agent is gone. If the body cannot remove or destroy the stimulus, the mediators keep being produced and the process cannot switch off — which is precisely the definition of chronic inflammation in Lecture 3B.

09

Acute inflammation: the 5 cardinal signs

Clinical signs and symptoms, each associated with an underlying cellular and tissue change.

Memory hook

The four classical Latin signs all end in -or: rubor, calor, dolor, tumor. Functio laesa was the fifth to be added and is the one most often forgotten in exams.

List all five cardinal signs with their English meanings and one mechanism each.

Rubor (redness) — vasodilation. Calor (heat) — increased blood flow. Dolor (pain) — prostaglandins and bradykinin. Tumor (swelling) — exudate in the interstitium. Functio laesa (loss of function) — the combined effect of pain and swelling.

10

What acute inflammation achieves

Three functional products of the response.

  1. Exudate formation
    An increased volume of extracellular fluid, which dilutes and flushes away toxins.
  2. Infiltrate formation
    Cells arrive at the site: neutrophils (polymorphonuclear leukocytes) and macrophages (histiocytes).
  3. Granulation tissue formation
    Lays down the scaffolding for healing and repair.
Note the design

Each product maps onto one of the goals from section 3: exudate dilutes the agent, infiltrate destroys it, and granulation tissue heals the damage.

The three tissue events

The rest of this lecture works through the machinery that delivers those three products:

A · Changes in vascular flow and calibre B · Changes in vascular permeability C · Leukocyte exudation
11

Tissue event A — vascular flow and calibre

Three changes, in strict order. The timing detail is examinable.

  1. Transient vasoconstriction of arterioles
    Lasts seconds to minutes only. Easy to overlook because it is so brief — but it is the first event.
  2. Vasodilation
    Initially involves the arterioles, then includes vessels which would otherwise be closed. Opens new capillary beds in the area and increases blood flow to the region.
    → This produces HEAT and ERYTHEMA (calor and rubor).
  3. Slowing of circulation: stasis
    Driven by increased permeability of the vessel walls → decreased plasma volume → increased red blood cell concentration, so the blood becomes viscous and slows.
    Timing: may not occur for 15–30 minutes with a mild stimulus, but within a few minutes after a severe injury.
Why does stasis have to happen before leukocytes can leave the bloodstream?

In normal flow, cells are carried in the centre of the vessel by the fast axial stream. Stasis slows the circulation and concentrates the blood, which allows white cells to move outward to the vessel margins — margination. Without slowing the stream, leukocytes would simply be swept past the site of injury.

12

Tissue event B — vascular permeability

How the endothelium becomes leaky, and the five ways it happens.

The principle

  • Occurs in the microcirculation
  • Usually a balance exists between the interstitial (extracellular) fluid and the plasma
  • During inflammation, the endothelium becomes leaky
  • There is an outflow of fluid from the vessels, containing blood cells and proteins
  • This fluid is an exudate
  • Oedema results from the accumulation of excess fluid in the interstitial tissues

Five mechanisms of vascular leakage

MechanismFeatures
Endothelial gapsMost common. Reversible and short-term — the immediate transient response.
Cytoskeletal reorganisationCytokine-induced. Produces reversible leakage.
Direct injuryUsually in necrotising injuries. Normally immediate leakage, lasting several hours — the immediate sustained response.
Leukocyte-dependent injuryLeukocytes may become active and damage the vessels themselves.
Leakage from new vesselsOccurs during repair, as new vessels are immature and inherently leaky.
Classic experiment

Increased permeability is concentrated in the post-capillary venules. This was demonstrated by injecting carbon particles into a rat and then administering histamine: the carbon labelled precisely where the vessels leaked. Endothelial cells become leaky either from direct endothelial cell injury or via chemical mediators.

High yield

Distinguish the immediate transient response (endothelial gaps; reversible, short-lived) from the immediate sustained response (direct injury; lasts several hours). Examiners like this pair because the names are similar but the mechanisms are not.

Name the five mechanisms of vascular leakage and say which is most common.

Endothelial gaps (most common; reversible, immediate transient response), cytoskeletal reorganisation (cytokine-induced, reversible), direct injury (necrotising injuries; immediate sustained, several hours), leukocyte-dependent injury, and leakage from new vessels during repair.

13

Tissue event C — leukocyte emigration

The accumulation of leukocytes is the major feature of inflammation. This sequence is the heart of the lecture.

Why leukocytes matter

Their functions at the site are to engulf and degrade bacteria, immune complexes, and cell debris. A defined sequence of events produces their accumulation.

The sequence in words

  1. Margination, or pavementing
    In normal blood flow, cells remain toward the centre of the vessel. During stasis, white blood cells move outward toward the vessel walls — the margins. Pavementing occurs when the entire wall appears to be covered by these white cells.
  2. Adhesion
    Attachment of the white cells to the endothelial surface. Blood then flows past without disturbing them. Involves a complex chemical reaction, mediated in part by inflammatory agents and chemicals.
  3. Diapedesis
    The process by which white cells move from the blood vessel into the interstitial fluid, using amoeboid-like movements.
  4. Chemotaxis
    The unidirectional migration of cells along a chemical gradient. This is not a random movement — it is directed.
  5. Phagocytosis and intracellular degradation
    Ingestion and destruction of the target (see section 14).
  6. Extracellular release of leukocyte products
    Which, as section 14 shows, can damage the host’s own tissue.
Memory hook

M-A-D-C-P: Margination → Adhesion → Diapedesis → Chemotaxis → Phagocytosis. Note the logic: you must reach the wall before you can stick to it, stick before you can cross it, and cross before you can follow a gradient.

Distinguish diapedesis from chemotaxis.

Diapedesis is the physical process of a white cell crossing the vessel wall into interstitial fluid, by amoeboid movement. Chemotaxis is what happens next: unidirectional migration along a chemical gradient toward the target. Diapedesis is about getting out; chemotaxis is about knowing where to go, and is directed rather than random.

14

Phagocytosis — and how it harms the host

'-phage' means to eat. But eating goes wrong in two specific, named ways.

Definition

Phagocytosis is the ingestion and digestion of cell debris and micro-organisms.

The four steps

  1. Recognition
    The phagocyte identifies the target as foreign or as debris.
  2. Adhesion
    The phagocyte binds to the target.
  3. Engulfment
    The membrane surrounds the target, forming a phagosome.
  4. Killing or degradation
    The phagosome fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome, in which the ingested material is destroyed.

Self-harm: when phagocytosis damages the host

Ideally a phagocyte surrounds its target to form a phagosome, which then fuses with a lysosome to destroy the target. However, sometimes the destructive lysosomal products are released into the extracellular space instead. There are two named ways this happens:

Failure mode 1

Regurgitation during feeding

The phagocytic vacuole is not completely closed, so lysosomal contents spill out into the surrounding tissue while the cell is still ingesting.

Failure mode 2

Frustrated phagocytosis

Cells attempt to digest non-digestible materials — for example flat surfaces that cannot be engulfed. Unable to close a vacuole, the cell discharges its enzymes externally.

Consequence

Both mechanisms cause cell and tissue damage, which further stimulates the inflammatory response — a self-amplifying loop. This is one important way in which a protective response becomes a harmful one.

A suture left in the tissues provokes ongoing inflammation. Which phagocytic failure mode explains this, and why does it perpetuate?

Frustrated phagocytosis. The suture is a non-digestible material with a surface that cannot be engulfed, so macrophages cannot close a phagocytic vacuole around it and instead release lysosomal enzymes into the extracellular space. That causes tissue damage, which further stimulates inflammation — and because the stimulus cannot be removed, the loop continues. (Lecture 3B lists sterile foreign bodies such as suture material as a cause of chronic inflammation arising de novo.)

Chemical mediators of inflammation

Learn these by the effect they produce, not as an alphabetical list.

EffectMediator(s)
VasodilationProstaglandins
Increased vascular permeabilityHistamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes
ChemotaxisComplement, leukotrienes
FeverProstaglandins
PainProstaglandins, bradykinin
Tissue damageNeutrophil and macrophage contents
Clinical link

Prostaglandins appear three times in that table — vasodilation, fever and pain. That is exactly why NSAIDs, which inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, are simultaneously anti-inflammatory, antipyretic and analgesic. One mechanism, three clinical effects.

Which single mediator class accounts for vasodilation, fever and pain — and what drug class targets it?

Prostaglandins, targeted by NSAIDs. This is why one drug can reduce swelling, bring down a temperature and relieve pain at the same time.

16

Sequelae — the four outcomes

Every episode of acute inflammation ends in one of four ways.

Resolution

  • A complete restoration of normal structure and function
  • Rarely occurs
  • Requires a minor injury
  • Requires labile cells — those able to regenerate

Suppuration

  • The formation of pus
  • Pus consists of neutrophils, cell debris, and bacteria (pyogenic bacteria)
  • An abscess is a localised collection of pus
  • Release of pus allows organisation and healing with scarring
Clinical link

The last point is the entire rationale for incision and drainage. An abscess is walled off from the blood supply, so neither immune cells nor antibiotics reach it reliably. Healing begins only once the pus is released.

Why is complete resolution rare, and what two conditions must be met for it?

Resolution requires that the injury be minor and that the tissue contains labile cells capable of regeneration. Most injuries are either too extensive or involve tissue whose cells cannot divide, so the defect is filled with connective tissue instead — repair with scarring rather than resolution.

17

Regeneration and repair

The most common outcome of acute inflammation. Note carefully that regeneration and repair are not synonyms.

Two different words

Regeneration — replacement by parenchymal cell division. The functional tissue itself is restored.
Repair — restoration by connective tissue. The defect is filled with collagen; function is not fully restored.

Both involve the restoration or replacement of tissue lost as a result of necrosis.

The steps back to normal

  1. Neutralise the injurious agent
    The cause must be dealt with first.
  2. Parenchymal cells regenerate / stromal cells repair
    The two compartments respond in their own ways.
  3. Neutralisation or decay of chemical mediators
    The signals that drove the response are removed.
  4. Return to normal vascular permeability
    The endothelium seals; exudation stops.
  5. Cessation of leukocytic infiltration
    No further cells are recruited.
  6. Death of neutrophils by apoptosis
    Note: apoptosis, not necrosis — the cells are cleared without provoking further inflammation.
  7. Removal of waste products by the lymphatics
    The site is cleared.
High yield

Step 6 is a lovely link back to Lecture 2. Neutrophils are removed by apoptosis precisely because apoptosis does not elicit inflammation. Had they died by necrosis, their contents would spill and restart the whole process.

What regeneration requires

  • Cells that are capable of division and differentiation
  • The stroma to be retained — the architectural framework must survive, or there is nothing for new cells to build on

Cell types by regenerative capacity

TypeBehaviourExamples
LabileContinue to multiply throughout life; functionally short-lived cells. Capable of an increased rate of multiplication if required.Epithelial cells (oral mucosa, skin), haemopoietic cells, lymphoid cells
StableCapable of regenerating if necessary, but do not divide and differentiate under normal circumstances. Normally long-lived. Replace number and weight of cells and restore function — but the histological anatomy may be abnormal.Liver, fibroblasts, smooth muscle
PermanentHighly differentiated, extremely specialised. Unable to undergo mitosis or differentiate. Replaced by scar tissue → loss of function.Nerve cells, striated muscle

Stem cells

  • Highly undifferentiated, immature cells with rapid mitosis
  • Pluripotential — capable of differentiating into any cell type
  • Multipotential — capable of differentiating into a limited number of cell types, e.g. fibroblasts into osteoblasts

Repair in detail

  • Replacement of tissue defects by collagen — this is wound healing
  • Organisation proceeds in three stages:
  1. Initial replacement by granulation tissue
    Characterised by increased capillary budding and inflammatory exudate.
  2. Replacement of granulation tissue by collagen
    Fibroblasts lay down matrix.
  3. Maturation of collagen: scar tissue
    The collagen remodels and strengthens into a mature scar.
Dental relevance

Oral mucosa is a labile tissue — which is why minor mucosal injuries heal so quickly and so completely. Compare with a nerve injury after a third molar extraction, where permanent cells cannot regenerate and function may not return.

A patient loses cardiac muscle after a myocardial infarct. Will this regenerate? What determines the outcome?

No. Striated muscle consists of permanent cells — highly differentiated and unable to undergo mitosis. The defect is therefore replaced by scar tissue, with a corresponding loss of function. Contrast with the liver (stable cells), which can regenerate if needed, and oral mucosa (labile cells), which multiplies continuously throughout life.

18

Wound types

A wound is any disruption of tissues or organs of the body caused by injury — most commonly physical or mechanical trauma.

WoundFeaturesExample
AbrasionAn open surface wound. Superficial — surface layers of epithelium only. A friction injury.Carpet burn
ContusionA bruise. A blunt injury with damage to deep tissues and haemorrhage. The surface stays intact.Blunt trauma
AvulsionA large, open wound with extensive loss of tissue volume. Caused by cutting, gouging, tearing.Avulsion of a tooth — dislocation of a tooth from its socket
PunctureA small, deep wound caused by sharp objects.Needle-stick injury; dog bite
LacerationOpen wounds with ragged edges extending deep into tissues. Crushing of tissues, with necrotic debris and blood clot. Contaminated.Laceration of the tongue
Incisional / surgicalClean — made under aseptic conditions. Minimal tissue loss; margins are sharp and clean and can be approximated. The situation is controlled to provide ideal conditions for healing.Surgical incision
The organising principle

Read the table as a spectrum of healing potential. A surgical incision has clean approximable margins, minimal tissue loss and no contamination, so it heals fast with a fine scar. A laceration has ragged crushed edges, necrotic debris and contamination, so it heals slowly with a broad scar and a high infection risk. Everything about wound management is an attempt to move a wound leftward along that spectrum.

A patient presents with a contusion. Why is there no open wound, and what has been damaged?

A contusion is a bruise caused by blunt injury. The force is transmitted through the intact surface to the deep tissues, damaging vessels there and producing haemorrhage into the tissue. Because the epithelium is not breached there is no open wound — which also means no direct route for contamination.
