Abstract · the must-knows

The core facts across both sessions, one line per topic. If you only have an hour, learn these.

  • MRONJ: antiresorptive/antiangiogenic drug + exposed bone > 8 weeks + no jaw radiotherapy. Stage 0 no exposed bone · 1 exposed, asymptomatic · 2 + pain/infection · 3 + fracture, extra-oral fistula, oro-antral communication.
  • Medical: don't stop antithrombotics · IE: amoxicillin 2 g 1 h before · defer elective care 6 months after MI · BP ≥ 180/110 defer · unconscious hypo: nothing by mouth, call 000.
  • X-rays: straight lines, can't be focused, ionise gases. Stochastic = no threshold, sublethal DNA damage → cancer. ALARA: retake only when criteria aren't met.
  • Technique: bitewing beam ⟂ through the contacts, tube 5–8° down · paralleling = accurate, reproducible, low cone-cut · SLOB: same = lingual.
  • OPG: posterior = lateral, anterior = AP · read the periphery first · ghost = contralateral, higher, reversed, magnified, blurred.
  • Occlusion: key = upper 6 MB cusp in the lower 6 buccal groove · II/2 = retroclined centrals · CR is independent of tooth contact · overbite vertical 1–2 mm · lateral pterygoid opens.
  • CRA: disease indicators (WREC) + risk factors (BAD) + protective factors (SAFER). Any WREC → high; high + hyposalivation → extreme.
  • Carious tissue: NOC vs TPOC by stage + activity · depth decides soft (deep) vs firm (shallow–moderate) · endpoint by hardness · repair, don't replace.
  • Biofilm in 6 stages: pellicle → reversible → irreversible (adhesins) → co-aggregation → stratification → climax. Calculus crystals = OCP. Materia alba washes off with water spray.
  • Periodontium: gingiva, PDL, cementum, bone · JE (hemidesmosomes, 4–6-day turnover) · biologic width ≈ 2.04 mm · stage = severity/complexity, grade = rate.
  • Endo-perio: narrow defect + no pulp response = endodontic · Abbott: communication · treat the canal first · prognosis = periodontal prognosis + OH.
  • DH: needs open tubules · hydrodynamic fluid movement · occlude or desensitise · arginine and SnF₂ have the best evidence.

Part APatient assessment, medicine & planning

A1 · History taking

Structure (in order)

  1. Reason for attending / chief complaint, in the patient's own words
  2. History of presenting complaint, using SOCRATES: Site · Onset · Character · Radiation · Associations · Time course · Exacerbating/relieving · Severity (0–10)
  3. Medical history: systematic, recording a YES and NO for every system
  4. Dental history: attendance, past treatment, attitude ("dental IQ"), home care, expectations
  5. Social history (smoking in pack-years, alcohol, drugs, occupation) and diet history

Principles

  • History and exam must be comprehensive, systematic, reproducible.
  • For every system, think of 4 critical processes (Dr Kang): stop bleeding · wound healing · blood goes "around and around" · air goes "in and out".
  • Record the generic drug name + dose + frequency, and cross-check medications against stated conditions.
  • Allergy: record the drug and the reaction. Type A ADRs are predictable (85–90%); Type B are hypersensitivity (10–15%: intolerance, allergy, idiosyncrasy).
  • Laws of diagnosis: listen to the patient · hoofbeats → horses, not zebras · a patient can have as many problems as they please.

A2 · Medical red flags

MRONJ: definition (AAOMS 2014). All three must be present

  1. Current or previous antiresorptive (bisphosphonate, denosumab) or antiangiogenic therapy
  2. Exposed bone, or bone probeable through an intra-/extra-oral fistula, in the maxillofacial region, persisting > 8 weeks
  3. No history of radiotherapy to the jaws and no obvious metastatic disease of the jaws
MRONJ stageNecrotic boneSymptoms / infection
0No clinical evidence of necrotic boneNon-specific findings, radiographic changes, symptoms
1Exposed necrotic bone, or a fistula that probes to boneAsymptomatic, no infection
2Exposed necrotic bone / fistula to boneInfection: pain + erythema ± pus
3As stage 2 plus ≥ 1 of: necrosis beyond the alveolar bone (inferior border/ramus; maxillary sinus/zygoma) → pathological fracture, extra-oral fistula, oro-antral/oro-nasal communication, osteolysis to the inferior border or sinus floorPain + infection

Don't confuse stages 1 and 2: pain, inflammation or infection makes it stage 2. Stage 1 is exposed bone that is asymptomatic.

MRONJ risk by drug

  • Oral bisphosphonates (alendronate) for osteoporosis: low–moderate; risk rises with > 4 years of use, steroids, diabetes, smoking.
  • IV zoledronic acid (oncology, monthly) and denosumab 120 mg (Xgeva): high.
  • Denosumab 60 mg (Prolia), 6-monthly: reversible but never stop it abruptly (rebound vertebral fractures); liaise with the prescriber.
  • Antiangiogenics / TKIs (bevacizumab, sunitinib): high, even without bisphosphonates.

MRONJ management

  • Prevention beats treatment. Do extractions before IV antiresorptives start.
  • Prefer RCT and keeping the root over extraction. Avoid implants in high-risk patients.
  • If extraction is unavoidable: atraumatic technique, primary closure, review at 1, 2, 4 and 8 weeks. Oncology patients → refer to oral surgery.
  • Risk persists after the drug is stopped. Read the TG v4 (2025) MRONJ chapter.

Other red flags

Hypoglycaemia in the chair

Conscious and able to swallow: stop treatment, give 15–20 g fast-acting glucose (glucose gel, jelly beans, ~150 mL juice), recheck at 15 min and repeat if no better, then a longer-acting carbohydrate.

Drowsy, uncooperative or unconscious: nothing by mouth (aspiration risk). Stop and call 000, recovery position, airway. Glucagon only if trained; CPR if not breathing.

A3 · Extra- & intra-oral examination

Extra-oral sequence

Gait and asymmetry → skin/hair → cervical lymph nodes → TMJ → muscles of mastication. Observe from the moment the patient walks in.

Node levelLocation
ISubmental + submandibular triangles
II–IVAlong SCM (anterior triangle), upper → lower
VPosterior triangle
VIBelow hyoid, anterior central neck

Inflammatory node: enlarged, tender, soft, mobile. Neoplastic node: enlarged, firm, non-tender, ± fixed.

Intra-oral

  • Two mirrors + gauze. Check saliva, dentition and mucosa systematically.
  • Mucosa types: masticatory (keratinised: gingiva, hard palate) · lining (non-keratinised: buccal, floor of mouth, alveolar, ventral tongue) · specialised (dorsal tongue, vermilion).
  • High-risk cancer sites: lateral and ventral tongue, floor of mouth, retromolar trigone, tonsillar pillar, commissure.
  • Describe a lesion: colour, shape, size, number, margins, texture, effect on surroundings. Ulcer = full-thickness epithelial loss; erosion = partial.
  • Oral cavity vs oropharynx: anterior ⅔ vs posterior ⅓ of the tongue; hard vs soft palate; the border is the palatoglossal folds.

Emergency signs

Swelling with dyspnoea, stridor, cyanosis or spreading infection, or trismus with the patient unable to keep fluids down, means a medical emergency.

A4 · Treatment planning (Stefanac)

Rule zero

Diagnosis dictates treatment. No diagnosis means no irreversible treatment: reassess later or refer.

PhasePurpose / content
1 · SystemicFour purposes: (1) recognise undiagnosed systemic disease and refer; (2) limit or modify dental treatment; (3) prevent emergencies in the practice; (4) prevent post-operative complications. ASA class, physician consultation, smoking and diet change.
2 · AcuteEmergency = incapacitating, potentially life-threatening (severe pain, swelling, bleeding, systemic infection, facial trauma) → now. Urgent = patient wants it "soon" (mild pain, broken tooth, lost filling) → palliative care, then schedule.
3 · Disease controlThree purposes: eradicate active disease and infection · arrest deterioration · control causes and risk factors. Caries control protocol, OHI, SRD, remove overhangs, extract hopeless teeth, direct or provisional restorations only. Reassess before moving on (PPD, CAL, BOP, plaque index, pulp tests).
4 · DefinitivePerio surgery (residual PPD ≥ 6 mm; or 4–5 mm with recurrent BOP/furcations), regeneration (best in deep 3-wall infrabony defects), orthodontics, crowns and onlays, elective endo, extractions, pre-prosthetic surgery, tooth replacement (implant / bridge / RPD / none).
5 · MaintenanceGingivitis: prophylaxis every 6–12 months. Periodontitis: SPT at intervals usually ≤ 3 months, set by risk (PRA). Reassess caries risk, restorations, implants and endo every visit.

Dr Ball's Session 2 lectures use a 4-phase version (systemic → disease control → definitive → SPT) and fold the acute phase into disease control. Both come from Stefanac. Exam types: comprehensive · periodic · problem-focused (limited) · post-treatment assessment.

Basic caries control protocol (disease control)

  • Prophylaxis + OHI
  • Professional F varnish (22,600 ppm) at each visit, most effective at < 6-month intervals
  • Diet: cut the frequency of sugar and acid
  • Daily F toothpaste ± rinse; 5,000 ppm for high risk
  • Sealants on at-risk fissures; xylitol gum
  • Restore cavitated lesions with direct or provisional restorations (no indirect work yet)

Records, consent, infection control

  • "Good records = good defence; no records = no defence." Records must be contemporaneous, accurate, legible and secure; keep radiographs 7–10 years.
  • Informed consent always includes the option of doing nothing, with the risks, benefits and costs of each option.
  • Standard precautions apply to every patient. Hand hygiene is the single most effective measure (WHO 5 moments).
  • Spaulding: critical instruments (penetrate tissue, e.g. scalers) → sterilise. Semi-critical (mucosa, e.g. mirrors) → sterilise or high-level disinfection. Non-critical → clean ± disinfect.

A5 · Special tests & endodontic diagnosis (AAE)

Diagnosis is a jigsaw: never rely on one test. Always give both a pulpal and a periapical diagnosis, and test adjacent and contralateral teeth first as a baseline.

PulpalKey feature

Symptomatic irreversibleLingering thermal pain, spontaneous, referred; percussion often normal
Asymptomatic irreversibleNo symptoms, but deep caries or exposure → won't heal

  • Sensibility tests (cold, heat, EPT) measure nerve response. Vitality tests (laser Doppler flowmetry) measure blood flow, the truer measure of pulp health.
  • No response to cold suggests necrosis, unless the canal is calcified. No EPT response = 72% total and 25.7% partial necrosis (Seltzer). Percussion → periapical inflammation. Tenderness over the apex is reliable; its absence doesn't exclude inflammation.
  • Other tests: bite test (Tooth Slooth / FracFinder; pain on release = cracked cusp), transillumination, test cavity (invasive, last resort), selective anaesthesia.
  • Standard example combination: SIP + normal apical tissues. Pulp necrosis + symptomatic apical periodontitis + condensing osteitis → RCT, and the osteitis regresses.
Part BRadiography & occlusion

B1 · X-ray physics & radiation protection

Properties of X-rays

  • Electromagnetic, invisible, massless, very short wavelength
  • Travel in straight lines at the speed of light; cannot be focused
  • Penetrate matter; differentially absorbed (dense tissue → white)
  • Ionise (they can ionise gases); can scatter; cause fluorescence; cause biological change

The tube

Heated tungsten filament (cathode) → thermionic emission → electrons accelerated to the tungsten target (anode) in a copper stem. ~99% heat, ~1% X-rays. Most photons are bremsstrahlung ("braking"); a few are characteristic radiation.

Exposure factors

mA × timeQuantity of photons (10 mA × 1 s = 20 mA × 0.5 s); ↑ = darker image, ↑ dose
kVpQuality / penetration; ↑ kVp → lower contrast (more greys). 70 kVp typical
CollimationRound beam ~7 cm diameter; rectangular cuts dose further
DistanceInverse square law: intensity ∝ 1/distance²

Interactions with tissue: Compton scatter 57% · photoelectric absorption 27% · coherent scatter 7% · no interaction 9%. About ⅔ of biological damage is indirect (free radicals from water).

EffectMechanismThresholdExamples · relevance
DeterministicMass cell killingYes; severity ↑ with doseBurns, cataract, hair loss. Not reached by dental doses
StochasticSublethal damage to single cells (DNA)No (LNT); probability ↑ with doseCancer, heritable mutation. This is the dental concern and the basis of ALARA
  • ALARA / ALADA (as low as reasonably / diagnostically achievable): every radiograph must be justified. Retake only when the diagnostic criteria are not met, and log every retake.
  • Dose units: exposure (C/kg, ionisation of air) · absorbed dose (Gy) · equivalent dose (Sv) · effective dose (Sv, the risk estimate). Radiosensitive: thyroid, salivary glands, bone marrow, brain. Children are more radiosensitive.
  • Geometry: longer source–object distance → sharper, less magnified. Longer object–film distance → blurrier, more magnified.

B2 · Intra-oral techniques

Bitewing

  • Best for approximal caries and crestal bone. Doesn't show apices.
  • Film horizontal for posterior, vertical for anterior. Posterior teeth and film parallel.
  • Beam at right angles through the contacts in the horizontal plane; tube angled 5–8° downward in the vertical plane.
  • Ideal image: from distal of the lower canine back, minimal overlap, ≥ 2 mm of root below the CEJ.
  • Cervical burn-out ≠ caries. Confirm clinically.

Periapical

Paralleling (preferred)Bisecting angle
Set-upHolder; film ∥ tooth; beam ⟂ bothFilm against tooth; beam ⟂ the bisector
ProsGeometrically accurate, reproducible, low cone-cut risk; bone levels true; zygomatic buttress clear of molar apicesComfortable, quick, no holder (gag reflex, shallow palate)
ConsGagging; anatomy may prevent itNot reproducible; foreshortening/elongation; zygoma over molars; finger dose

Show 2 mm beyond the apex. Too steep a vertical angle → foreshortening; too shallow → elongation; horizontal error → overlapped contacts.

SLOB rule (tube shift)

Shift the tube (e.g. mesially). If the object's image moves the Same way → Lingual. If it moves the Opposite way → Buccal. Used for impacted canines and canal location.

Occlusal views: upper standard occlusal 60–70° (unerupted canines, anterior maxilla) · lower true occlusal 90° (submandibular duct calculi, bucco-lingual position) · lower 45°.

B3 · The OPG

  • Panoramic perspective: the tube rotates, so the posterior regions are lateral views and the anterior is an AP view, like a composite of two laterals plus one AP.
  • Challenges: lower resolution than intra-orals, multiple superimpositions and distortion, extra-jaw structures, ghost images, and no bucco-lingual information (→ CBCT/CT). Patient positioning is critical. Dose ≈ 0.014 mSv (chest X-ray 0.02 mSv).
  • Reading order (Perschbacher): start at the periphery and corners (TMJ, styloids, sinuses, hyoid, C-spine) so you don't zone in on the teeth; read teeth last. Cosson: count teeth → mandible R→L → sinuses → peripheries. Either way, be systematic.
  • Ghost image: a structure outside the focal trough, e.g. the contralateral mandible, appears higher, reversed, magnified and blurred. Also the C-spine (patient stands tall) and earrings (remove them).
  • Air spaces: the tongue–palate gap (tongue to palate) and the "kiss" lip shadow (close lips) can mimic fractures. Mastoid pneumatisation of the articular eminence is a normal variant.
  • Posterior maxilla, three parallel lines: sinus posterior wall, zygomatic process, pterygomaxillary fissure. A missing line may mean malignancy.
  • Cyst vs abscess: cyst is well-defined, often asymptomatic, may not touch a tooth; abscess is painful and always contacts the tooth. OKC/ameloblastoma need biopsy; OKC recurs in up to 62%. Mandibular fracture: angle 32%, with a second fracture (parasymphysis) in 40%.

B4 · Occlusion

Angle's classification

Key to occlusion: the maxillary first molar; its mesiobuccal cusp occludes in the buccal groove of the lower first molar.

Class IMB cusp of upper 6 in the buccal groove of lower 6
Class IIMB cusp anterior (mesial) to the groove; retruded mandible
· Div 1Upper incisors proclined
· Div 2Upper centrals retroclined; laterals and canines proclined
Class IIIMB cusp posterior to the groove; reverse overjet

Overjet = horizontal overlap, normally 2–3 mm (reverse = anterior crossbite). Overbite = vertical overlap, normally 1–2 mm, with lower incisal edges contacting the upper palatal surface at or above the cingulum. Crossbite: maxillary teeth lingual to mandibular (posterior crossbite usually means a narrow maxilla).

Static positions

  • Centric relation (CR): a jaw relationship independent of tooth contact; condyles anterior-superior against the posterior slopes of the eminences; purely rotary movement; repeatable reference.
  • Centric occlusion (CO): tooth contact when the mandible is in CR; may or may not equal MI.
  • Maximum intercuspation (MI): best fit of the teeth regardless of condyle position.
  • Dynamic: working side = the side the mandible moves towards; non-working side moves to the midline.

Muscles of mastication

  • Masseter: elevation + protrusion. Temporalis: elevation + retraction. Medial pterygoid: elevation + protrusion.
  • Lateral pterygoid: protraction and forward TMJ movement; the only muscle that opposes the others and depresses the mandible (opens).

Curve of Spee = anteroposterior; curve of Wilson = mediolateral.

Part CCariology

C1 · What caries is, and how we classify it

Caries vs carious lesion (ICCC)

Dental caries is the disease: a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, dynamic ecological shift → net mineral loss. The carious lesion is its sign. You can't remove caries, only carious tissue. Caries management controls the disease at patient level by preventive, non-invasive means. Carious lesion management treats established, non-cleansable lesions at tooth level, sometimes removing tissue.

Classifications

  • G.V. Black (1896), by surface: I pits/fissures · II posterior proximal · III anterior proximal · IV anterior proximal + incisal edge · V cervical ⅓.
  • Mount & Hume (1997), by site: 1 pits/fissures · 2 contact areas · 3 cervical / root.
  • ICDAS (2002): stages by visual appearance, matched to histological depth. ICCMS (2013) wraps ICDAS in risk assessment and management.
  • Detection = is disease there? · Assessment = characterise/monitor it · Diagnosis = the clinician's synthesis of all the data, including activity.
ICDASAppearance (clean, dry tooth)Stage
0Sound after 5 s air-dryingSound
1First visual change, seen only when dryA · initial
2Distinct change, visible wet
3Localised enamel breakdown, no dentine visibleB · moderate
4Underlying dark dentine shadow
5Distinct cavity with dentine, < ½ surfaceC · extensive
6Extensive cavity, > ½ surface
  • Non-cavitated enamel lesion (white spot): matt and chalky when dried; four zones: translucent (1% porosity) · dark (2–4%) · body (25%) · relatively intact surface zone. Active = white, matt, rough, plaque-covered. Arrested = brown/black, shiny, hard.
  • Dentine: outer infected dentine (soft, wet, necrotic) over inner affected dentine (leathery to hard, remineralisable; sclerotic/reactionary zone).
  • Approximal lesions are found late, so use bitewings. A sharp probe can cavitate an intact lesion; use a ball-ended probe. Root caries: active = yellow/light, soft, plaque-covered; arrested = dark and hard. Recurrent caries: softness at a defective margin.
  • Caries-detector dyes stain less-mineralised organic matrix, not bacteria → over-removal. Not recommended.

C2 · Caries risk assessment & CAMBRA

CAMBRA philosophy

A concept, not a product or form, for managing caries as a multifactorial biofilm disease according to risk: (1) assess risk → (2) reduce pathological factors → (3) enhance protective factors → (4) minimally invasive restorative care. Restorations alone don't control the disease.

The caries balance weighs disease indicators + risk factors against protective factors, and decides whether caries progresses, halts or reverses.

Disease indicators: WRECRisk factors: BADProtective factors: SAFER
White spots on smooth surfacesBad (cariogenic) bacteriaSaliva & sealants
Restorations for caries in the last 3 yearsAbsence of salivaAntibacterials
Enamel approximal lesions (radiograph)Destructive habits (frequent sugar, drugs, poor OH)Fluoride (+ Ca/PO₄)
Cavities / dentine lesionsEffective lifestyle · Risk-based reassessment
  • Disease indicators are clinical observations that confirm past or present caries activity. They are the strongest predictor of future disease, but don't reveal the cause.
  • Any one WREC → HIGH risk, unless effective therapy is already in place. Extreme = high risk + hyposalivation (~5% of patients). Moderate = "when in doubt".
  • Objectives of a CRA: diagnose current caries, predict future disease, and identify which factors are out of balance, so prevention and recall are tailored. Repeat it at every new-patient and recall exam.
  • Outcomes (UCSF): new cavities in 24% low · ~40% moderate · ~70% high · 88% extreme risk.
  • RCT: CHX + fluoride → 24% lower 2-year increment in high-risk adults. Restorations didn't lower bacteria; ~70% of controls got new cavities.
  • High-risk regimen: 5,000 ppm F toothpaste twice daily + F varnish + 0.12% CHX daily for 1 week/month + xylitol. Recall every 3 months for extreme risk, 6 months for low.

ICCMS: risk × lesion status → likelihood

ICCMS has 3 principles: prevention first, surgery last · minimal tissue removal · tissue preservation. It runs as a 4-element cycle: patient assessment → lesion detection, activity and risk → synthesis and decision → treatment with prevention. It combines patient risk with lesion stage and activity. High risk + active moderate/extensive lesions = high likelihood of new or progressing lesions → intensive, personalised plan combining home and clinical non-operative care with operative care, and short recalls.

C3 · NOC vs TPOC & carious tissue removal

Non-operative care (NOC)

Fluoride, sealants, diet, OH, antibacterials. For initial lesions and active non-cavitated lesions, guided by lesion stage and activity. Rule: "preserve tooth structure and restore only when indicated".

Tooth-preserving operative care (TPOC)

Only when a lesion is cavitated, non-cleansable and can't be sealed: extensive lesions, or moderate ones that are cavitated into dentine. Radiographic depth alone doesn't decide it. Cavitation does, because bacteria can't cross intact enamel.

Dentine (by feel)Description
SoftDeforms under a hard instrument; scoops away easily ("sticky")
LeatheryDoesn't deform, but lifts without much force ("tacky")
FirmResists a hand excavator; needs some pressure
HardNeeds a bur; "cri dentinaire" (scratchy sound)
  • The primary criterion for the endpoint is hardness, not colour, dye or fluorescence.
  • Shallow / moderate lesions → selective removal to FIRM (restoration longevity first). Deep lesions (inner ⅓–¼ of dentine) in a vital, symptom-free pulp → selective removal to SOFT over the pulp (avoids exposure, protects vitality), with hard/firm peripheral walls for the seal. Stepwise removal (re-entry at 6–12 months) is an option in permanent teeth only.
  • Non-selective removal to hard dentine = over-treatment, not advocated. Choose by lesion depth and pulp-exposure risk, not by symptoms, tooth type or cost.
  • Evidence: sealed lesions arrest. Stepwise vs complete removal gives fewer exposures (15% vs 53%; 17.5% vs 40%). Mertz-Fairhurst sealed soft dentine stayed arrested at 10 years. Kidd: the job of a restoration is a cleanable surface + seal. Banerjee: "any lesion at any stage can arrest".
  • Existing restorations: minor defects or radiolucencies under a symptom-free restoration → monitor, refurbish or repair. Replacement is a last resort. No evidence for cavity disinfection; liners aren't needed to control a sealed lesion. Low-anxiety options: ART, Hall technique, Carisolv.

C4 · Tooth prognosis (presentation papers)

Samet & Jotkowitz 2009

  • Classes A (80–100% bone support or sound crown) · B (50–80%) · C (30–50%) · D (< 30%) · X = non-salvageable (VRF, no supragingival tooth).
  • Criteria: periodontal, restorative, endodontic, occlusal + anatomic and iatrogenic factors.
  • Rules: the worst criterion sets the class; an anatomic/iatrogenic factor drops it 1 class; considerable patient-level risk drops every tooth 1 class. Not validated.

McGuire & Nunn 1996 · Hirschfeld & Wasserman 1978

  • McGuire: 81% of prognoses correct overall, but only 43% (5 yr) / 35% (8 yr) for teeth worse than "good", "a coin toss". Smoking: odds of improving 0.418, worsening 1.90. Endo involvement OR 3.87.
  • H&W: 600 patients over 22 years; 7.1% of teeth lost; 499 well maintained / 76 downhill / 25 extreme downhill. Most "questionable" teeth survived. Loss concentrated in a minority of patients.
Part DPeriodontics: foundations (Session 1)

D1 · The periodontium

The tissues that invest and support the tooth: gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum, alveolar bone. Cementum, PDL and alveolar bone proper come from the dental follicle; the gingival epithelium comes from oral epithelium and reduced enamel epithelium.

TissueMust-know facts
GingivaFree, attached and interdental. Oral epithelium is orthokeratinised. Sulcular epithelium is non-keratinised. Junctional epithelium (JE): non-keratinised, 15–30 cells thick coronally to 1–3 apically, attached by hemidesmosomes + internal basal lamina, turnover 4–6 days, wide intercellular spaces (permeable; lets GCF and neutrophils through). Lamina propria ~60% collagen; no submucosa, so attached gingiva is a mucoperiosteum. Gingival fibres (dentogingival, circular, transseptal…) brace the margin.
GCFTransudate in health, exudate in disease. Flushes the sulcus, carries antibodies and plasma proteins, aids adhesion.
Biologic widthGargiulo 1961: sulcus 0.69 + JE 0.97 + CT attachment 1.07 → ≈ 2.04 mm (JE + CT). Margins placed inside it → inflammation and bone loss.
PDL~0.2 mm wide (hourglass, narrowest mid-root). Type I collagen. Principal fibres: alveolar crest · horizontal · oblique (most numerous) · apical · inter-radicular; ends embedded = Sharpey's fibres. Cells: fibroblasts, osteo- and cementoblasts, rests of Malassez (HERS remnants), stem cells. Functions: transmit forces, physiological mobility, proprioception, repair.

Development: bud (~8 wk) → cap (~11 wk) → bell (~14 wk). HERS shapes the root; the dental papilla gives dentine and pulp; the follicle gives cementum, PDL and bone.

Biofilm formation: 6 stages (Marsh & Bradshaw 1995)

  1. Conditioning film (acquired pellicle): forms immediately after cleaning; < 1 µm; plateaus at 90–120 min (mucins, statherin, PRPs, amylase, lysozyme, sIgA).
  2. Non-specific, reversible attachment: long-range physico-chemical forces between salivary bacteria and the pellicle.
  3. Specific, irreversible attachment: short-range stereo-chemical binding of primary colonisers (streptococci, Actinomyces) via adhesins (fibrils, fimbriae) to pellicle receptors.
  4. Co-aggregation: cell-to-cell recognition of genetically distinct partner cells via adhesin–receptor pairs, so secondary colonisers attach to primary ones. Fusobacterium is the bridge between early and late colonisers.
  5. Stratification and succession: horizontal and vertical micro-environments (pH, O₂, nutrients) let otherwise incompatible species coexist.
  6. Climax community: stable composition (~2–3 weeks); obligate anaerobes increase.
  • Biofilm = matrix-enclosed communities on a surface (cells 15–20% of volume, matrix 75–80%, water channels). Three components: a surface, the biofilm, and bulk fluid. Features: altered gene expression, quorum sensing, reduced antimicrobial susceptibility (slow penetration, quiescence, stress response, persister cells).
  • Socransky complexes: early/health = purple, yellow (streptococci), green. Orange (F. nucleatum, P. intermedia) is the bridge. Red = P. gingivalis, T. forsythia, T. denticola: deep pockets, BOP.
  • Hypotheses: non-specific → specific → ecological → keystone pathogen (P. gingivalis) → polymicrobial synergy & dysbiosis (PSD), the current model → IMPEDE (inflammation-driven).
  • Calculus: supragingival (creamy/yellow, mineralised by saliva, near duct openings: lingual of lower incisors, buccal of upper 6s) vs subgingival (dark, hard, mineralised by GCF/inflammatory exudate). Mineral by volume: supra 37%, sub 58%.
  • Crystals: small, thin, needle-shaped: octacalcium phosphate, hydroxyapatite, whitlockite, brushite.
  • Calcification starts in 4–8 h; ~50% mineralised at 2 days.
  • Plaque is the primary aetiological agent. Calculus is secondary: a plaque-retentive surface.
  • Gingivitis appears in 10–21 days without OH and reverses when OH resumes (Löe 1965).

D4 · 2017 AAP/EFP classification

  • Gingival health: BOP < 10%, PPD ≤ 3 mm (intact, or reduced periodontium).
  • Gingivitis: BOP ≥ 10%; localised 10–30%, generalised > 30%. BOP is the only objective sign. Reversible.
  • Periodontitis case: interdental CAL ≥ 3 mm at ≥ 2 non-adjacent teeth, or buccal/oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with PPD > 3 mm at ≥ 2 teeth, not explained by other causes. "Aggressive" no longer exists; it is now Grade C.
  • Extent: localised < 30% of teeth · generalised ≥ 30% · molar–incisor pattern.
  • Status after treatment: stable (BOP < 10%, PPD ≤ 4 mm, no BOP at 4 mm) · remission · unstable (PPD ≥ 5 mm, or 4 mm with BOP).
  • Statement: Generalised periodontitis, stage III, grade B, currently unstable, risk factor: smoker 15/day.

D5 · Mechanical & chemical plaque control

Mechanical: the gold standard

  • Brush 2×/day for 2 min (30 s per quadrant), soft brush, modified Bass. Optimal manual force ~400 g. Brushes reach ~0.9 mm subgingivally.
  • Powered: only rotation-oscillation beats manual (plaque −11%, gingivitis −6% short term; Deacon 2010 Cochrane), and it is safe.
  • Plaque forms first interproximally, where the brush can't reach.
  • Interdental brushes are the aid of choice: they remove more plaque than floss and give more pocket reduction (Slots 2008). Floss only where the papilla fills the space or the gap is too tight.
  • Deep pockets (> 6 mm): supragingival care alone won't change the subgingival flora, so professional instrumentation is needed.

Chemical: an adjunct, never a substitute

  • Chlorhexidine is the gold standard: a cationic bisbiguanide that binds negatively charged bacterial membranes → leakage (bacteriostatic) or precipitation (bactericidal). Strong substantivity: antibacterial activity up to 5 h after a 60 s rinse with 10 mL of 0.2% (Roberts & Addy). Optimal quantity 40 mg/day = 10 mL of 0.2% bd or 15 mL of 0.15% bd (Jones 1997); less gives less effect.
  • CHX side effects: extrinsic staining, ↑ supragingival calculus (precipitated salivary proteins/salts), taste disturbance, mucosal erosion, rare parotid swelling. Poorly absorbed (cationic), so no systemic toxicity; reduces fibroblast proliferation in vitro (avoid in regenerative surgery). Inactivated by anionic SLS.
  • Essential oils (Listerine): no staining; less plaque reduction than CHX. CPC: substantivity only 3–5 h. Triclosan + copolymer/zinc. SnF₂: anti-plaque + anti-caries, but stains.
  • Desensitising pastes: K⁺ (depolarises the nerve) vs tubule occluders (SnF₂, arginine, CSPS, strontium).
Part EPeriodontics & endodontics: Session 2

E1 · Non-surgical periodontal therapy full note →

  • Goal: disrupt the biofilm and remove plaque-retentive factors, plus self-performed plaque control. In pockets > 5 mm, supragingival care alone doesn't change the subgingival plaque.
  • Debridement / SRD = disrupt and remove plaque. SRP also removes calculus and "contaminated" cementum (Kieser 1994). Endotoxin is loosely bound and doesn't penetrate cementum (Smart 1990), so don't plane away cementum. Root smoothness doesn't affect healing (Hunter 1984).
  • Not all deposits need removing for healing (Nyman 1988). 90% of teeth keep some deposit (Waerhaug). Calculus-free surfaces: 86% / 43% / 32% at 1–3 / 4–6 / > 6 mm (Caffesse 1986).
  • Protocols: full-mouth vs quadrant SRD = same outcome (Wennström 2005). Full-mouth disinfection (Quirynen 1995) was never replicated. Lasers and PDT lack evidence (Cobb 2017). Hand vs powered instruments give similar outcomes; slim ultrasonic tips are better in pockets.
  • Critical success factors: thoroughness of debridement · patient's OH · compliance with SPT.
  • Microbiology: a transient fall in red complex (A.a may be eliminated), so supragingival control is needed to prevent recolonisation. Furcations respond worse (25% lose attachment vs 7% at non-molars).

E2 · Healing after SRD full note →

  • Repair (architecture not restored; what SRD gives) · reattachment (reunion with a root that still has its attachment) · new attachment (onto a root that has lost it) · regeneration (reconstitution; the ultimate goal).
  • Healing is by a long junctional epithelium (~2.5 mm vs normal JE 0.1–1.4 mm), not new CT attachment (Caton & Zander 1979). Fast epithelium beats slow PDL cells to the root. GTR membranes exist to block the epithelium.
  • The LJE's barrier function equals normal JE (Magnusson 1983). It may shorten over time and be replaced by CT (Katayanagi).
  • New dento-epithelial junction by 2 weeks; orientated collagen by 4–8 weeks.
  • Probing: 0.5 mm tip at 0.25 N (25 g), about the pressure that blanches a fingernail. The probe stops 0.39 mm short of the JE in health and 0.10 mm short in gingivitis, but goes 0.24 mm past in periodontitis (Armitage 1977, dogs).
  • 90% of readings are reproducible within ± 1 mm (Badersten 1985), so changes of ≤ 1 mm may be error.

E5 · Acute pain & acute periodontal conditions full note →

  • Short, sharp pain to hot/cold/sweet → exposed dentine, caries, lost filling. Sharp pain on biting → cracked cusp, loose filling, fracture. Dull, throbbing → periapical infection, pericoronitis, NUG, dry socket (Wetherell 2001).
  • Periodontal abscess: pus in the pocket wall; 7.7–14% of emergencies (3rd after dento-alveolar abscess and pericoronitis). Drain + debride under LA; antibiotics only with systemic signs.
  • Pericoronitis: inflamed operculum, usually over a lower 8. Debride, irrigate, relieve occlusion; later operculectomy or extraction.
  • Necrotising periodontal diseases: punched-out necrotic papillae, pain, bleeding, pseudomembrane, halitosis. Spectrum NG → NP → NS → noma. Risk: HIV, malnutrition, stress, smoking. LA debridement + CHX/H₂O₂ + metronidazole 400 mg 12-hourly for 3–5 days, review at 48–72 h.
  • Listgarten's zones (surface → deep): bacterial · neutrophil-rich · necrotic · spirochaetal infiltration.

  • Sequence: treat the canal first. Root planing removes cementum and opens tubules, so canal bacteria reach the PDL. With communication: Ledermix, then at 4 weeks Ledermix/Ca(OH)₂ + start perio; reassess at 3 months; then root-fill.
  • Prognosis depends mainly on the periodontal prognosis and the patient's oral hygiene.
  • Herrera 2018: EPL with root damage (fracture, perforation, resorption; poor prognosis) vs without, graded 1–3 (narrow / wide pocket on 1 surface / > 1 surface).

E7 · NCCLs, dentine hypersensitivity, tooth wear

NCCLs (Grippo · Goodacre)

  • Multifactorial: stress (abfraction) + friction (wear) + biocorrosion (acids, enzymes). Stress concentrates at the cervix and accelerates biocorrosion ("stress corrosion").
  • Saucer (progresses in height) vs wedge (height + depth). Mostly facial surfaces of maxillary premolars; rare lingually (5× more saliva).
  • Abfraction is unproven clinically. Don't adjust the occlusion to treat NCCLs. Delay restoration, then bond carefully to sclerotic dentine.

Tooth wear (Mehta · Loomans)

  • Physiological enamel wear ~20–38 µm/year (Lambrechts). Severe = an amount (dentine exposed, ≥ ⅓ crown lost). Pathological = atypical for age and causing problems.
  • Prevent and monitor first (BEWE). No definitive restorations while wear is active. Additive, minimally invasive work (Dahl). Avoid the restorative death spiral.

Dentine hypersensitivity (Bartold · West)

  • Short, sharp pain from exposed dentine to thermal, tactile, osmotic or chemical stimuli. It needs exposure (localisation) and open, patent tubules linking the outside to the pulp (initiation).
  • Hydrodynamic theory (Brännström): stimuli disturb tubular fluid, and the fluid movement mechanically activates pulpal nerve receptors (Aδ fibres).
  • Two strategies: occlude the tubules (SnF₂, arginine, CSPS, strontium, oxalates, resins) or desensitise the nerve (K⁺).
  • West 2015 (systematic review): arginine and SnF₂ (high-quality evidence) > strontium acetate > CSPS. Potassium not recommended. Prevalence usually 10–30%, peak at the end of the 3rd decade; upper premolars.
  • DH ladder: remove the cause → desensitising toothpaste → topical occluders → resin, restoration or graft.
Part FLong answers & numbers

F1 · Long-answer practice

Use headings, define terms first, give structure and function, then answer the "why". Name the evidence where you can.

"What is the periodontium and why is it important?"

  1. Define: the tissues that invest and support the tooth: gingiva, PDL, cementum, alveolar bone (Lang & Lindhe). Gingiva = protective; PDL + cementum + bone = attachment apparatus.
  2. Gingiva: free/attached/interdental; oral (keratinised), sulcular and junctional epithelium (hemidesmosomes, internal basal lamina, 4–6 day turnover, permeable, neutrophils); gingival fibres; GCF (transudate → exudate). JE + fibres = the dentogingival unit.
  3. Biologic width ≈ 2.04 mm (JE 0.97 + CT 1.07, Gargiulo). Margins must respect it.
  4. PDL: ~0.2 mm, hourglass; principal fibre groups (oblique most numerous), Sharpey's fibres; fibroblasts, osteo-/cementoblasts, rests of Malassez, stem cells.
  5. Cementum: acellular extrinsic (cervical, attachment) vs cellular intrinsic (apical, repair); avascular; CEJ 60–65 / 30 / 5–10%.
  6. Alveolar bone: bundle bone = lamina dura; tooth-dependent; crest ~1.5–2 mm below the CEJ.
  7. Why it matters:
    • Retains the tooth; transmits and cushions occlusal load; physiological mobility
    • Barrier and defence against the biofilm (JE, GCF, neutrophils, rapid turnover)
    • Proprioception; nutrition; repair and remodelling (eruption, orthodontic movement)
    • Aesthetics (papillae, contour)
    • Clinically: plaque → gingivitis → periodontitis destroys these tissues → tooth loss. Health/disease is judged by probing, BOP, CAL and radiographs (lamina dura, PDL space). Restorations must respect biologic width. Systemic links (diabetes, CVD). Normal anatomy is the baseline for diagnosis, staging and treatment.

Detail: D1 · disease: D3, D4.

Multiple active lesions + a stressed patient who can manage one change: "stop sugary snacks" or "brush 2× a day with fluoride toothpaste"?

Either answer can score if it is justified. The stronger case is brushing twice daily with fluoride toothpaste (high-fluoride 5000 ppm for this high-risk adult):

  • Caries balance: it acts on both sides. It disrupts the biofilm and adds fluoride (remineralisation, acid-resistant fluorapatite, antibacterial), so the balance tips toward repair even if diet is unchanged.
  • Evidence in the set papers: on the CAMBRA form, twice-daily is significantly better than once; fluoride toothpaste lowered the odds of cavities in the UCSF CRA data (OR 0.67). Banerjee 2017: if the biofilm is regularly disturbed, "any lesion at any stage of its progression can arrest". The ICCC (Schwendicke 2016) says caries is managed behaviourally.
  • Behaviour change under stress: adding one simple, routine habit is more achievable than removing a habitual (often stress-driven) sugar habit. Early success builds self-efficacy for the next change.
  • Extra benefits: plaque control also helps the gingiva and periodontium.

Then say what you'd do next: acknowledge that diet is the root cause (frequent fermentable carbohydrate = BAD "destructive lifestyle habits"). Introduce sugar reduction next using motivational interviewing (frequency, keep to mealtimes, xylitol/sugar-free alternatives). Re-run the CRA, add clinic fluoride varnish and CHX by risk, 3-month recall. Seal or restore the cavitated lesions; NOC for non-cavitated ones.

If you choose diet instead: argue it removes the substrate (cause, not symptom) and also helps general health. But concede it's harder to change under stress and that the lesions still need fluoride.

More long-answer practice (from the presentation papers)

"One thing to change about a patient?"

Stop smoking. McGuire & Nunn: smoking OR 0.418 for improving, OR 1.90 for worsening. PRA: ≥ 20/day = high on its own; a former smoker of > 5 years scores low. Samet step 3: patient-level risk drops every tooth one class. Close second: frequency of sugary/acidic intake (caries, erosion, NCCL, DH).

"Endo + perio + caries: what first?"

Systemic → acute (endo first: pulpectomy, Ledermix; seal cavities) → disease control (with communication: Ledermix/Ca(OH)₂ at 4 weeks + NSPT; selective caries removal, GIC seal; CAMBRA chemical therapy) → reassess at 3 months → definitive (Samet re-grade; coronal seal; repair over replace) → maintenance (PRA/CAMBRA recall; root filling review at 6–12 months).

"Five concepts the papers change"

  1. Treat the patient's risk, not just the tooth (CAMBRA RCT: restorations didn't lower bacteria; CHX + F cut new caries by 24%).
  2. Remove less, rely on the seal (stepwise 15 vs 53% exposures; Mertz-Fairhurst arrested at 10 years).
  3. Monitor first; restore late and additively (Loomans, Goodacre; BEWE; Dahl).
  4. Hold prognoses loosely (McGuire: below "good" only 35–43% accurate; H&W: most questionable teeth survived a mean 22-year follow-up).
  5. Endo before perio when both are present, and judge the outcome by the periodontium (Abbott).
